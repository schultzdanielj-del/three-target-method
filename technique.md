# Technique checks, movement by movement

Talk to the person in layman's terms and simple English, never in biomechanical terms. Ask the person for a video of a set, and listen to how the set feels. Most people cannot see their own technique faults. These are the things Dan looks for in a video. General principles that apply across many movements (scapular control, rib cage, hips) are in [programming.md](programming.md), section 23. What to swap a movement for is in [programming.md](programming.md), section 8.

A note on the cues: the cues here are for people to think about, not an anatomy textbook. They help a person correct back toward the right position. They are directions, not literal end positions. Dan: if someone is looking straight up at the ceiling and you want them to look forward, you say "look down some", and that does not mean look at the floor.

A note on the studies cited here: most are EMG (muscle activation) studies done in short lab sets. Dan: "You can EMG study if you want, but that's not the same as a heavy training set." Activation in a lab set is not the same as the effect of a hard set taken to a PR-level effort.

Source of these rules: Dan's coaching observations from 17 years of coaching, 10,000+ hours of one on one coaching, across easily over 1000 people. They are anecdotal, not studies. Many of them have no direct study behind them, as is true of much of fitness. They are built by taking the known truths and logically assembling a model. Where a study is cited, it is named.

## Chest

**Any bench press, fly or incline press with the back anchored against a bench (barbell, dumbbell or machine).** The scapula positioning is the same regardless of equipment.
- The scapula must remain retracted and depressed at all times. The lats should be flexed to do this, and that is what causes the elbows to stay slightly tucked.
- Do not deliberately tuck the elbows. The correct scapular mechanics make the elbows tuck naturally. This is how you keep the shoulders and elbows healthy. People who just tuck their elbows without the scapular mechanics do not get the same biomechanical safety benefit.
- More advanced users can allow a slight protraction or retraction of the scapula, as long as the lat and scapular depression mechanics stay sound. That might let them isolate the chest better. For most beginners and intermediates, keep the scapula completely static, glued to the bench.
- At the bottom of the lift the shoulders should not roll forward.
- The line of the hands, viewed from the side, is lower on the body than one might think. In flat or horizontal pressing the knuckles end up around the nipple line, or where the abdominals meet the pecs (on women, the line of the sports bra). The more incline the press, the higher up it touches. A high angle incline press touches around the collarbones, or an inch or two below them.
- On a machine with nothing touching the chest, or with dumbbells, the principles, hand position and body position are the same.
- At the bottom, if the scapula are anchored correctly and the position is good, the elbows are directly underneath the weight, and directly underneath its center of gravity. A sign that technique is off is the elbows going behind the center of gravity. Example: watching a bench press from the side, with the bar touching the chest, the elbows are not directly under the palms but rolled slightly back toward the head. That positioning and biomechanics is poor, and the person will eventually suffer an overuse injury.
- Hand position for pressing is different from pulling. The bar rests as low down on the palm as possible, ideally pushing as much of the load as possible into the heel of the palm on the pinky side of the hand. This loads through the ulna as much as possible, which connects the biomechanical chain into the larger muscle groups. If the bar is held higher up in the palm (around the finger crease or the lines of the palm), the wrist has to extend backward, and there is a moment arm between the wrist and the forearm, which creates an energy leak as well as pain. Many people cannot get the bar that low in the palm, because they lack ulnar and radial deviation, but the goal is to do it anyway. It is harder in overhead pressing, especially with a barbell, but the principle is the same.
- The opposite is true for pulling movements: put the bar right at the base of where the fingers start, because that is where it ends up anyway.

**Incline DB bench.** Look at scapular stability and elbow position at the bottom.

**Decline DB bench.** The elbows stay situated under the handles of the dumbbells the entire time. Retracting the scapula is less of a big deal, but depression of the scapula is still important.

**Pushup.**
- Hips locked out and abs keeping the back flat, so there is no break in rigidity from head to toe.
- The scapula stays depressed but freely protracts and retracts.
- The elbows are tucked, but not so far that they roll the shoulders forward.
- The neck is neutral and the chin is tucked.
- Full range is almost chest to floor and up to lockout.
- The fingers should not point at each other, and the elbows should not flare out wide: about 45 degrees when viewed from the top.

**Dip and assisted dip.**
- A slight forward body lean at the top, with the head looking down at the floor in front of them.
- As they descend, the scapula should retract. The idea is to absorb the tension into the upper back and spring out of it.
- The idea is to have something to bounce off of at the bottom. We are not using momentum, but we need some kind of support and stability at the bottom of the lift. We get it by winding up into the upper back and having the lats and triceps flex against each other to create a sort of floor.
- People often get too much forward shoulder roll at the bottom because they cannot stabilize the scapula, due to cueing or weak lower traps that do not depress the scapula properly. Some shoulder roll is inevitable in the dip.
- There are different dip styles, some emphasizing triceps and some chest, such as the Gironda dip. Do not deliberately alter the dip to isolate a muscle group, because it is not worth the risk to the joints. Use the best biomechanically sound position, which is a mix of everything.
- Full depth is the upper arm parallel to the floor at the bottom. Deeper is okay but not necessary, and can stress the shoulder too much.
- Progression: assisted dip machine first, then bodyweight dips, then a dip belt.

## Shoulders

**Seated DB shoulder press and high incline press.**
- Scapular tilt and external shoulder rotation. This creates a torque and tension that anchors the shoulder joint and connects the tension through the chain to the axial body.
- The elbow is always under the handle of the weight, never behind it. A slight tuck of the elbows.
- Depth is by comfort, but the elbows should travel below the shoulders.

**Lateral raise.** It is mostly a technique problem.
- Some internal rotation at the top, with the elbows leading.
- People externally rotate too much, bend their elbows too much, and lift from their hands too much.
- The hands should not go above the elbows.
- "Some" internal rotation means the palm faces the floor, not a full turn. If the palm does not face the floor, the shoulder rolls open and the front delt starts working (Dan).
- Research. In 10 competitive bodybuilders, the medial (side) delt was highest with the arm neutral and clearly lower with the arm externally rotated (ES 1.47). Turning the arm inward shifted work to the rear delt, upper trap and triceps (Coratella et al. 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32824894/). The abstract does not say how far the study's inward turn went compared with a palm-to-floor position, so this does not test Dan's cue.

## Back

**Pulldown.**
- The scapula rotates up at the stretch, stretching up toward the ears.
- The movement is initiated by the scapula depressing, then the momentum is ridden through by driving with the elbows.
- There is a slight external rotation tension on the bar, which winds the elbows forward a little.
- At the bottom of the lift the elbows point down, not back.
- Most people's scapulae do not move at all, so they pull with their hands and then roll their shoulders forward at the bottom, with no lat or low trap usage.

**Pullup and chinup.** All the same rules as the pulldown apply. The person can also cheat the movement:
- Not getting to full extension at the bottom. They do not need a dead hang to make the lift as hard as possible, but they do need to show some degree of range of motion.
- Not getting full contraction at the top. It is easy to keep piling on reps at the end that are not full range of motion and still mark them as PRs. Watch for this. It is a qualitative measurement.
- The top, where the chin is toward or over the bar, is the most challenging part biomechanically, so that is where the lift fails first.

**Assisted pullup or chinup machine.** Everything said for the pulldown and for the pullup and chinup applies here.

**Chest supported DB row.**
- The scapula stays in depression the entire movement, with protraction at the bottom into the stretch.
- The movement is initiated with scapular retraction and then followed through with the elbows.
- From a side view the shoulder joint goes behind the chest, with no forward shoulder roll at the peak squeeze.

**Chest supported row machine.** Exactly the same technique requirements as the dumbbell chest supported row. Any handle works, but a neutral grip is the best choice. A very wide grip might not be strong enough and might not be comprehensive enough across the upper back. A very narrow grip might be too heavy on the lats and overlap too much with the vertical pulling movements. Find a 45 degree, middle of the road grip.

**Seated cable row and other rows without chest support (T-bar, lever T-bar).** Be careful with how they are used. If the person has back problems, Dan tries to avoid non-chest supported row variations. A seated cable row lies somewhere between an unsupported barbell or T-bar row and a chest supported row in terms of risk.
- Start by grabbing the handle and dropping back into the motion, instead of reaching forward to grab it. Lever with the legs.
- A slight amount of forward and backward upper body motion is okay, but it should be minimal.
- The scapula and the lat should stretch, and full contraction is reached at the end.
- The person should not lean back. A slight forward lean at full contraction is more ideal.
- A seated cable row is more lat dominant because the V-bar handle touches low on the body. That is fine.
- Drive the handle into the xiphoid process (where the ribs meet) or low toward the belly button, not to the chest where a bench press would touch. This keeps the scapula from coming out of depression.

**Single arm DB row.** It is a higher skill movement than people think.

**Rear delt fly and face pull.**
- The scapula stays depressed, but freely protracts and retracts.
- Rear delt fly: there is internal rotation in the shoulder, so the elbows point out to the sides at the bottom and to the ceiling at the top. The elbows stay far away from the sides of the body.
- Face pull: the shoulder externally rotates at the top.
- Research. In a prone raise with the thumb up (full external rotation), the rear delt was at 88% of max, and the middle (lateral) delt was almost as high at 87% (Reinold et al. 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15296366/, 10 people). On a reverse fly machine, a neutral hand gave more rear delt and infraspinatus activation than a palms-down hand (Schoenfeld et al. 2013, 19 people). No study compares the rear delt against the lateral delt share, or tests an internally rotated version for that. Dan: "If I turn my thumbs to the ceiling my lateral delt fires way harder." Dan's reasoning for the internal rotation cue on the rear delt fly: if people externally rotate at the top, they hit more lateral delt, and the likelihood of them adducting the arm (pulling it toward the body) goes up. The cue aims to cut the lateral delt's share and keep the arm out wide. It is untested.
- Most people lack shoulder rotation range of motion and scapular mobility, so their elbows pull into the sides of the body and they try to use the teres major and lats instead. This mobility restriction boxes out the position the rear delts actually work in, so the rear delts are extremely atrophied.

**Side lying external rotation.** A remedial exercise, done occasionally, to create some insurance via external rotation strength in the shoulder joint.
- If a person has general shoulder issues, it is an option instead of a second shoulder slot.
- Training the teres minor and infraspinatus lets the shoulder joint center better in the socket, which prevents impingement through full ranges of motion.
- It is not a fix for all shoulder issues. Those are usually a complicated chain between rib cage and thoracic spine mechanics and scapula mechanics. The shoulder joint is simply the final step in the chain.
- The elbow stays pinned to the side of the body.
- A lot of people do not have the scapula retracted enough, so their range of motion is artificially limited. Some people are missing external range of motion as well. All that matters is externally rotating the humerus in the socket. Some people can get the hand almost all the way to 90 degrees, others only 45. Do as much range of motion as the isolation of rotation in the socket allows.
- It cannot be loaded up large and cannot be done to failure with heavy weights. It works much better with the two set, high rep progression (as with the reverse hyper). Start with an extremely light weight and work up from there.
- At first it may create a lot of burning and uncomfortable sensation. If the starting weight is light enough, it will not harm the shoulder. That goes away as the rotator cuff gets stronger and the joint centers in the socket better.

**Kelso shrug.** Prescribed if people have particularly weak scapular motion and weak mid and low trap muscles.
- The technique is exactly the same as a chest supported row, but with zero elbow bending and zero shoulder joint movement.
- The only motion is full scapular protraction at the bottom, then winding into full scapular retraction with a slight pause at full retraction.
- Dumbbells or a barbell both work, with many methods depending on equipment and bench. Some adjustable benches have too much structure underneath, which stops a barbell from reaching full range of motion, so use dumbbells then.
- The goal is to squeeze between the shoulder blades in the middle and low down: retract the shoulder blades while driving them into the back pockets (depression).
- A good Kelso shrug stays out of the neck and upper traps.
- Most people cannot do the movement with zero weight, or any motion at all, because of their technique and the motor patterns wired in their brain.

## Arms

**Dumbbell curl.** Look for cheating: too much swinging, not opening the elbow joint at the bottom, or not keeping the palm turned up. Most people cannot turn their palm up flat for a while.

**Triceps extension and pushdown.**
- Look for too much shoulder movement. People try to turn it into a pullover.
- The elbow joint should bend until the weights touch the shoulders and then lock out.
- The lats need to depress the scapula toward the hips the entire time, or the person cannot put full force through the triceps.
- Look for too much ulnar deviation. People try to move the weight with their hands, so the dumbbells tilt at the top for no reason, to fulfil the idea of a lockout. They need to drive through the heel of the palm. Most people lack the radial deviation to position right.

## Suspension trainer (TRX)

A suspension trainer is a better choice if the person does not have access to equipment. With only a few dumbbells or bands, the ability to gradate progress and improve incrementally is stuck. A suspension trainer can be gradated inch by inch.
- Keep track of two positions: where the feet are (or the hands, in some small circumstances), and how long the strap is. Both are different for each exercise.
- If the person accurately tracks the strap length and the position of their body on the floor in relation to where the strap is anchored, for each exercise, they can track progress very accurately and set PRs incrementally. This is true for all suspension training movements.
- All of these movements should have a slight pause at end range, just to demonstrate a lack of momentum. Do not pause longer than needed and make it harder than it needs to be.
- The strap can rub against the forearms uncomfortably. Wear a long sleeve shirt.

**TRX row.** The exact same scapula mechanics as the chest supported dumbbell row or any rowing motion, with the added need to keep a straight, locked body from head to toe. So it takes a bit of plank mechanics and core strength.

**TRX chest press.** Similar to a pushup, with the same plank mechanics.
- Active scapular depression the entire time. The lats and low traps must fire to do it. If they do not, the shoulders roll up into the ears and the scapula collapse, and the person cannot use the chest muscles effectively or spread the load across the shoulder complex safely.
- Because the straps float freely, they wobble more, so the person needs a stronger grip and even more scapular stability.
- The scapula freely retract and protract during the motion.
- At the bottom the shoulders should not roll forward. If the scapula are effectively depressed, this will not happen, and the pecs get loaded.

**TRX fallout.** An ab wheel rollout in suspension trainer mode, with the same plank mechanics. The hips do not need to sit back. They can stay extended the entire time.

## Legs

**Leg press, hack squat and pendulum squat.** Very challenging to mess up. They are all machine movements, so each person just changes their foot position, or how much they push their knees apart, to fit their own proportions. That is a case-by-case basis, with much less risk of injury or overuse issues.

**Front loaded squats (goblet squat, heels elevated front squat).**
- Start with the body stacked: ankle, knee, hip and shoulders in the same vertical line from the side.
- Most people do not start with their hips forward under the body. Their hips tilt back a little, and then they overextend the spine to get the load over their center of gravity. Instead, start with the hips totally forward under the body and locked out, then tilt the rib cage down slightly with a cough or slight exhale. That connects the rib cage to the pelvis and locks them together as a single piece, so the force transmits through the axial skeleton into the load held on the front, and the thighs and legs do all the work.
- From that start, unsqueeze the glutes, tip the hips back slightly first, then drive straight down between the legs. Put the axial body between the legs instead of bending the legs and staying on top of them.
- For a lot of people the knees need to be driven apart as the body descends between the legs. The goal is to squat straight between the heels, so the stance is slightly outside the width of the pelvis. How far apart to drive the knees, and how far to sit back, depend on body proportions.
- At the bottom, use the glutes to drive straight up and all the way back to the start position.
- As people get fatigued or sloppy, they just straighten the knees. Their top position is slightly bent forward, with the knees locked out but the hips not driven all the way through.
- This needs core tightness. Bracing with a breath hold is fine, and you can breathe between reps (see programming.md, section 23).

**Hip abduction machine.** Tell the person in plain English, not biomechanical terms.
- They should feel it up around the bony point at the side of the hip (the greater trochanter), and ideally between that point and the top edge of the hip bone.
- They should not feel the outside of the quad working all the way down toward the side of the knee. That is the tensor fasciae latae (TFL) trying to compensate for a lack of hip abduction and external rotation strength. The target is the glute medius and glute minimus.
- They are on the right track when the outer glute around the hip joint is working, instead of the crease of the hip that extends down the thigh.
- A slight forward lean can often help them feel it better.
- Hold peak extension for a moment. Peak extension is just the knees at their furthest distance apart.

**Leg extension.** Just peak lockout for a moment. That is about it for technique.

**Single leg press.**
- Do the weak leg first, then match it with the strong leg. Everybody has a weak leg. The primary reason is that one hip does not use its musculature as well as the other. The person finds it harder to feel the glute squeeze correctly on the weak leg, which creates muscle imbalances: more quad and hamstring development on one leg and more glute development on the other. This is normal. Doing the weak leg first and matching it with the strong leg counteracts it.
- Foot position: as low on the pad as possible, so the person gets as deep as possible into the knee joint. The higher the foot is on the pad, the more it de-emphasizes the quad and knee and emphasizes the glute and adductor. The lower on the pad, the more it emphasizes the quad, with glute action still present. The limit is ankle mobility. The heel must stay flat.

**ATG split squat.** This movement fixes more lower body problems than anything else, and people hate doing it.
- Full ankle flexion and full knee flexion, with the knee all the way out in front past the toe.
- The hips go to rock bottom, dropping straight down right behind the front foot heel.
- The back leg is long and straight. The pelvis is neutral.
- The upper body is vertical, not leaned forward like most single leg movements.
- The weight is spread 50/50 on each foot, not 80/20 like a reverse lunge.

**Reverse lunge.**
- A big, long, greedy stride back.
- A slight forward lean of the upper body at the bottom.
- Weight shifted mostly to the heel of the front foot. The front knee goes forward but not far.
- The glute drives smoothly from bottom to top. The center of gravity does not shift back and forward.
- The back leg takes minimal load.
- The hip locks out at the top, driving forward and back under the body so the joints are stacked.

**Bulgarian split squat.**
- Weight on the front heel and minimal weight on the back foot.
- The knee comes forward but the foot stays flat.
- The back knee almost taps the floor.
- The glute drives from the bottom up to almost the top. The body position will not let you lock out the glute hard at the top.

**Sissy squat.**
- Anyone with tight hip flexors should not do it. It is not for overweight people.
- The person must stay in the hip thrust locked out position (hips fully extended) to spread the tension through the whole quad muscle belly. Otherwise the stress isolates in the patellar tendon and rectus femoris. Tight hip flexors make this impossible.
- The person needs a lot of time in the deep squat position, or the loaded knee through full flexion (hamstrings to calves), the way the ATG split squat does.
- The movement has a barrier of entry that requires high strength and mobility.

**Leg curl.** It depends on the type.
- A machine curl is whatever.
- On a ball curl the hips stay locked in extension the whole time, with a slight tailbone tuck toward the chin. The abs and glutes work to do this. It loads the hamstring higher up (the proximal side) more heavily, not just behind the knee.
- Most people are extremely weak at the ball curl and cannot do a single rep, even trained gym goers.
- Hip flexor mobility restrictions can box out the locked out position entirely and force the lower back to extend instead (see [programming.md](programming.md), section 23).

**Nordic ham curl.** As much of the eccentric time as possible happens below 60 degrees, and ideally there is no give out through to the bottom with a free fall. This is subjective, there is no black and white. It is not an ideal movement to use. It is for a person who can do max reps on single leg ball hamstring curls and has no leg curl machine or glute ham raise bench.

**Hip thrust.**
- The same full extension: locked out, clenched glutes, a slight tailbone tuck, and a split second hold at lockout.
- The weight goes through the heels.
- The bench should not slide back, because that means the person is pushing with their quads. It is not a push, it is a tuck and drive up to the ceiling.
- Research. Glute activation was 87% (lower glute max) against 45% for the back squat in 13 trained women (Contreras et al. 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26214739/). In 8 trained men the Romanian deadlift matched the hip thrust (Delgado et al. 2019). In the only growth trial, 34 untrained adults over 9 weeks gained similar glute size from the hip thrust and the squat, and the squat grew the quads and adductors more (Plotkin et al. 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37877099/, MRI). The comparison was with the back squat, which TTM excludes, so the hip thrust is well supported as a glute movement, not shown to beat other options.

**Back raise.** Back raises and reverse hypers move opposite parts of the body.
- All the movement occurs at the hip joint, not the spine.
- The hamstrings stretch deep at the bottom, then the glutes lock out at the top with a tailbone tuck and a small ab crunch.
- It is not a down-up movement. You push your hips into the ceiling behind you, then drive them forward against the pad toward the floor in front of you.
- A good back raise has zero lower back movement and works the hamstrings and glutes. Hip flexor tightness and weak glutes prevent this.

**Reverse hyper.** A rhythmic exercise.
- Do not slow the negative. There are many technique tweaks that emphasize various parts of the body, but in general the knees stay locked out.
- Let the legs swing with momentum under the body, then spill the tailbone to the floor and squeeze the glutes to come back up. How far you arch up depends on how the joint feels.
- It is not a good movement for 1 set to failure. Do two sets instead, adding small increments when both sets have maxed out reps. Start with bodyweight, then add the pendulum weight.
- A reverse hyper machine that blocks the legs from swinging all the way under is unusable.
- The benefit is a shear free, unloaded exercise focused on the lumbar erectors, with a traction effect alternating with a blood pumping effect. It is therapeutic in nature and strengthening. It is the best lower back isolation movement that exists.
- Research. By "unloaded" Dan means less shear and compression on the lower back. Lawrence et al. 2019 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30946266/, 20 recreational lifters, equal loads) measured the net torque at the low back, not shear or compression, and found it 129% higher at the peak on the reverse hyper than on a hyperextension, with less measured lumbar flexion (20.4 against 31.1 degrees). Cuthbert et al. 2021 (10 people) found 34-71% higher peak activation of the erectors, glutes and hamstrings on the reverse hyper. The two studies disagree on the glutes. Neither measured shear or traction, so Dan's claim is untested. Dan's position: back raises (hyperextensions) are better for the glutes and hamstrings, and the reverse hyper is better for the lumbar erectors. Lawrence supports the first half (higher glute and hamstring activation on the hyperextension) and found equal erector activation. Cuthbert found higher activation of everything, including the erectors, on the reverse hyper. So the evidence is mixed. The reverse hyper also covered more total hip movement (76.6 against 64.7 degrees).

**Calf raise.**
- Look for actual ankle range of motion. It is possible to have very minimal ankle motion and go up and down by moving the rest of the body.
- No crazy bounce off the bottom, but you do not have to slow it down on purpose.
- No peak squeeze is needed.

## Abs

**Plank.** Locked out glutes with a slight tailbone tuck, hips down, and knees straight. Diaphragmatic breathing is encouraged but not mandatory.

**Stir the pot.**
- All the same rules as the front plank for the lower back, hips and body position.
- The body holds static while the forearms make slow circles on the ball, clockwise alternating with counterclockwise. The full forearms rest on the ball and the fingers are clasped together.
- Common mistakes: sagging the body, moving the whole body with the ball by pushing through the toes, and resting only on the points of the elbows instead of the entire forearm.
- The circles do not need to be fast or slow, because the movement is done for total time. How many circles in each direction is irrelevant, as long as each circle alternates.
- It is more challenging than a regular front plank, because the demands and the center of gravity keep shifting. The core works much harder in a micro-eccentric way, which creates a large burn.
- It is an excellent exercise for people who can do a 90 second front plank but cannot reliably get weight on their lower back. If they can do 90 seconds of alternating stir the pots, they have graduated the exercise.
- It can be loaded, but the feet would need to be elevated on a bench so the plate does not slip off, and it is impossible without a lifting partner. That adds constraints and would violate the rules of minimal sacrifice.

**Side plank.**
- One foot on top of the other, the hand on the hip, and a straight line from head to the side of the foot.
- The hips want to drive back to make it easier. They must remain locked out.
- A second point of failure: the topside shoulder (the one not supporting the load) rolls over, so the body turns it into a front plank, which is biomechanically easier.
- It can be weighted by putting a plate on the side of the hip, over the greater trochanter.
- Stop performing it once the person's weak point is simply the shoulder's ability to hold up all of the load.

**Roman chair side hold.** Another version of the side plank, where the limiting factor is no longer the shoulder's ability to stabilize the weight, because the person hangs off the side of the Roman chair.
- Hold a dumbbell in the hand hanging right below the shoulder, and a stopwatch in the other hand.
- Foot anchoring: the top leg is in front, and the back leg is behind, where they attach to the pads. Flexing the thighs creates more stability and anchoring.
- The hold slowly degrades as the dumbbell sags lower and lower. Pick the dumbbell up from something that is extremely close to the position they need to be in. If the dumbbell sags low enough to rest back down where it started from, they are no longer doing the exercise.

**Captain's chair knee raise.**
- The person rests on the captain's chair with the elbows and forearms on the pad, and depresses the scapula to keep the body elevated.
- Drive the knees up into the chest and do not let the feet swing behind the body at the bottom.
- The tailbone should be driven up toward the chin on each rep.
- This movement can suffer some momentum and body English in the pursuit of PRs. As long as they are aware of that, it is okay.
- Progress to straight leg captain's chair raises after bent knee raises reach the top end of the rep range.
- These are better choices than hanging variations, because the limiting factor for hanging is often grip strength. With straps, or if grip is not an issue, the hanging versions are fine too.

**Ab wheel.** A dynamic roll out into a plank position, from the knees with the arms outstretched in front of the body.
- The lever arm is similar to a plank (the arms are way out in front instead of below the shoulders, but the person is on their knees), so it is a wash between the two anchor points.
- The biggest mistake is not extending the hips all the way through. As you roll out, lead by driving the hips through and tucking the tailbone, into a tucked tailbone, glutes locked out, plank position. The arms extend out at the same time.
- On female clients the limiting factor is often upper body strength, not ab strength. The triceps, lats and the sternal head of the pec major do a great deal of work to stabilize with the wheel way out in front.
- A full range of motion rep is either a gentle nose tap to the floor, so the person controls the end range, or being able to rest the forearms flat on the ground in full extension.
- It can be weighted, but putting a plate on the back usually just overloads the upper body and is not worth it.
- The jump from knees to toes is exponentially harder. Once someone can do 30 clean ab wheel rollouts, they should move on to other, more weighted abs variations. Rolling up or down a ramp from the toes is logistically tricky. Someone who knows how to alter the rollout can do that. If someone is trying to figure out what to do, it is clear they do not really understand the movement.

**Situp (decline or Roman chair).** The main thing is that it does not hurt the person's back. A slight pause at the bottom is good. On the Roman chair, do not go below parallel, and keep the tailbone tucked so the lumbar spine does not extend.
