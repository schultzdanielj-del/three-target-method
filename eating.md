# Eating: how to run a person's eating

## Quick start (read this first; read the rest only when the person raises something it covers)

For a typical person who works hard all day and wants the least sacrifice. Everything here is a rule already stated below or in [protocol/eating.md](protocol/eating.md); nothing new.

1. Check first, briefly: lifting experience (the method is for people who already know the lifts), age (55 or older: not built for them, say so and stop), any medication, medical condition, pregnancy or eating disorder history (say the one-line doctor check once; pregnant or nursing: the fat loss plan is not for them), and when they wake up. One question at a time, short.
2. Mode 1 runs from waking until about 3:30-5 pm: 4 palms of protein, 3 fists of vegetables (at least one cruciferous a day), and 1 or 2 thumbs of fats only if every protein source is ultra lean. Any order, any combination, eat all of it, and no other foods in that time. Zero calorie drinks, diet soda, coffee with a splash of milk or creamer are fine (about 30 calories or less). Bigger people have bigger hands.
3. Mode 2 is the rest of the day: whatever you want within reason, plus one more palm of protein. Eat healthy whenever you have the chance (fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein), but Mode 2 is for freedom. A big Mode 2 is not blowing it.
4. A win is Mode 1 adhered to. Weekends: still eat all the Mode 1 foods; try to keep it to one meal that is not Mode 1 foods.
5. Log one yes or no a day for each: Mode 1 portions hit, and the extra palm of protein. No calorie counting, no calorie target, no meal plan (if they want one, ask questions and they write their own). Weigh-ins are their call; look at a channel of data, not one number, until they are in their cruise weight range, then it is PRs and the mirror.
6. If fat loss stalls, the answer is almost always slipping adherence: pay attention again, tone down Mode 2, or add steps.
7. If they have a question not covered here, find the topic below by its heading (travel, shift work, women, underweight, GLP-1, alcohol, sickness, hunger, supplements, vegetarian or vegan, medical conditions) and read only that section. Where the repo is silent, say so.

These rules come from his answers and his coaching, and add to [protocol/eating.md](protocol/eating.md) and [faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md). They never replace them. They are anecdotal (17 years of coaching), not studies. Where a study is cited, it is named.

## 1. The deal

The design, in Dan's words: it does the biggest bang for buck habits in a way that forgives poor behavior, and then adds from there. The diet problem is not "what works". Fewer calories work, with enough protein and adequate nutrition. That is a done deal. The problem is the least sacrifice required to stay adherent.

The eating style gives the person the best chance of success possible. The rules earn the most freedom and the least sacrifice while still reaching the goal. After that they make their own choices, knowing this is as easy as it gets. It is their call to give up that deal.

Do your best as much as you can, but a win is Mode 1 adhered to. A win does not guarantee fat loss: it puts you on third base, and you still have to show up to bat (Dan). Adherence needs to survive low points, not high points. Aside from being really sick, your worst week should still be able to check the boxes. The pain point is when people find it too hard to keep doing, for any reason (emotional, physical, mental), and stop adhering.

People fail fat loss because they go backwards and stop, not because of how fast they lose it. If you stop doing what makes you lose the weight, you gain it back. That is arithmetic.

People want either flexibility and freedom or hard rules. They cannot have both.

Do not write a calorie plan. Do not count calories. There are no meal plans: the idea is to follow guidelines, and meal plans are too rigid. In Dan's experience, people who ask for a meal plan have already decided not to be adherent. If a person still wants one, that is their choice, but the AI has to ask them questions to write it, so really they are writing their own.

If they ask whether to follow the rules when they are not following them, the answer is "do it". The system fixes obstacles. It cannot fix "not doing it".

## 2. Emotional bandwidth

Many questions here will be psychological or emotional. This is soft skill coaching. We use emotional bandwidth to make ourselves do things that are restrictive or short term sacrifices. The method's job is the cheapest possible cost on that bandwidth. People often tailor what they will do to their bandwidth and trim the cost until almost nothing is expected of them, like taking a single pill, and even that often is not adhered to. Once the cheapest cost has been delivered, it is the person's job to pay for it. If they cannot pay for it, they do not have a health and fitness problem. They have an emotional regulation, accountability and self clarity of values problem, and that is not the method's job to handle.

## 3. Why it works (the AI explains this)

People have to understand why the style works or they have no accountability. They blindly ask for rules, and when the rules get hard they drop everything because they cannot make a contextual decision. The idea: keep unstructured eating for the parts of life where other values are in play, like family or social events. During work hours, or whenever it is just you eating, eat the Mode 1 foods. That puts a very large part of the day in a caloric deficit while checking off the mandatory food types. There is almost always a solution for whatever kind of day someone is having that gets them very close to or on the mark. There may be adjustments here and there. The goal is a very large part of the day in a deficit while eating the mandatory foods, then finding the moments that would take a much greater sacrifice and using the wiggle room from the deficit to fill that gap.

The structure puts the strict part in a chunk of the day when most people are working and busy, and saves the calories for the time of day when it is hardest to be perfect anyway (Dan).

The floor and the swings (Dan). The floor is so high that the damage from a bad week is barely anything, and in a good week you are absolutely killing it: drawdowns are capped and breakouts have a tailwind. You still have to pilot yourself in Mode 2, but you can live your life, work around things and have some freedom. Some nights you might have salmon, rice and vegetables and go to bed with a monster deficit. Other nights it is wings and trivia at the bar. The aggregate is a deficit over time with bare minimum sacrifice. You are not wildly hungry all the time, and you are not saying no to every temptation 24 hours a day. No study tests this aggregate pattern.

The hybrid car metaphor. It does not translate directly into the science of how it works, but it is a good way to view the structure. The body is a hybrid car that runs on gas or electricity. Gas is carbohydrates, glycogen or blood sugar. Electricity is body fat stores. When you are cruising around, you should be in electric mode all the time. When you do hard activity, you burn gas. If you keep filling the tank with gas, there is too much gas in the tank and the body struggles to switch to electric mode. The goal of the eating style is to fill the gas tank only as much as it needs to be filled to do your lifting, filled by the unavoidable moments of life that conflict with other values. The rest of your life is electric mode: a big caloric deficit, with the difference filled by tapping body fat stores, while you eat the mandatory foods for health and body composition.

## 4. Portions

Bigger people have bigger hands. The hand sizes the portions: 4 palms of protein, 3 fists of vegetables, 1-2 thumbs of fats in Mode 1, and one more palm of protein in Mode 2. That gets a bigger or smaller person about 90% of the way there. If they think they need more, they make up the difference in Mode 2.

Protein target: eating the portions gets you to about 1.6 g/kg (0.73 g/lb), more or less. See section 40 for what the research does and does not say.

The Mode 1 foods are about 800 calories of the day. That is not the day's total: they are the mandatory food types and general amounts, a minimum, and Mode 2 follows them.

## 5. Setting up the first day

There are two kinds of people: those who put in a daily effort to eat the right thing, and those who don't. Everyone falls into one camp, and both can fail at times or not get the result they want.

Group 1 can tell you what a good day of eating looks like for them, and the staple meals they eat when they are on point. They give all that to the AI, and the AI makes a bimodal (Mode 1 and Mode 2) version of it. This is the "writing their own plan by answering questions" case from section 1. Exception: for anyone on diabetes medication or insulin, the AI does not build the day (see section 33).

An example from Dan: the person's agent might load up a meal plan and even order the food for them. If the agent can place grocery orders, it builds the list from the Mode 1 food lists using what the person says they like, the person confirms the order before anything is bought, and the AI does not recommend stores or brands. Group 2 needs to decide that they will eat certain foods. They pick foods from the list and start with that. A food they can't or won't eat: don't eat it, and pick another from the list. If there is an entire category of foods they won't eat, they have no solution and cannot do the plan. Tell them plainly and stop.

## 6. Tracking in an app

Dan's opinion is that counting calories in an app does not work well. If they eat the right foods, the macros line up well enough. If they try to hit the numbers, they rarely eat the right foods. The FAQ allows counting the Mode 1 macros once, to check the choices are adequate. Someone who wants a number for protein can simply count one day of eating and see where their protein is. There is no formula (Dan). They switch to tracking that they ate the right types and amounts of Mode 1 foods.

## 7. Foods not on the list: protein

People will fail if they cannot easily tell what a food type is and how to use it in Mode 1. Dan's answers for protein foods not on the list:
- Cottage cheese: awesome.
- Deli meat: stick to ham, chicken or turkey instead of the higher fat kinds. It will do the job, but not as an everyday thing.
- Bacon and sausage: no, unless lean, like peameal bacon or leaner sausages. Most have about 20 g of fat.
- Shellfish: sure.
- Tofu and tempeh: sure, but not as a staple unless the person is vegetarian.
- Beans and lentils: good food, but they are not primarily a protein source and not a non-starchy vegetable. They are primarily carbohydrate. No.
- Whole milk: no.
- Yogurt: only high protein, low sugar: skyr or Greek. Plain, or if they must, one of the new low sugar flavoured ones with under 10 g of sugar.
- Protein powder: any protein powder is fine, any style.

## 8. Foods not on the list: vegetables

- Corn is not a vegetable.
- Potato is not: it is a root/tuber. Carrot is okay.
- Peas: no.
- Squash: yes.
- Vegetable juice: no.
- Frozen mixes are fine even with some corn sprinkled in. This is not rocket science: the goal is to generally hit the mark.
- Everything else is good (onions, tomatoes, mushrooms, zucchini, lettuce, cauliflower rice, pickles).
- Salad: you cannot drown it in croutons and dressing.

## 9. Fats

A common pitfall: most people who make an effort to eat well and have staple meals have swung the "carbs are bad" pendulum all the way to the other side. They add healthy but copious fats to every meal, like avocado, olive oil, dressing and nuts. All healthy choices, but they throw in hundreds of extra needless calories. That is why the rule is a thumb of fats.

A thumb is about 1 tablespoon at most. You only add fat thumbs if literally every protein source you have is ultra lean, which is unlikely for almost everyone. Someone who eats eggs, salmon, dark meat or ground meat has their fat absolutely already covered. People don't need as much fat as they think for optimal health (Dan's view). Lots of people easily eat 100 g or more of fat a day, and they are not low carb. Any protein portion that comes with 10 g of fat or more already counts as a fat thumb (see [protocol/eating.md](protocol/eating.md)).

## 10. Are carbs bad

People will ask whether carbs are bad and how many to eat. Carbs are not bad. You should eat them every day. They are not Mode 1 foods, because Mode 1 is not the time for them; they go in Mode 2. Most people will eat enough carbs and do just fine, because they are not very muscular or physically active. They don't need to deliberately add them, other than maybe a fruit to kick off Mode 2, and that fruit is not mandatory. If energy is consistently poor, the FAQ says to add carb servings after work. The more muscle someone carries, the more carbs they benefit from (section 15).

## 11. Women

Portions are the same as for men. Not all women need the morning carbs. Not everyone's body is the same. Women can try it out. It is not required of every woman. A woman can try it if she is not seeing the outcome she likes, which means difficulty with cravings and appetite regulation. The change: eat in the morning and before training (one meal can count as both), and add up to a half cupped handful (about 15g) of high-fiber carbs to the morning Mode 1 meal (berries, a bit of grains, a little fruit, legumes). This is the one exception to Mode 1 Rule 3 for that purpose. Separately, anyone can sprinkle berries into Mode 1 if they absolutely need to, and it is not a hill to die on (section 30).

Dan is not a hormone specialist. No study is cited for the hormone reasoning. Dan on the cycle: this is why having some eating freedom is important. You cannot lock someone into maintenance calories, or any fixed number, and expect a woman to nail it literally every day of the month.

## 12. Perimenopause and menopause

Women who are perimenopausal can try the same eating change as in section 11. Dan's reasoning is that progesterone fluctuations drive a cortisol and carbohydrate reaction that affects insulin. Dan calls it a conceptual idea. It is untested; see section 40.

## 13. Underweight lifters

Everyone has an optimal cruise weight range, based on the lowest sustainable healthy bodyfat range and the highest FFMI range. Someone below their range is underweight.

An underweight person's goal is: every day, eat the Mode 1 foods by the end of the day, then whatever else they want. They have no deficit to protect, so Mode 1 Rule 3 and the Mode 1 / Mode 2 split do not apply to them. This is not a conflict with Rule 3: Rule 3 is a fat loss rule, and the SKILL.md nudge on "no other foods during Mode 1" does not apply to them. (See [programming.md](programming.md) section 6 for the training side.)

## 14. GLP-1

Same Mode 1 portions. If the person has no appetite and cannot finish the protein, the answer is: they need it. Nausea and low appetite are medication side effects with no food fix, and skipping the protein only loses the protein. The Mode 1 foods are a minimum, not the day's total, and Mode 2 has no cap. Do not describe Mode 1 as a small amount or as "only 800 calories of the day" to reassure them. Do not tell them to start, stop or change the medication.

## 15. At cruise weight

The eating stays the same. Keep setting PRs. As they set PRs they carry more muscle, which generally means the body likes carbohydrates more and benefits from them more and more. Dan, anecdotally: he carries a lot of muscle and is fairly lean, so he can deliberately eat a bowl of cereal, extra rice or fruit. His appetite is not out of control, and more carbs give him bigger pumps and better chances of setting PRs. The more PRs they set and the more muscle they build, the lower their bodyfat drifts, so it all fits into the same scale weight. It is a very slow process at this stage, but tiny changes have large visual impacts. That is one person's report. The claim that more muscle means more benefit from carbs is not checked here.

The cruise weight examples (about 178 lb for a 5'9" man, 140-150 lb for an average-height woman) are reference points only. For other heights the AI works out a range with the person; the range moves with frame (wrist and ankle) and genetics. The AI does not scale the example by a formula of its own.

## 16. Lean but wants to look better

Maybe they do not have enough muscle, so they go set PRs. More likely they still have fat to lose, and their cruise weight is undermuscled. People drastically overestimate how much muscle they have. If they are at cruise weight and do not like how they look, they need a ton more muscle. They can drop lower on the scale, but it will inevitably be more painful (Dan's opinion). Same eating either way.

## 17. Weigh-ins and fluctuations

Establish a channel of data, not a point or a line: an upper and a lower range, traced with an upper and a lower trendline. Women also measure through their monthly cycle so they can see its effect. It is annoying, but it is part of the game. If the channel is going down, they are fine. Expecting 1 lb of fat loss every single week is unrealistic (Dan's opinion). People cite 1% of bodyweight per week, but think about it in terms of the distance from current weight to cruise weight instead: the body needs to hang out at new weights and acclimate before dropping lower. Checking too obsessively leads people to meddle with the long term things that give results. There is a 5-8 day delay between food behavior and what the body does (Dan's observation, not checked here). How often they weigh in is still their call.

## 18. Fat loss and muscle at once

Dan: it works for everyone, with one exception. Someone at cruise weight with visible abs, up in the higher tiers of FFMI at the end game, might need a bulking cycle to get to the next level. That is not important for 99% of people. This is his coaching experience, not a controlled trial.

## 19. Stalls

What to do depends on how close the person is to their cruise weight range. Options: add steps, or stop going nuts in Mode 2. No stall length and no step number is given, because people's bodies are not buckets you pour out; it is more complicated than that. Name the options and let the person choose.

Dan: 99% of the time the reason for a stall is obvious: slipping adherence and ignoring what you are eating (his estimate from coaching). The answer is to pay attention again. The scale matters until the person is in their cruise weight range, and after that it is PRs and the mirror. His view: with this system you are in the best possible position mentally and biologically to exert that willpower, and with any other program you would be fighting an uphill battle (opinion).

## 20. Alcohol, social eating, parties, restaurants

These are all Mode 2. Alcohol is not a Mode 1 food. The best amount is none, but it is their choice. In Mode 2, get the extra palm of protein, then do not eat like it is a competition.

## 21. Weekends

Same as the FAQ (all Mode 1 foods that day, and try to keep it to one non Mode 1 foods meal): ideally still eat all the Mode 1 foods and portions, no matter what, because weekends are more unstructured for most people. The sooner they eat whatever in the day, the more the whole day can fall apart. It is their call. If they have social events at lunchtime, they can choose to have a Mode 1 dinner. If they want to go to everything and say no to nothing, they must still eat all their Mode 1 foods and accept they could be in a caloric surplus that day. That is just reality.

## 22. Sickness

Sick from the neck down: whatever they can handle. Sick from the neck up: eat all the foods. (Dan's rule of thumb. This is the one exception to the FAQ's "always eat your Mode 1 foods no matter what" and to the daily nudge on Mode 1 portions. The doctor check in SKILL.md ground rule 3 still applies once for a stated medical condition.)

## 23. Pregnant or nursing

This is a fat loss diet and it is not for pregnant or nursing people. Mode 1 foods are good for you no matter the goal: Dan's view is that for a human being without a kidney condition, including the Mode 1 foods is optimal regardless of goals; a kidney condition goes to their doctor. So the AI tells them the fat loss plan is not for them, and that they can eat the Mode 1 foods every day, and whatever else, as ordinary food. Say the one-line doctor check once.

## 23a. If the goal is not fat loss

You do not need to eat only Mode 1 foods at certain times if your goal is not fat loss, for example an athlete with high energy needs. But you have to eat the Mode 1 foods every day with pretty much any successful eating style (Dan's opinion). This is the same rule as for underweight lifters in section 13.

## 23b. Religious and cultural eating

A food they cannot eat (for example pork or some shellfish) is skipped, and they pick another from the lists (section 5). Vegetarians can try it (section 27). During a religious fast, Mode 1 cannot start when they wake up: they eat all the Mode 1 foods every day by the end of the day.

## 23c. Cost

The AI can work out the cheaper foods from the existing lists. No new rule is needed (Dan does this himself).

## 24. Hungry during Mode 1

It depends on when they are hungry during the day. Move the portions around (Rule 2 allows any order and combination). This is usually not a problem you solve during Mode 1 hours.

## 25. Not hungry in the morning

Then they don't have to eat in the morning. It does not matter when during Mode 1 hours the portions are eaten, or in what combination. Eat them all. If they truly are not hungry or were busy, they can move 1 protein to a night time snack. A woman who is using the morning eating change from section 11 is the exception.

## 26. The Mode 2 protein palm

A shake counts. It cannot be skipped, but a lot of things count: a meal like pizza probably handles a protein block. You would have to eat only something like potato chips in Mode 2 to miss it, and Dan says that would be extreme. Timing does not matter. Dan's own practice (anecdote): he often eats his yogurt as a night snack and does only 3 proteins during the day because he is focused and working, and sometimes eats it in Mode 1 hours if he is hungry.

## 27. Vegetarian and vegan

Vegetarians can try it. It will be tough: they will probably have to eat the same thing daily, with no variety. Vegans: this eating style is not for them. They need to make more sacrifices in their life to meet their nutritional needs. Tell a vegan that it is not for them: they cannot really run the plan. They can use the principles. All vegans would benefit hugely from a list of mandatory foods that fill the majority of their nutritional needs, even if it does not nail protein or the timing, just for health. That is not the plan, but it is a decent tip.

## 28. Night hunger, and eating Mode 1 style all day

Night hunger has a variety of causes. A big one is staying up too late and looking for dopamine. Simple habit gating, like phone lockouts or brushing teeth, trims down how much of it happens. (The FAQ steps still apply: sleep first, then a fifth protein serving.)

Many people go all or nothing: "if Mode 1 is so great, I'll eat Mode 1 style in Mode 2 time too." That does not last long. They can do it 1-2 days a week, but not every day, because they are just depleting themselves. They need calories and carbs.

## 29. Drinks in Mode 1

Diet soda and energy drinks are fine. It is not impactful versus the sacrifice of not having them. A splash of milk or creamer in coffee is fine too: nobody got fat from that. The FAQ's roughly 30 calorie guide and its advice to skip milk as a drink stay.

## 30. Bars, powders, fruit

Berries can be sprinkled in. They are high fiber and low calorie, so they sneak under the radar, which is why they suit the women's rule in section 11. Two allowances, kept separate: the women's morning change in section 11 (up to a half cupped handful, about 15 g, of high-fiber carbs) is the one exception to Mode 1 Rule 3 for that purpose; and anyone can sprinkle berries into Mode 1 if they absolutely need to, though it is not a hill to die on (Dan). Do not otherwise add fruit to Mode 1. Fruit is an awesome way to start Mode 2, and Dan recommends eating lots of it instead of junk. Mode 2 is for freedom, but eat healthy whenever you have the chance: nutritious carbs, another protein block, another veggie, etc. Protein bars are for unique days, like travel, not an everyday staple. Sugar alcohols probably don't count (in the net carb figure).

## 31. Training time

It does not matter whether they lift during Mode 1 or Mode 2, and the AI tells them nothing about eating around the workout. There is real science on meal timing, workout timing and anabolic windows. Dan's position: all of it costs more sacrifice than is remotely worth it and piles on massive adherence risk. Eat the right foods inside the window and train the muscle groups. (That all of it costs more sacrifice than is worth it is his judgment.) Evidence: a meta-analysis of 23 trials found no significant effect of protein timing around training on strength or growth once total protein intake was controlled for, though the simple pooled result showed a small to moderate effect on growth (Schoenfeld 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/). The one exception is the women's rule in section 11 (eat before training).

## 32. The first days

They might be a little mentally foggy for a few days while adapting, but this is not a zero carb diet. Bloating: try different foods within the categories. Dan does not see why someone would be constipated eating this way (3 fists of vegetables a day), so the AI does not build a rule for it.

## 33. Diabetes and other medical conditions

Dan cannot give dietician advice for a medical condition. His opinion is that for type 2 diabetes the Mode 1 window should be helpful, not harmful. That is his opinion, not a studied result. The AI says the one-line doctor check once (SKILL.md ground rule 3) and follows the method if they go ahead.

For anyone on diabetes medication or insulin, the AI does not build the day, does not promise that shaky spells will go away, and does not set rules about gaps between meals. It says the one-line doctor check once and leaves the plan to them and their doctor.

Dan's view (opinion, not medical advice): it depends on the type of diabetes. With type 2, the body needs to get better at switching to the "electric car mode" of section 3, and it will be stubborn. If you ask too much of it too soon it can crash on you, and then you have to eat something, because that is a medical emergency. People turn that into "if i feel hungry a bit i have to just slam sugar," which fixes the emergency and sustains the chronic condition. It is triage, not treatment. Limit: a true blood sugar crash is mainly a risk with insulin and some diabetes drugs, and mild hunger is not that. A doctor sets the plan for anyone on those drugs. The AI does not give medical advice. Dan's view above stays his opinion; the AI does not restate it as its own instruction, and "if you feel shaky, eat something" is something for the person to take to their doctor, not a medical instruction from the AI.

Medical edge cases are dietitian questions: blood thinners such as warfarin (vitamin K in greens), thyroid conditions, kidney disease, and similar. They are outside the method. The AI says the one-line doctor check once and does not give advice on them (Dan).

## 34. Travel and Mode 1 foods not available

A tiered layer of defense. Only tier 1 (bring the foods) comes first. Tiers 2 to 5 are unranked options, not an order of use: the person picks what fits. Whatever they pick, all the Mode 1 foods are still eaten that day.

1. Bring the foods ahead of time.
2. Eat Mode 1 around an unavoidable meal, like a work lunch, then have a Mode 1 meal at dinner.
3. Don't eat. If your metabolism is working right, that should not be a big deal.
4. Make the closest to a Mode 1 type choice.
5. Eat more of the Mode 1 foods at a different time in the window.

## 35. Shift work

Mode 1 starts when they wake up. Mode 2 starts at whatever time equates to similar timing. Shift workers benefit from 1 mg of melatonin before bed more than most people would (Dan's recollection from years ago that there is some evidence for shift work and not for normal schedules, not a study he checked; see section 40 for what is checked). Where the repo is silent, the AI says so. It does not invent routine rules such as "keep it the same most days" or set clock times.

## 36. Supplements

Creatine monohydrate (5 g a day), vitamin D and fish oil as in [faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md). All are optional. Vitamin D: Dan takes 4000 IU a day, which is his own practice and not advice. 4000 IU is the official upper limit for adults, and the usual recommended intake is lower (600 IU a day to age 70). The evidence supports vitamin D for people who are deficient and does not show benefit above the usual intake in healthy adults (section 40). Dan's view is that it is certainly more than 400 IU and that the research has been going on a long time. The dose and any blood test are for their doctor. Melatonin for shift workers is in section 35. Nothing else is added. For nutrient gaps, Dan's answer is: or just take a multivitamin.

## 37. Struggling, guilt and "I blew it"

What "blew it" means:
- Missing or breaking Mode 1 is blowing it. Reply: just eat nutritiously today, and don't do that again.
- A big Mode 2 is not really blowing it.

If they feel guilty about what they ate in Mode 2, they can eat nutritious foods instead: fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein.

If they hate it, check in this order:
1. Are they undereating Mode 1 because of an "all or nothing" mentality, and then feeling bad the next day?
2. Have they acclimated? It takes about 4 days, maybe longer if they are in rough shape.
3. If both are handled, it is a trade. They ask themselves whether they care about being leaner, or about eating whatever they want all the time instead of every day for a chunk of the day. That is the trade they are struggling to make.

If they want more variety than the food lists give: Dan says that is valuing food as entertainment more than being lean. It is an unbelievably reasonable deal, the best possible one in fact. If it is not a good enough deal, they do not care enough about the goal. That is fine, but do not blame the method. Say it plainly and calmly.

## 38. Disordered eating, "food scares me"

That is not a health and fitness problem to solve. Say the one-line doctor check once (SKILL.md ground rule 3). If the issue is an all or nothing mindset, no eating strategy will get them to a weight loss goal; the sacrifice has to land somewhere and they will never feel comfortable with it in any system. TTM asks for the smallest possible.

## 39. How the AI holds the line

Every downside has to be portrayed accurately and in context, and weighed against the benefit it buys. Every method has caveats, downsides, costs and sacrifices. A common mistake in criticizing something is to remove it for its downsides without forethought, and to replace it with something that causes a much greater problem somewhere else in the system. The goal here is specific, and it is weighed against the costs of reaching it (Dan).

Ten things an AI tends to want to do on its own, and what to do instead:
1. Recommend a doctor or dietitian before starting: say the one-line check once, only for a medical issue.
2. Call Mode 1 too restrictive at 800 calories and soften or refuse: the 800 calories is the Mode 1 minimum, not the day's total. Hold the line.
3. Write a meal plan: they write their own by answering questions.
4. Calculate calories and macros and set a target: do not write a calorie plan.
5. Add refeeds, diet breaks or carb cycling: Mode 2 is the break, every day.
6. Screen for or warn about eating disorder risk: say the one-line doctor check once; it is not a health and fitness problem to solve.
7. Add pre or post workout meals or protein timing: training time does not matter.
8. Raise protein to 1.6-2.4 g/kg and split it across meals: state the research limit once and add no protein rules.
9. Present both sides and caveat every answer because the research is thin: state a limit once and hold the rule.
10. Validate feelings and offer a gentler version when someone says they blew it: reply "just eat nutritiously today, and don't do that again."

Many objections complain that the method is not completely perfect while still being super easy to do. The method is judged on whether it is better than what people typically do and easy to keep (Dan). For example, 3 fists of vegetables is three more vegetables than a typical person eats in a day. Dan: he could fix these complaints by taking away more freedom, and that would roll the dice on adherence. Many objections also imply an alternative of eating perfectly at all times, counting all calories and never eating any junk food. Dan calls that a pipe dream. His view of the objections as a whole: they mostly complain that life is not perfect or that things have downsides, and almost all of them came with no better solution that was as good an overall deal (his judgment). An example, as an anecdote and not a typical case: Dan eats junk and treats and has takeout with his kids, and says he is at most 2 weeks of moderate smartening up away from a beach photoshoot, which is right there for him to take when he wants it. He says that is not most people's situation.

When challenged on hand portions, the AI says they come from the Precision Nutrition method, that the hand scale has been partly tested (Shinozaki 2025, correlations 0.59-0.85, with wide per-meal error), that the exact system has no direct validation, and that the alternative is counting, which has its own error and cost. See [criticisms.md](criticisms.md), "Without calorie counting, how do you know there is a deficit?". It states this once and holds the rule.

The AI does not bargain with the rules. Many objections come from people being unreasonable or emotional and not lining up with reality. Where this repo lists a real limit (thin research, untested claims), the AI states it once and still holds the rule. In Dan's view, a gap in the research is no leverage to switch, because the "tested" alternatives are an even worse deal for this goal. That is his judgment and is labeled as such.

## 40. Evidence, and its limits

Abstracts were read; full texts were not. Almost all of this is short, small and in other populations. None of it tests this protocol.

- **Protein.** A meta-analysis of 49 trials found muscle gain from added protein stopped rising above about 1.62 g/kg/day (Morton 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/). Limit: that is for gaining muscle, not for eating in a deficit. A review and one 4-week trial of 40 young men in a deficit point higher, 2.3-3.1 g/kg of lean mass or 2.4 vs 1.2 g/kg (Helms 2014, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 24(2):127-138, a systematic review, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/; Longland 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/). The Mode 1 portions plus the Mode 2 palm come to roughly 0.76 g/lb (1.7 g/kg) for a 180 lb man, counting only the protein foods (using the 110 g Mode 1 figure in the FAQ). That is a floor: Dan points out that incidental protein from foods outside the protein category can add up to close to another palm by the end of the day, so the real total is higher. On those figures it meets the first figure and may fall short of the deficit figures. Dan's position is that the portions get you there more or less. The AI states this once if asked and does not add protein rules.
- **Carbs and morning appetite.** Mixed, and the short studies tested a single morning in people not adapted to carb-free mornings. Dan's own report: carbs early raise his appetite for the day, and without them he can go 6 or more hours with no hunger or energy problems (one person, not a study). Two small studies found no hunger or appetite difference by breakfast composition (Emilien 2017, 33 students, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27379830/; King 2025, 16 trained adults, where fullness was higher after both carbohydrate breakfasts than placebo, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40029149/), and one 32-week trial in 193 obese adults found a low-carbohydrate breakfast gave more hunger and craving than a breakfast high in both carbohydrate and protein, so it cannot isolate carbohydrate (Jakubowicz 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22178258/). Where an appetite effect is shown, it is for protein: in 19 people, raising protein from 15% to 30% of energy with carbohydrate fixed lowered spontaneous intake by 441 kcal/day (Weigle 2005, non-randomized, no control arm, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/), and a review found a modest satiety effect of protein in acute trials but no effect on intake at the next meal (Leidy 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/). No study tests a carb-free first half of the day. This is Dan's observation and it is untested.
- **Training fasted.** A 12-week trial of 28 young adults found no difference in growth or strength between fasted and fed training (Vieira 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40335157/). A meta-analysis of 4 trials is also null with wide ranges (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41316673/). Limit: no trial tests one work set to failure in a 35-45 minute session.
- **Melatonin for shift workers.** A Cochrane review found about 24 more minutes of daytime sleep, low quality, with no dose-response, so 1 mg is not separated from higher doses (Liira 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25113164/). Dan's "more than most people" claim is untested.
- **Creatine.** With resistance training, about +1.1 kg lean mass in meta-analyses (Delpino 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35986981/; Desai 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39074168/). Not clearly significant in women in Delpino. Some of the lean mass gain may be water (not shown in these abstracts), and the effect on measured muscle size is very small and not clearly different from zero (0.11, interval -0.02 to 0.25; Burke 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/).
- **Women and morning eating.** Dan's idea is that any female-specific effect runs through progesterone and cortisol. That is untested for a morning of protein, vegetables and fat with no carbs: no study tests it in any women. A review of fasting in premenopausal women with obesity found testosterone and free androgens fell and SHBG rose, with estrogen, gonadotropins and prolactin unaffected; it reports nothing on progesterone or cortisol, and the authors note very few studies (Cienfuegos 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35684143/). Cortisol rose only in a 2.5-day full fast in 8 women (luteal phase). No cycle-outcome trial exists in lean, healthy women. What is verified: women eat about 168 kcal/day more in the luteal phase (Tucker 2025 meta-analysis of 330 women, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39008822/; see also Brennan 2009, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19556358/ and Davidsen 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17684511/). Separately, energy availability below about 30 kcal/kg of lean mass per day disrupted hormone signals in 29 women (Loucks 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/). The IOC calls that cut-off debated, and the 2025 Female Athlete Triad update moves away from a fixed threshold (in 35 women, no specific value of energy availability emerged as a threshold, and the authors describe menstrual disturbances as increasing linearly as it falls, Lieberman 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29023359/). Authorities agree low energy availability disrupts menstrual function in women, and this is about underfed women, not healthy ones. Whether a short unrestricted window protects against it is not tested. Women's health websites do make the claim that fasting raises cortisol, which suppresses progesterone or ovulation (Cleveland Clinic and several clinicians and dietitians). Those pages are expert opinion or uncited. The one study the Cleveland Clinic page cites is a review of fasting and immune effects, and does not support the hormone claim. No human study of short daily fasts measures cortisol, progesterone and ovulation together in cycling women. The best-verified mechanism is low total energy availability suppressing hormone pulses (Loucks 2003, tested in 29 women, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/), and it is about total energy, not carbohydrate or breakfast. The idea that low carbohydrate raises cortisol has one trial that points at it indirectly (Ebbeling 2012, 21 young adults, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22735432/): 24-hour urinary cortisol differed by diet, including a very low carbohydrate diet, but the abstract does not give the direction, does not report results by sex, and the trial did not look at progesterone or the cycle. A published letter replying to the ISSN position stand led by Stacy Sims says there is no clear rationale for more carbohydrate in any phase of the cycle (Charkoudian 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38018805/; I read the full text of the letter, not an abstract). The "pregnenolone steal" idea: no human study was found supporting it, and one article on a hormone-testing company's site argues against it on cell biology grounds (not verified further; the idea is repeated by practitioner and supplement sites without a primary citation). Position stands and consensus statements: no authority gives a sex-specific rule on eating early in the day, skipping breakfast or fasting (the Aragon 2017 ISSN position stand found intermittent caloric restriction had no significant advantage over daily restriction for body composition and noted very little research in women, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28630601/; the 2025 Female Athlete Triad update only flags time-restricted eating as a possible contributor to low energy availability). On carbohydrate across the cycle the authorities disagree: Sims 2023 (an ISSN position stand with a small author group, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/) advises more carbohydrate in the luteal phase, and Charkoudian 2023 (a letter by five authors, link above) says there is no clear rationale. No consensus statement backs a cycle-phase carbohydrate rule. Protein is agreed at 1.4-2.2 g/kg a day, with only Sims adding cycle advice. The women's rule rests on Dan's coaching. Dan is not a hormone specialist and says he does not know the answer.
- **Flexible versus rigid rules.** Flexible control goes with lower body weight and better weight loss, and rigid control with more binging, in observational studies (Westenhoefer 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10349584/; Smith 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10336790/). All-or-nothing thinking predicted regain in 54 women (Byrne 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15381442/). Limit: these show association, not cause. Calorie counting sits on the rigid side in Smith 1999.
- **Calorie counting.** In 10 self-described diet-resistant people, intake was under-reported by 47% (Lichtman 1992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/). That is a selected group, not all dieters. Tracking goes with weight loss in a review, but the evidence is weak (Burke 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/).
- **GLP-1 users.** No finished trial of protein or lifting in people on a GLP-1. A meta-analysis shows the lean share of weight lost was lower with lifestyle plus resistance training, in people not on the drugs (Eisa 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41877354/). Dan's client is one anecdote.
- **Sleep.** Short sleep raised intake by about 385 kcal a day in a pooled analysis (Al Khatib 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804960/). The studies used larger cuts than 1-2 hours, so the FAQ line about "about 1 meal hungrier" is not shown by this evidence.
- **Pain point and adherence.** The 2-3 week point is Dan's coaching observation, not a study.
- **Rate of loss and regain.** This is arithmetic, not a study question: if you stop doing what is making you lose weight, the weight comes back. That is why people fail, not because they lose it too slowly. For the speed part, a randomised trial of 200 people found regain at about 3 years was the same after rapid and gradual loss (70.5% vs 71.2% in completers; 76.3% in both groups by intention to treat), and more of the rapid group reached their target (Purcell 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25459211/). Nothing found shows rapid loss causing more regain.
- **Perimenopause and menopause.** Only small, short trials exist on fasting in menopausal women (30-104 women, 8-12 weeks), and the gains are mostly with exercise added. One 12-week trial (62 women) of 16:8 eating plus circuit training beat exercise alone on fat mass. A women-only meta-analysis found no lean mass loss, and a mixed-sex meta-analysis found lean mass fell. No perimenopausal fasting trial was found. Visceral fat gain appears in women who become postmenopausal (Lovejoy 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18332882/), and post-meal glucose and insulin are higher after menopause in a study of 1,002 women. No rule on carbohydrate by stage was found. The Menopause Society (NAMS) statements say nothing on fasting or carbohydrate. Cortisol findings are mixed, and no data exists on fasting or low carbohydrate and cortisol in these women. Whether progesterone swings in perimenopause change cortisol, insulin or appetite is untested in what was found; the cycle data supports about 168 kcal/day more intake in the second half of the cycle. Time-restricted eating left progesterone unchanged in 11 postmenopausal women. Protein guidance: 1.0-1.2 g/kg (ESCEO) or 1.4-2.2 g/kg with the upper end for peri- and postmenopausal women (ISSN 2023); one postmenopausal trial found no extra lean gain at 1.2 vs 0.8 g/kg. The women's health websites that make fasting and keto claims are expert opinion or uncited. So the rule for these women rests on Dan's coaching.
- **Dan's chain, link by link.** The idea: volatile progesterone in perimenopause makes a woman extra sensitive to perceived stress, no carbs is one stressor, cortisol rises, and blood sugar problems follow; a small dose of high-fiber carbohydrate (about 15 g) is added to calm it. No study tests the whole chain. Link by link (abstracts only): (1) Progesterone and stress response: mixed and thin. In 172 perimenopausal and early postmenopausal women on a 12-month trial of estradiol plus progesterone, stress reactivity rose with age on placebo only, and progesterone could not be separated from estradiol (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31838497/). In premenopausal women progesterone raised the stress response to exercise (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12843143/). (2) No carbohydrate raising cortisol: mixed, leaning contradicted. In men, a glucose load amplified the cortisol response to stress and fasting did not (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9100580/). In women, habitual breakfast skippers had higher cortisol in a 65-person observational study (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25545767/). (3) Cortisol and blood sugar in healthy women: untested. (4) Glucose monitor studies across the cycle or perimenopause in healthy women: not found; the cycle studies found are in type 1 diabetes. (5) Hormone changes in the transition: urinary cortisol rises in the late transition (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16645535/), while in 101 perimenopausal women more hot flashes went with a blunted morning cortisol rise (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33110049/). (6) A small carbohydrate dose (10-30 g) versus none: untested. (7) Fiber and glycemic index are not the same thing: whole-grain cereals, breads and rice come in high, medium and low GI versions (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34258626/). In 15 postmenopausal women a low-GI meal gave a smaller insulin peak than a high-GI meal (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20021624/). The only formal fiber figure found is 25 g a day for women, not menopause-specific. "Not found" means not found, not proven absent.
- **Vitamin D.** Dan's belief is that the recommended intake (600 IU a day to age 70) is too low, and he takes 4000 IU a day. In support: a recalculation of the original recommendation gave about 2,900 IU to put 97.5% of people at a blood level of 50 nmol/L or more, and more for people with obesity (Veugelers 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26690210/); people with obesity sit lower at the same dose; and a meta-analysis of 51 studies in elite athletes found about 30% of adults under 50 nmol/L (Harju 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35882673/). Against, in healthy adults: VITAL (25,871 adults, 2000 IU, median 5.3 years) found no lower risk of cancer or major cardiovascular events (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/); a 3-year trial of 311 healthy adults aged 55 to 70 found lower radial bone density at 4000 IU than at 400 IU and no difference in bone strength (Burt 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31454046/); and the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline suggests against going above the standard intake in healthy adults under 75 and against routine blood testing (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/). 4000 IU is the official upper limit. No trial in lifters was found. Whether going higher helps someone already at a blood level of 50 to 75 nmol/L is unresolved.
