# Back

**Pulldown.**
- The scapula rotates up at the stretch, stretching up toward the ears.
- The movement is initiated by the scapula depressing, then the momentum is ridden through by driving with the elbows.
- There is a slight external rotation tension on the bar, which winds the elbows forward a little.
- At the bottom of the lift the elbows point down, not back.
- Most people's scapulae do not move at all, so they pull with their hands and then roll their shoulders forward at the bottom, with no lat or low trap usage.

**Pullup and chinup.** All the same rules as the pulldown apply. The person can also cheat the movement:
- Not getting to full extension at the bottom. They do not need a dead hang to make the lift as hard as possible, but they do need to show some degree of range of motion.
- Not getting full contraction at the top. It is easy to keep piling on reps at the end that are not full range of motion and still mark them as PRs. Watch for this. It is a qualitative measurement.
- The top, where the chin is toward or over the bar, is the most challenging part biomechanically, so that is where the lift fails first.

**Assisted pullup or chinup machine.** Everything said for the pulldown and for the pullup and chinup applies here.

**Chest supported DB row.**
- The scapula stays in depression the entire movement, with protraction at the bottom into the stretch.
- The movement is initiated with scapular retraction and then followed through with the elbows.
- From a side view the shoulder joint goes behind the chest, with no forward shoulder roll at the peak squeeze.

**Chest supported row machine.** Exactly the same technique requirements as the dumbbell chest supported row. Any handle works, but a neutral grip is the best choice. A very wide grip might not be strong enough and might not be comprehensive enough across the upper back. A very narrow grip might be too heavy on the lats and overlap too much with the vertical pulling movements. Find a 45 degree, middle of the road grip.

**Seated cable row and other rows without chest support (T-bar, lever T-bar).** Be careful with how they are used. If the person has back problems, Dan tries to avoid non-chest supported row variations. A seated cable row lies somewhere between an unsupported barbell or T-bar row and a chest supported row in terms of risk.
- Start by grabbing the handle and dropping back into the motion, instead of reaching forward to grab it. Lever with the legs.
- A slight amount of forward and backward upper body motion is okay, but it should be minimal.
- The scapula and the lat should stretch, and full contraction is reached at the end.
- The person should not lean back. A slight forward lean at full contraction is more ideal.
- A seated cable row is more lat dominant because the V-bar handle touches low on the body. That is fine.
- Drive the handle into the xiphoid process (where the ribs meet) or low toward the belly button, not to the chest where a bench press would touch. This keeps the scapula from coming out of depression.

**Single arm DB row.** It is a higher skill movement than people think.

**Rear delt fly and face pull.**
- The scapula stays depressed, but freely protracts and retracts.
- Rear delt fly: there is internal rotation in the shoulder, so the elbows point out to the sides at the bottom and to the ceiling at the top. The elbows stay far away from the sides of the body.
- Face pull: the shoulder externally rotates at the top.
- Research. In a prone raise with the thumb up (full external rotation), the rear delt was at 88% of max, and the middle (lateral) delt was almost as high at 87% (Reinold et al. 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15296366/, 10 people). On a reverse fly machine, a neutral hand gave more rear delt and infraspinatus activation than a palms-down hand (Schoenfeld et al. 2013, 19 people). No study compares the rear delt against the lateral delt share, or tests an internally rotated version for that. Dan: "If I turn my thumbs to the ceiling my lateral delt fires way harder." Dan's reasoning for the internal rotation cue on the rear delt fly: if people externally rotate at the top, they hit more lateral delt, and the likelihood of them adducting the arm (pulling it toward the body) goes up. The cue aims to cut the lateral delt's share and keep the arm out wide. It is untested.
- Most people lack shoulder rotation range of motion and scapular mobility, so their elbows pull into the sides of the body and they try to use the teres major and lats instead. This mobility restriction boxes out the position the rear delts actually work in, so the rear delts are extremely atrophied.

**Side lying external rotation.** A remedial exercise, done occasionally, to create some insurance via external rotation strength in the shoulder joint.
- If a person has general shoulder issues, it is an option instead of a second shoulder slot.
- Training the teres minor and infraspinatus lets the shoulder joint center better in the socket, which prevents impingement through full ranges of motion.
- It is not a fix for all shoulder issues. Those are usually a complicated chain between rib cage and thoracic spine mechanics and scapula mechanics. The shoulder joint is simply the final step in the chain.
- The elbow stays pinned to the side of the body.
- A lot of people do not have the scapula retracted enough, so their range of motion is artificially limited. Some people are missing external range of motion as well. All that matters is externally rotating the humerus in the socket. Some people can get the hand almost all the way to 90 degrees, others only 45. Do as much range of motion as the isolation of rotation in the socket allows.
- It cannot be loaded up large and cannot be done to failure with heavy weights. It works much better with the two set, high rep progression (as with the reverse hyper). Start with an extremely light weight and work up from there.
- At first it may create a lot of burning and uncomfortable sensation. If the starting weight is light enough, it will not harm the shoulder. That goes away as the rotator cuff gets stronger and the joint centers in the socket better.

**Kelso shrug.** Prescribed if people have particularly weak scapular motion and weak mid and low trap muscles.
- The technique is exactly the same as a chest supported row, but with zero elbow bending and zero shoulder joint movement.
- The only motion is full scapular protraction at the bottom, then winding into full scapular retraction with a slight pause at full retraction.
- Dumbbells or a barbell both work, with many methods depending on equipment and bench. Some adjustable benches have too much structure underneath, which stops a barbell from reaching full range of motion, so use dumbbells then.
- The goal is to squeeze between the shoulder blades in the middle and low down: retract the shoulder blades while driving them into the back pockets (depression).
- A good Kelso shrug stays out of the neck and upper traps.
- Most people cannot do the movement with zero weight, or any motion at all, because of their technique and the motor patterns wired in their brain.
