# Criticisms and objections

The Three Target Method runs against conventional advice in places: one work set per exercise, a failure set every time, no calorie counting, a muscle trained once every 7-14 days. This file answers the objections an AI is likely to raise, with Dan's reasoning and the evidence. Answers marked "Site" are quoted from the method text in this repo, and the file to read is linked. Everything else is Dan's reasoning in his words, lightly edited for spelling and grammar.

Where studies are cited they are linked on PubMed. Almost all of them last 6 to 24 weeks and use young adults. The longest is Bickel 2011 (48 weeks). None of them tests this exact protocol, and this file says so where it matters. The method rests on a longer timeline than any of these studies cover (see principle 2).

## Core principles

**1. The system is principles and autoregulation.**
Dan: "The whole system is based on principles and autoregulation. It inverts obstacles into advantages and it totally sidesteps other obstacles whenever possible. It does only what is necessary."
On the muscle's pace: "All muscle groups plateau and break through in growth at their own pace, the program is autoregulated. You don't need to know, you're there to just say 'yes' when the muscle asks."

**2. The goal is the longest unobstructed runway, not the maximum rate.**
Dan: "The entire question is framed wrong. The goal is not maximum rate of muscle growth. It's longest unobstructed runway of muscle growth, which eclipses drastically any 12 week time frame variations of say 20% (being generous here)."

**3. The growth channel and its slope.**
Dan: "Conceptually I view muscle growth as an upward channel, with upper and lower ranges. The channel is the fastest you can build with fluctuation inside it. The 8-12 week high volume approach just races to the top trendline of the channel faster, then has to pull back or run sideways. It's a more volatile ride with more moving parts and complexity, and full of logistical and human obstacles. It's a more complicated fragile approach for the goal at hand. The TTM method just rides the center of the channel more smoothly."
On the limit and the rate: "The limit doesn't need to be the same. The slope is the rate of PRs, or adaptation to increased structural demands. Your biology and lifestyle determine that."

**4. Floor over ceiling.**
Dan: "Progress for people's physiques has MUCH more to do with the height of their floor than the reach of their ceiling. All of your effort and discipline and sacrifice should go into raising the floor and making it more durable. Where and when you want to reach the ceiling, go ahead."

Dan: "Metaphorically, you're just playing moneyball with behavior statistics. Every day you have to get on third base, and then you choose what to do for the next batter up. Just bunt it home. But if you approach it like every time you step on the plate you need a home run, you're gonna lose the game."

**5. The discipline bank.**
Dan: "The whole idea is to maximize people's autonomy." Rules that people already know (for example that alcohol does not help their goals) "just needlessly take a withdrawal from your discipline bank psychologically." So the method writes down only what moves the floor.

**6. People backtrack because they didn't do it, mostly.**
Dan: "People rarely lose progress or backtrack because their plan isn't strict enough. It's because they didn't do it." This is mostly about the doing, not the plan. The plan still matters at the edges: in a 12-month behavioural programme, hunger and deprivation predicted lapses (Forman 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28281136/). That is why the method keeps hunger and sacrifice low.

**7. Readiness: two groups.**
Dan: "How objectively difficult something is has zero bearing on a proportion of someone's adherence. They're simply just not ready or willing at the time." For people who are not ready, difficulty does not matter and nothing the system does will make them do it. For people who are ready, lower sacrifice is what lets them keep going for years. The system's goals are to "minimize overhead and sacrifice and maximize results", which is not the same as making someone feel good about exercise.

**8. Eating rules are for not noticing a deficit.**
Dan: "Most of the rules around eating are just so you mentally and biologically don't notice a deficit as much."

**9. The question is the whole journey, and how many obstacles are on the road.**
Dan: "The question is: what will get me through the whole journey with the least sacrifice possible?" The studies mostly describe the first few months of adaptation, which is about 3 to 5% of a training life by arithmetic. For the rest, Dan's argument is that a high-volume approach is likely to get stuck and lose progress time more often, "for a million reasons": you need deloads if you keep adding volume, you need other phases, and you get dinged by the sheer hours under the bar. Obstacles come one after another. TTM is "a slow train that can't be stopped."
Evidence: this is Dan's argument and it is untested over years. The pieces that are measured are the time (about 64 to 83 hours a year against 255 to 758) and the injury exposure at a given hourly rate (see the entry on the research in context). In one 12 week trial of trained men, the group doing 10 sets per exercise lost lean mass between week 6 and week 12 (Hackett 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184/), one small data point. No trial is long enough to count stalls or deloads.

## Training

### Isn't one work set per exercise (about 2 hard sets per muscle per workout) far below the 10-20 weekly sets research favours?
Dan: "The question we are answering is not what the most muscle growth you can get is. It's what the least sacrifice is that still gets muscle growth, all the way up to an advanced level." And that undersells it: "Because it is unencumbered by plateaus and runs indefinitely, your absolute muscle growth is likely higher."

Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md)): "The benefit you might get from that optimization costs you double or triple the time spent in the gym." Also "Why only one PR set?": "Will this eventually plateau you? Possibly, but it'll happen well past the point where you're in the best shape of your life."

Dan: "I'm not convinced that more volume is better beyond a shorter time frame that doesn't encompass the required timespan to achieve maximum natural muscle mass." See principles 2 and 3.

Dan: "You will also sacrifice much more, so if you are measuring results per unit of sacrifice, you will likely still underscore compared to the TTM system."

High volume costs double or triple the time. To match TTM on results per unit of sacrifice, it would have to deliver double or triple the results, and no study shows that over the long term.

Dan on the count: "It's 2 direct working hard sets + don't forget all of the other movements that hit the muscle group on the side. E.g., triceps getting hit on pressing or biceps on pulling." and "There is plenty of overlap on non-targeted exercises to bridge most of that gap."

Evidence, stated plainly: the dose-response research says more weekly sets give more growth, with diminishing returns (Schoenfeld 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/; Krieger 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300012/; Pelland 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). In trained men, 1 set per exercise gave less size than 3 or 5 sets over 8 weeks, with equal strength (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/). Counting indirect sets at half a set, as Pelland 2026 does, the sample plans in the protocol work out to roughly 1 to 4.5 weekly sets per muscle depending on the plan and how often a letter repeats. That is below the 4-set weekly minimum suggested by Iversen 2021 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/) for most muscles. So overlap narrows the gap but does not close it on paper. The method's position is that the studies span weeks and the goal spans years.

### What does the volume research actually say once you look at the studies in context?
Dan: "my argument is that the very idea of focusing on short term results is the key fragility of the entire science side of things." And: "How fast we go from zero to cruise speed has no bearing on long term muscle growth. It's like saying high volume burns up all the gas in your tank faster. But does your car end up further at the end of the road trip?" Dan frames the research as measuring only the benefit side. It does not price the time, the nutrition demands, or how long progress lasts.

What the studies show, in numbers:
- Higher weekly volume grew muscle more, with diminishing returns. Schoenfeld 2017 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/) found about +0.37% muscle size per extra weekly set, and about 3.9 percentage points more gain for higher versus lower volume (a trend, P=0.074). In an 8 week trial of 34 trained men doing 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise (6-9, 18-27 and 30-45 weekly sets), mid-thigh thickness rose 3.4% versus 12.5% for 1 versus 5 sets (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/).
- TTM sits below every studied arm. Its roughly 1.3-2 weekly sets per muscle, with each muscle trained less than once a week, falls under the lowest arm of any trial found. The closest arms were untrained people and lasted 12 weeks or less. Nothing at TTM's dose has been tested in trained people over years, so the research cannot say what happens there.
- The "low volume" in the studies is not TTM's volume. The lowest arm in the 8 week trial was 6-9 weekly sets, several times TTM's.
- Failure is not the same set to set. In the trial above, failure was instructed and not verified. Across the pooled studies only about 30% clearly defined momentary failure (Pelland 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). Dan: "failure at the 14th set of the workout is nowhere near what the muscle can actually do fresh." Reps fell 15-55% across 5 sets with short rest (Ratamess 2007), and no study tested 10 or more consecutive failure sets. Late failure sets probably carry less stimulus, but no study gives a number.
- Some of the early gain is not tissue. Early gains in people new to a stimulus include swelling (Damas 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26280652/). A review of the confound estimated roughly 10-20% of the volume advantage could be non-tissue (swelling, glycogen, water), with low-to-moderate confidence. That is an estimate from arithmetic, not a published figure. In trained men swelling was gone within 24 hours.
- Short trials cannot show the long run. No volume trial found runs 12 months or longer. The only 6 month trial used untrained people.

What it costs, in numbers (modeled, not measured):
- Time. TTM takes about 1.2-1.6 hours a week. Conventional training at 10, 15, 20 and 30 sets per muscle takes about 4.9, 7.3, 9.7 and 14.6 hours a week, which is 3 to 12 times TTM.
- Nutrition. The benefit of carbohydrate for performance was small in sessions under 45 minutes (SMD 0.23, not significant) and larger over 45 minutes (SMD 1.02) (King 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9584980/). Dan: all high volume sessions run over 45 minutes by a mile. TTM sessions run 35-45 minutes, and Mode 1 has no carbohydrate requirement. A caloric deficit of about 500 kcal a day or more blunts lean mass gain.
- Injury exposure. Bodybuilding has the lowest injury rate of the weight sports, 0.24-1 per 1000 hours (Keogh and Winwood 2017, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0). TTM is bodybuilding, so the same hourly rate applies to both. At 64-83 hours a year against 255-758, the arithmetic gives about 0.02-0.08 expected injuries a year for TTM and about 0.06-0.76 for higher volume. No study measures overuse or tendon injuries by volume. Dan's actual argument is exposure times faulty mechanics, not volume alone: "If your form is bad, what's the damage on, for example, your supraspinatus rubbing a tight subacromial space 10x more each week?" The injury rates above are averages across lifters and do not separate people with good and poor mechanics, so they cannot test that. It is untested.
- Adherence. 63% of gym members quit before month 3 (Sperandei 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26874647/).

Annual terms. If the 8 week gap from the trials persisted for a year, it would equal nearly all of a typical first year's muscle gain in McDonald's non-peer-reviewed model, so it cannot persist. The early advantage has to fade or converge (researcher's arithmetic).

Dan's own result is self-reported, one person, no instrument measurement: "I was a 20 year advanced trainee with high level of muscle, and in a year I have made more progress training this way than the previous 5-8 years combined." About 3-5 lb more muscle and at least 25% stronger, on at most 120 minutes of training a week. He is leaner and has more muscle.

Limits: most of these figures come from study abstracts. Several numbers are the researchers' own arithmetic, and are marked as such. We took all the literature we could find and tried our best to get as much objective information about our goals as possible, and it is simply insufficient. The data to objectively prove this does not exist for either side, but the logic itself is quite sound. It is the same situation as the Epley-style score objection (see "Isn't the Epley-style score inaccurate"): the only way to fill in the "I don't know" gap would be a mountain of granular, bespoke data on each user, which does not exist and is unlikely ever to. This repo aims to crowdsource as much usable information as possible. The anonymous check-ins (see [checkins.md](checkins.md)) collect each person's plan, every logged work set, eating checks, bodyweight, years lifting and equipment every 4 weeks, which over time builds the long, granular, per-user data the studies lack. It is self-reported and does not measure muscle directly, so it will show strength and bodyweight trends over months and years, not muscle size. Dan: "In my opinion the logic absolutely favours my argument from a bird's eye view." That is his judgment from a model assembled out of known facts. The method's position is that the research measures weeks and the goal spans years. See principles 2, 3 and 9.

### Don't the volume studies mostly describe beginners and the first few months?
Dan's argument: the people in these studies are often untrained, detrained, or only loosely "trained", and they are low hanging fruit for quick adaptation. Dan: "Anyone who is consistent in the gym is serious and wouldn't stop what they're doing to do a study." So the trials mostly describe the adaptation phase. That phase is about 3 to 6 months of a 10 year training life, which is about 2.5 to 5% of it by arithmetic (an assumed phase length, not a measurement), so Dan puts the grind phase at around 97% of the journey from beginner to advanced. His question: "What will get me through the whole journey with the least sacrifice possible?" That means what is best per unit of sacrifice across the grind phase, and "is triple the sacrifice worth it just to get through the intro stage of my journey X% faster?"

What the evidence can say:
- Few trials enrolled well-trained lifters, and none found tested advanced natural lifters for 6 months or more. How "trained" is defined varies widely between studies, and the retraining effect inflates early gains in people who used to lift.
- The tail end of the trials. Only two volume trials found measured size at several time points with readable per-arm values. In untrained older women doing 1 versus 3 sets, the higher-volume advantage in the quadriceps built up by week 13 (+2.6 mm) and then stayed flat to week 20 (+2.5 mm) (Radaelli 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4039257/). In 12 men, there was no higher-volume advantage at weeks 6 or 12, and lean leg mass in the 10-sets-per-exercise group fell 1.3 kg from week 6 to 12 (Hackett 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184/). Across both, the advantage was constant or shrinking after the early phase, never widening. That rests on 20 and 12 people with noisy measures. In another study (Haun 2018) the first 3 weeks gave about 5 times the water-adjusted lean mass gain of the next 3 weeks. A strength-athlete study (abstract only) found 50% more volume for weeks 11-17 gave no extra growth.
- In the model, if the early advantage of higher volume lasted all year, the 10-30 set programs would produce about 2.6 to 3.6 times TTM's gain for about 3 to 12 times the hours. If the advantage fades once the stimulus is familiar, TTM wins per hour by a wide margin. This is modeled, not measured. See the entry on the research in context.
- The research does not test the grind phase. TTM's case for it rests on the mechanism argument and on the client record, which is self-reported.

### Wouldn't more volume plus deloads beat steady progress?
Dan's hypothesis, untested: once past the beginner stage, the job is to be there to say yes when the body asks to build muscle, and the limit is likely connective tissue remodelling, which takes time. High volume races up to that wall, then needs a deload or easier phase, waits, and hits it again, with more sacrifice and more mess. Dan: "It's like a traffic jam. If all the cars just moved the same speed, no stop/start. But people race up to close the gap, then hit the brakes. That's what I think is happening with the high volume stuff. The studies are all just going 'see, if you stomp the gas you go faster', but none of them are long enough to see where the brakes need to be hit." And: "Every single high volume study is like, there you go! And you ask, okay then what, and they have no answer."

What the research says:
- Tendon adapts more slowly than muscle. One study found no change in muscle size or tendon stiffness for about 2 months despite a roughly 30% strength gain (Kubo 2010). Another found collagen and muscle protein synthesis timing similar (Miller 2005), which cuts against "muscle outgrows tendon". So the connective tissue limit is plausible and not established.
- No volume trial found runs 12 months or longer, so none shows where a high-volume lifter must back off. The maximum recoverable volume used in coaching is a coaching idea, not a measured quantity. The research has no answer to "then what" once volume is maxed out.
- Rep progression at a fixed load grew muscle as much as load progression over 8 weeks in trained people (Plotkin 2022, https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14142). That is short, at 8-12 reps, and it does not test years of PR-driven progress.

### Why take every work set to failure, every workout?
Dan: "There is plenty of science showing minimum intensity thresholds for muscle growth signal." Failure "will eventually be required to put your body in a novel environment you need to adapt to. It's simply a side effect, not the reason."

It also depends on training state: "Plenty of subjects will experience muscle growth for many sessions without hitting true failure, depending on their initial condition. All that is required is a new level of environmental demand. For those who are untrained, failure isn't required. For those who are advanced, the only edge remaining lies in failure. The system doesn't discriminate."

Evidence: meta-analyses find no clear advantage of true failure over stopping short (Refalo 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/; Grgic 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/). In the resistance-trained subgroup of Grgic 2022, hypertrophy favoured failure (effect size 0.15). Hypertrophy improves gradually as sets end closer to failure (Robinson 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/; the authors call it exploratory). With light loads, going to failure grew muscle where the same load stopped short did not (Lasevicius 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/), which fits the 8-30 rep range. Failure is not shown to be required in general, and it costs more fatigue (Refalo 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/).

### Can I train to failure safely on my own (bench, skullcrushers, leg press)?
Dan: "Exercise selection has its own guide and that's covered. Movements you cannot logistically or technically take to failure are not appropriate for the program. That's why back squats and barbell deadlifts are out."

On barbell bench: "That's up to the discretion of the lifter. If they are experienced they can attempt it because there is lower risk of injury vs spine loaded heavy compounds like squat and deadlift. Users can keep the collars off the bar and dump the plates on either side in the case of tail risk. They can use machine, smith, dumbbell, floor press and those would be bench press variations that don't have that risk."

### Why is each muscle trained only once every 7-14 days?
The 14-day limit is the floor. Dan: "There's multiple studies that show minimum stimulus frequency before deconditioning begins."

Evidence: in young adults, one-ninth of the original training dose kept muscle size for 32 weeks (Bickel 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/). A review concludes strength and size, at least in younger people, can be kept for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session a week and 1 set per exercise if relative load is maintained (Spiering 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/). Another review finds intensity matters most and volume can be cut a lot (Mujika and Padilla 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999420/). When volume is equated, frequency does not significantly change hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/).

Limits: these are maintenance studies, not growth studies, and they were done mostly on younger adults. Older adults needed more (Bickel 2011; Spiering 2021). When volume is not equated, higher frequency is favoured. No study tests growth on a once-per-7-to-14-days schedule.

### Why no sooner than 7 days?
Dan: "Less PR frequency." Repeat a workout letter sooner and the body has not finished adapting, so PRs come less often. "Beginners can fast track it, but they're just eating their runway up faster. There's no long-term benefit to it."

### Why no deloads or planned breaks?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "When to take a break"): life provides breaks, and "If you are feeling extremely beat up, you can instead perform a 'quality set' that's 75% of your best performance weight for the same reps."

Limit: the one planned-break trial found (Ogasawara 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/, 14 young men, 24 weeks, much higher volume) found similar gains with planned breaks. It supports taking time off, not training less often.

### Why no barbell squat, deadlift or barbell row?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why no squats or barbell deadlifts?"): "Do you want to be a strong squatter or do you want a kickass physique in minimal weekly time without getting hurt? Choose wisely." Dan adds that movements you cannot logistically or technically take to failure are not appropriate for the program.

### Isn't the Epley-style score inaccurate, and where does +1-4% come from?
Site ([protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md), note on the formula): it is not as accurate above 10 reps, "But it's the most straightforward, practical way to objectively measure a muscle improving". On pace, [faq/training.md](faq/training.md), Check 1: "One PR per muscle group per month is still a good pace." The +1-4% figure is a rule of thumb. The site does not cite a source for it.

### Doesn't the score distort PRs at high reps?
Dan: "I don't see any problems here with both of those answers being true." Both answers stand:
1. The rules already cap it. Big leg movements add weight above 15 reps, and small muscles add reps up to 25+, so very high-rep PRs only happen on small muscles.
2. A new demand is a new demand and the body adapts either way. Over a long runway extra reps turn into extra weight, and that takes muscle.

### Why a high rep range (8-30)?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why is the work set rep range so high?"; [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md)): reps on big compound leg movements become too taxing to perform accurately above 15 reps, so add weight instead.
Evidence: hypertrophy is similar from low to high loads when sets go to failure (Schoenfeld 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/; Morton 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/). Heavier loads gave larger strength gains in those studies.

### Never changing the plan when stalled looks dogmatic. What if all six checks pass?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Making Changes: Don't."): go through the six checks in order before changing anything.

Dan: "Completely depends on their situation." He adds: "I guarantee you the percentage of people who have done everything exactly right and aren't seeing ANY progress in 3 months straight are the people who are pretty jacked and close to their natural limit." and "Most people won't be patient enough to even get close to that situation." If they are not advanced, "there's obviously a huge glaring blind spot in what they're doing", and if they are advanced "they have to decide if they want to bulk etc." For the long-term answer see "What happens once I'm lean?" under Whole system. Anything past that is a coach's call.

### Won't the same movements for years go stale?
Dan: "You're going to have to define what going stale means and what you mean by overuse injuries." On staleness: "Being fully adapted to a movement is the ideal environment to use it to build muscle. That means muscle growth is mandatory." In other words, once skill and coordination on a movement are maxed out, the only way left to beat the old number is more muscle, so a PR on a mastered movement is a clean growth signal. The method keeps 2 movements per muscle for coverage of the muscle.

Dan on his own removals: "Now, once in a blue moon I had to take a movement out. Single arm pulldowns, sissy squats sometimes. But those were all because there was too much overlap and it was more like 3 hard sets or more. Barbell lunge, sissy squat, but also the roman chair situps were hitting rectus femoris hard. The single arm pulldowns was just too many back sets combined with two hand pulldowns, chest supported rows, single arm DB rows. Also the movement was cheatable. Not a moneymaker." And: "Yeah, but it was overlap AND the movement itself just not being biomechanically good for my body." That fits the existing rule: movements are chosen by trial and error for your moneymakers, with no joint pain, and a movement that is bad for your body or cheatable gets swapped ([protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md), [faq/training.md](faq/training.md)). In years, he removed a movement rarely, and each time overlap had pushed a muscle to 3 or more hard sets.

Evidence, stated plainly: no study follows the same movements for years. Over 9 to 12 weeks, groups that kept fixed exercises gained about the same whole-muscle size and 1RM as groups that varied them (Fonseca 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24832974/; Baz-Valle 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881066/, 21 trained men; Costa 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35481889/). Heavy random rotation did not beat fixed exercises for growth (Baz-Valle 2019), and a systematic review of 8 studies in young men concludes that excessive, random variation may compromise gains while some systematic variation may help regional growth (Kassiano 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/). Excessive variation is therefore the risk, and the method has none. The case against a single movement per muscle: in 9 to 12 weeks the fixed groups missed some quadriceps heads (Fonseca 2014) and some sites along the thigh and arm (Costa 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33440446/). That supports more than one movement per muscle for coverage. No study compared 1, 2 and 3 movements, so "2 movements per muscle" is supported in direction but not tested as a number. These are small studies in young men, 8 to 12 weeks.

### Doesn't the same movements for years cause overuse injuries?
Dan: "There's no reason to get an overuse injury on the right muscle group. Sometimes the connective tissue will feel beat up at more advanced levels. That's your limiting factor. You'll be doing more quality sets and your performance will stagnate until it's remodelled and ready to handle more stress. It might take weeks. That's how your body works. That's not the same as an overuse injury. How would you get overuse doing the biomechanically correct movement for your body to technical only failure, like two times in 10 days?"

Dan on form: "Most people lift badly and have terrible form. For them, there are zero training plans that will give them results beyond a hard ceiling because of that. Imagine them doing 20 work sets hard, you think that's not going to give them overuse issues?" That is why the method is for people who already know how to perform the lifts generally correctly (see "Beginners" under Who it's for). Poor form caps results on any plan, and a high-volume plan multiplies the load on the same bad pattern. This plan has about 2 failure sets per muscle every 7 to 14 days.

Dan: "Many people cannot tolerate taking what the body has to give. They expect a PR at all times for emotional reasons and will violate the program to do so. That's not my problem. It's not the program."

Dan: "If they get overuse, it is the wrong movement, the wrong form, and cheating on executing. Every time."

Evidence, stated plainly: tendon and connective tissue adapt more slowly than muscle. In one small study, tendon stiffness and muscle size did not change until after 2 months of training, while strength rose sooner (Kubo 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/, 8 people). A meta-analysis of 27 studies finds tendons adapt to loading over weeks to months, mostly in response to high loads (Bohm 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/). Adult Achilles tendon core is hardly renewed at all after growth (Heinemeier 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23401563/). A physiology review says chronic loading probably makes tendon more load resistant, that tendon overuse injury involves altered collagen and poor collagen breakdown, and that the answer is adjusted loading, not no loading (Kjaer 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15044685/). So "connective tissue lags muscle and needs weeks to months" is supported. Limits: no study times how long tendon takes to recover from a hard run of training, none ties tendon lag to a PR plateau in lifters, and none tests a lighter quality set while it catches up. "Never an overuse injury" cannot be shown from this evidence; the doctor line in "Injuries, conditions, medication, pregnancy, under 18" still applies to pain, and the no joint pain rule stays.

### Is a PR really the growth signal ("tap, tap, BANG")?
Dan: failure and PRs are about adaptation to a new demand. "the slope is the rate of PRs, or adaptation to increased structural demands." Every PR is a new level of environmental demand the body has not met before (see principle 3 and the failure answer).
Limit: no study tests beating the previous number as the driver of growth. The dose-response research points to weekly hard sets. The method treats the PR as the measurable form of increased demand.

### Aren't sissy squats and ATG split squats risky for the knees?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why the ATG split squats and sissy squats?"; the "no joint pain" rule in [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md)). Individual knee history falls under [disclaimer.md](disclaimer.md) and the one-line doctor check in "Injuries, conditions, medication" below.

### Doesn't a lifting-only plan skip cardio?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why no cardio?"): "Cardio for health is great, add it if you like. But not instead of lifting."
Dan: no structured cardio is required. "You're much better off just adding weekly steps than cardio sessions. The protocol is light enough on recovery that you can add cardio if you want to, or HIIT or whatever, but that will aid fitness primarily."

## Eating

Weigh each downside against what it buys. Every method has costs, and many criticisms swap one problem for a bigger one elsewhere (Dan).

Dan's principle for all of it: "Most of the rules around eating are just so you mentally and biologically don't notice a deficit as much."

Dan's explanation: "You need a caloric deficit. But it can't be aggressively chronic, and it can't be malnutrition. So why not let the normal ebb and flow of life circumstances regulate the deficit/refeeds, and then just make the mandatory foods handle the malnutrition."

"Making the best choice when you are not backed into a corner with restriction AND already jam packed with satiating important foods has a significantly higher chance of happening. Most people don't need to eat perfect, just bring their floor up."

### Without calorie counting, how do you know there is a deficit?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md), "I want to count calories" and "Precision Nutrition Measurements"): "Calories and macros are the ultimate decider of weight. But counting them daily doesn't work in practice." Hand measurements are "sufficiently accurate at a fraction of the time and focus required."
If fat loss stalls, Dan's answer is: "add steps."
Dan also: "how accurate do they need to be for long term results. what is the added benefit of measuring vs the adherence risk."

Dan: the issue is that there is really no alternative other than counting calories and measuring food, and that has its own costs (below).

Dan: "The idea is you have to strike a balance between literally weighing and measuring every calorie and counting it, which in itself can be quite accurate, versus not caring about anything at all. I believe that the hand portion measurement is the best work to accuracy ratio." That is his judgment. No study compares accuracy per unit of effort across methods. The hand portions come from the Precision Nutrition method, which Dan says has a considerable amount of case studies. What is published: three small papers on Precision Nutrition Coaching with company-affiliated authors, none of which measures portion accuracy: a single-arm pilot in 46 cancer survivors (49% retained at 12 months, 5.3% weight loss; Lynch 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28392934/), a randomized trial of 35 women after premature surgical menopause (fitness and bone endpoints; Sturgeon 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27873046/), and a single-arm pilot in women (28 completers, 50% of those who started, 8.6% weight loss at 12 months; Heroux 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30135822/). The company's own case studies are client reports. They can show that retained clients lose weight. They cannot show how accurate the portions are or how the method compares with counting.

The downside, accurately: the evidence on the exact palm, fist and thumb system is thin. The closest test is Shinozaki 2025, a cross-sectional study of 1,081 Japanese adults over 12,148 meals with weighed records, using a hand scale of fists for grains and fruit, palms for protein and handfuls for vegetables: self-reported hand counts correlated with weighed grams at 0.59 for grains, 0.72 for protein, 0.76 for vegetables and 0.85 for fruit, with wide limits of agreement per meal and average differences close to zero (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40669561/). It is a Japanese hand system, not Precision Nutrition's, and the hand counts were self-reported. Another study tested a finger-width method: 80% of estimates within 25% of true weight, 13% within 10% (Gibson 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27547392/). A review of 136 estimation aids found mean error running from 92% under to 413% over (Fadeiye 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42176340/). People underestimate many portions (Almiron-Roig 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23932948/).

Weighing costs time (Harvey 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/: 14.6 to 23.2 minutes a day), and people stop. In context the portions are a minimum, so error that lands high does not cause a deficit problem, and Mode 2 and the weight trend carry the rest.

Not found: any trial comparing weighed food with estimated food on long-term weight loss. Dan's question is therefore open, not answered, and the evidence neither confirms nor rejects hands.
Dan also: "if it's a wash on the accuracy side then it's superior - you are eating better food quality and putting less effort in."

Counting is not accurate either. Self-reported intake undercounted energy by 12 to 14% (24 hour recall) and 31 to 36% (food frequency) in men, and 16 to 20% and 34 to 38% in women (Subar 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12835280/). Pooled studies found an average 28% underreport with questionnaires, more at higher BMI (Freedman 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24918187/). In 10 self-described diet-resistant people, intake was underreported by 47% (Lichtman 1992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/). That is a small selected group. App databases differ from lab values, with correlations of 0.73 to 0.96 but lower means for some nutrients (Griffiths 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29534771/), and these tests use recalls entered by researchers.

Honest limit: no study compares hand estimation with counting head to head, so "a wash" is Dan's judgment. The argument rests on effort and food quality: if error is similar, the cheaper method wins. Counting costs time and shifts choices toward numbers (Dan's opinion).
Dan also: "i don't advise against it."

The method does monitor: a daily yes or no on whether the Mode 1 portions were eaten, and a weight trend. It advises against counting calories in an app. Dan's reasoning (his opinion): if people eat the right foods the macros line up well enough, and people who chase numbers rarely eat the right foods. So the tracking is of the types and amounts of Mode 1 foods.

Evidence: a review of 22 studies found a consistent link between self-monitoring and weight loss but rated it weak (Burke 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/). People who tracked consistently lost 2.42 kg more at 3 months in a trial of 100, but they differed in other ways, and causation is unknown (Patel 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31396820/). In a 24 month trial, paper, PDA and PDA with feedback did not differ in weight change (Burke 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22704741/): adherence mattered more than method. Only about a quarter tracked daily over 12 weeks.

Not found: any trial of tracking food groups or portions instead of calories. That gap cuts both ways. The cost of calorie logging is the time and the dropout shown above, and the risk of a simpler check is that it measures less. The weight channel is the check.

### Mode 2 ("whatever you want") can erase the deficit.
Dan: "Are other nutrition strategies exempt from this concern?" and "Correct. There is no single diet strategy that eliminates poor human behavior. This one simply sets them up for the best chance of success."
Dan also: "we can fix obstacles. but we can't fix just 'not doing it'" and "the rules earn you the most possible freedom and the least sacrifice while still achieving your goals. it's your call if you want to give up that deal."
Evidence: in a 1-year trial of four named diets, weight loss followed self-reported adherence, not diet type (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/). A network meta-analysis found minimal differences between named diets (Johnston 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25182101/). Limit: adherence in Dansinger was self-reported, so people who lost weight may have reported better adherence. "Within reason" is not defined in numbers. The person decides it.

### Mode 1 is very low calorie.
Dan: "It's not for beginners, remember." And: "Mode 1 calories are not all your daily calories." Asked why that is not a problem, Dan: "because you have mode 2?" And: "mode 1 foods are like 800 cals a day". On a GLP-1: "even if you're on a glp you can eat your mode 1 foods. ... it's only 800 cals for the day". Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "Mode 1 foods are the minimum, not the maximum", and the low-energy answer tells the person to add carbs after work. For anyone with a medical condition or on medication, see "Injuries, conditions, medication" below.
Dan also: "It's not 800 calories a day. 800 calories of their day are mandatory food types and general amounts." Mode 1 is the minimum, not the day's total. Mode 2 has no cap.

Low energy availability is real. In 29 sedentary women over 5 days, hormone pulsing was unchanged at 30 kcal per kg of lean mass and changed below it (Loucks and Thuma 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/). A later randomized study of 35 women (Lieberman 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29023359/) found the odds of a menstrual disturbance fell 9% per unit of energy availability (odds ratio 0.91) and that no specific value emerged as a threshold; the authors describe disturbances as increasing linearly as energy availability falls, and a 2026 update moves away from a fixed threshold (Williams 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474492/). All of these set total daily intake over days. None tested a feeding window, and no study measures total intake on this method.

What it buys: a firm floor of protein and vegetables without counting. The risk sits with the person who also eats little in Mode 2, which the method does not measure. The alternative, a calorie floor, needs counting, with the error and effort that brings. A woman with a missed period gets the one-line doctor check.

### The same portions for everyone (4 palms, 3 fists, 1-2 thumbs)?
Dan: "It's your mandatory skeleton. The dynamic nature of the diet makes it so that if you're feeling good and not hungry, you can totally just have a mode 1 style dinner as well and then do a 1000+ calorie deficit day if you want. But that's not going to happen every day. Acute temporary calorie fluctuations generally work different than chronic. Acute deficits are powerful, acute surpluses are restorative, chronic of either is bad."
On refeeds: "It's called a 'refeed' or a 'diet break' and now instead of adding a ton of logistical headache and discipline to doing that, it's just built into your normal behavior. It's autoregulated."

Evidence: alternating 2-week diet blocks with 2-week energy-balance blocks gave more fat loss than continuous dieting in men with obesity (MATADOR, Byrne 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/), but that trial was small, per-protocol, and took 30 weeks versus 16. In resistance-trained adults (ICECAP, Peos 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/) there was no difference in fat mass or fat-free mass, but the intermittent group reported lower hunger and desire to eat. A 1-year trial found a 5:2 pattern did no better and no worse than continuous dieting (Headland 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30470804/). No study tested single-day deficits and appetite-driven refeeds, and the "chronic surplus is bad" half is untested.
Dan also: "yeah for their protein and veggie intake." Elsewhere he says bigger people have bigger hands, that this gets them about 90% of the way there, and that anyone who thinks they need more makes up the difference in Mode 2.

Writer's summary: hands scale the protein and vegetable portions to body size. Total energy is handled in Mode 2, which the person sets by appetite and the weight trend.

The downside: hand size tracks frame and height. A short heavy person gets a smaller palm than a tall lean one, which gives less protein than their body weight might suggest. Gains from added protein plateau around 1.62 g/kg a day (Morton 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/), and the Mode 1 portions plus the Mode 2 palm come to roughly 0.76 g/lb for a 180 lb man (the arithmetic in the protein entry below).

What it buys: one simple scaling rule with nothing to measure. The alternative is a calculator or table, which asks for weight, body fat and activity inputs and a daily number to hit. Not found: any study comparing hand size with lean mass or protein need, so the claim that hand size fits body size is Dan's reasoning, not tested.

### Does 0.7 g/lb protein hold up?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "About 1.6g/kg (0.73g/lb) bodyweight is sufficient for muscle building." The site line was changed on 2026-10-22 from "0.7g/lb bodyweight is sufficient for muscle building." Your Mode 1 foods hit this mark regardless of what you eat after. Dan: "You'll hit optimal protein without having to count it all."
Dan also: "whatever the research indicates. if you eat the portions you'll hit it", meaning about 1.6 g/kg (0.73 g/lb). The Mode 1 palm portions plus the Mode 2 palm come to roughly 0.76 g/lb for a 180 lb man, counting only the protein foods. That is a floor. Dan: you are not counting the incidental protein from foods outside the protein category, which often adds up by the end of the day to close to a palm of protein.
Verified limit: Morton 2018 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) found gains from added protein plateau around 1.62 g/kg/day for muscle gain. Studies in a calorie deficit point higher (Helms 2014, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 24(2):127-138, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/; Longland 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/).
Dan also: "muscle protein synthesis is irrelevant." On the four-palms gap, his answer was that nobody would eat that way, which is a point about the premise.

The premise: the objection pictures all four palms in one sitting, then up to 16 hours with nothing. Mode 1 portions are spread across the waking hours in any order, and Mode 2 adds a fifth palm in the evening, so most days spread across several meals.

Evidence on distribution: acute studies show per-meal effects. An even three-meal split gave 25% higher 24 hour protein synthesis than a skewed one in 8 people (Mamerow 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24477298/). 4 x 20 g beat 2 x 40 g and 8 x 10 g in trained men (Areta 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/). But 100 g in one dose gave a larger and longer response than 25 g (Trommelen 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/), against a fixed ceiling.

Longer term, evidence is thinner. A 6 week trial of 24 rugby players found no difference in lean mass between distributions (1.4 versus 1.5 kg; MacKenzie-Shalders 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26132746/). A review judged the benefit of distribution limited and not separable from total protein (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32429355/). Total protein was the strongest predictor in a 2013 meta-analysis (Schoenfeld 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/).

Dan's word "irrelevant" is stronger than the evidence, which says distribution is unresolved once total protein is matched. Cost of spreading protein by a schedule: more rules and a clock. Not found: a long-term trial of distribution in trained adults matched for total protein.

### Isn't Mode 1 repetitive?
Dan: "Mode 1 isn't the same foods every day." The food lists give many choices for each portion, and the person picks any order and combination.
If someone wants more variety than the lists give, Dan's view is that they are valuing food as entertainment more than being lean. He calls it an unbelievably reasonable deal, the best possible one. If it is not a good enough deal, Dan's view is that they do not care enough about the goal, which is fine. It is their call, and the rule stays as written.

### Can Mode 2 quality be poor?
Dan: this is the floor over ceiling principle (principle 4). Effort and discipline go into raising the floor and making it durable. "Where and when you want to reach the ceiling, go ahead."

Dan: even a truly grim Mode 2, say nothing but gummy bears, leaves you decent, because Mode 1 is already done. The whole point of the rules is that the floor is quite high. Dan also says that is the absolute worst case scenario. A typical Mode 2 includes the extra palm of protein and incidental protein from other foods, which together can add up to a palm or more. What the worst case still covers: the Mode 1 protein, vegetables and fats, about 110 g of protein for a 180 lb man in the FAQ example, which is above the standard recommended intake of 0.8 g/kg a day (about 65 g for that man, the writer's arithmetic). What it does not guarantee: the Mode 2 palm or a calorie deficit. That is the trade: a high floor for very few rules, with the ceiling left to the person.

Dan's suggested start to Mode 2 is a piece of fruit: fiber and water take the edge off before the free meal, it tops up liver glycogen after Mode 1, and it shows you can still enjoy clean foods in Mode 2. A suggestion, not a rule.

If someone feels guilty about Mode 2 foods, Dan: "then eat nutritious foods instead?" His examples: fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein.

Note: the original combined objection (poor variety, yet unconstrained Mode 2) contradicts itself. Mode 1 is the fixed part and Mode 2 is the free part, so both cannot be the problem at once.
Dan also: "i don't recommend ultra processed foods. but it's not a win/fail condition." A win is Mode 1 adhered to. Mode 2 also requires another protein block.

The downside: in 20 adults in a hospital ward, an ultra-processed diet raised intake by 508 calories a day over two weeks, and weight rose 0.9 kg (Hall 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/). In 55 adults over 8 weeks, a minimally processed diet lost 2.06% versus 1.05% on ultra-processed, a gap of 1.01 percentage points (Dicken 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40760353/). Both diets lost weight.

In context: those were whole-day menus that people were given. Mode 2 is one part of the day, after Mode 1 has already supplied protein, vegetables and fat, plus a required protein palm that crowds out some junk. The method does not set a Mode 2 menu. Dan's advice is to eat nutritious foods whenever there is the chance, and that is also his answer to someone who feels guilty about Mode 2.

The alternative, banning ultra-processed foods, adds a rule to the evening where social eating happens, which raises adherence risk. Not found: a trial of ultra-processed foods inside a partly structured day.

### Where does alcohol fit?
Dan: "The best amount is none but it's your decision." The method does not write it into the rules (principle 5).
Dan agrees that alcohol hits harder on a low-carb, low-calorie day. Alcohol is Mode 2 only, after the Mode 1 foods are eaten (writer's summary). "The best amount is none, but it's your decision."

Muscle: after a heavy session, alcohol at 1.5 g/kg (about 12 drinks) cut muscle protein synthesis by 24% to 37% versus protein alone in 8 men (Parr 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533082/). That is a very large dose. In men, alcohol raised strength loss at 36 hours (Barnes 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19230764/), but in 13 resistance-trained women it had no effect on torque recovery (Levitt 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386694/). A signaling study also found an effect in men and none in women (Duplanty 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27135475/).

Hypoglycemia with fasting or low carbohydrate: thin. The only systematic review found is in type 1 diabetes, where 8 of 13 studies linked alcohol to more hypoglycemia (Tetzschner 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29135074/). None was found in healthy dieters.

The benefit is freedom, since no alcohol rule is written (the discipline-bank principle). The alternative, an alcohol ban, adds a rule most people already know. The agent states the downside once and leaves the choice with the person.

### Can you lose fat and build muscle at once?
Dan on results: about 12 people have run it, and their PRs kept coming during the loss: "Yes, insanely." See "Proof" under Whole system.
Dan also: "i didn't say the early prs reflect muscle and not skill over a period. no program can make that claim." He also says it holds for everyone except lean people near their top FFMI, and that is client experience, not a controlled trial (Dan's coaching claim).

The downside: early strength gains are mostly neural. Neural factors drove most of the early gain in 8 weeks of training, and hypertrophy took over after 3 to 5 weeks (Moritani 1979, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/453338/; Sale 1988, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3057313/). So an early PR is not evidence of new muscle. Recomposition has been shown in short studies of young recreational people on high protein (Longland 2016; lean +1.2 kg, fat -4.8 kg over 4 weeks), but no source quantifies it in long-trained lifters. A deficit of 500 kcal a day or more blunts lean mass gain (Murphy 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/).

What the method claims: PRs keep coming during the loss in the people who ran it, about 12 clients and Dan's own year (self-reported). The method does not claim muscle gain from PRs alone.

The alternative, a separate bulk and cut, costs time and a second set of rules. Thin: client reports, no instruments.

### The "pain point", insulin and 2-3 week claims.
Dan: "Pain point isn't biological only. It encompasses all factors leading to adherence issues."
Dan's definition: the pain point is when people start finding it too hard to keep doing, for every possible reason (emotional, physical, mental), and they stop adhering. The 2-3 week mark comes from his coaching observation over 17 years, not a study.
There is no hard wall at 2-3 weeks. No study sets that limit. The closest is appetite: hunger rose in the first days and eased within about 3 weeks while ketotic in a very-low-energy diet study (Nymo 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28439092/), and each kg lost raised intake by about 100 kcal/day over 52 weeks in another (Polidori 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804272/). Trials show people can hold a reduced-calorie diet for months with support (Franz 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17904936/). The honest form of the claim is that appetite and adherence pressure builds, and that breaks help. For insulin, see the next entry.
Dan also: "It's a simple concept to understand what choices to make." On the car picture he says it "doesn't translate directly into the science of how it actually works, but it is a good way to structurally view it."

So it is a decision aid, not a physiology claim. The real literature is "metabolic flexibility", the ability to adapt fuel use to demand (Goodpaster and Sparks 2017, Cell Metabolism, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28467922/; Smith 2018, Endocrine Reviews, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29697773/). It has no single agreed measurement, and one analysis argues the usual measure is biased (Galgani 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18765680/). Evidence that carbohydrate restriction changes flexibility is thin: one small randomized crossover of 6 trained athletes over 14 days found no difference (Graybeal 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36673864/). Dan's travel tier 3, "don't eat. if your metabolism is working right that shouldn't be a big deal", is his judgment, untested.

What it buys: a person with no lab can still decide what to eat. Mode 1 every day, carbohydrate where life puts it. The alternative is a precise physiological claim, which would need measurements nobody has at home, or more rules to cover the gap. The method keeps the simple picture and says plainly that it is a picture.

### Why no carbs in Mode 1?
Mode 1 foods keep you full the longest per calorie. Insulin comes second. Dan: "Better insulin sensitivity lets you be okay not eating for longer." and "Better insulin sensitivity helps your hunger regulate better."
Fat loss comes from the deficit. Dan: "Insulin management alone doesn't lose weight. It just makes you not notice caloric deficits nearly as much when it's working right." No study is cited here for the insulin claims.
Dan also: "appetite is easier to keep low for the first half of the day without carbs". Verified limit: studies are mixed, and no study tests a carb-free first half of the day. Two small studies found no hunger or appetite difference by breakfast composition (Emilien 2017, 33 students, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27379830/; King 2025, 16 trained adults, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40029149/). Jakubowicz 2012 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22178258/) randomized 193 obese adults to a low-carbohydrate breakfast or a breakfast high in both carbohydrate and protein, and hunger and cravings were higher on the low-carbohydrate breakfast; because protein also differed, that trial cannot isolate carbohydrate. Where an appetite effect is shown, it is for protein, not for carbohydrate: in 19 people, raising protein from 15% to 30% of energy with carbohydrate fixed at 50% lowered spontaneous intake by 441 kcal/day (Weigle 2005, non-randomized, no control arm, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/), and a review of acute trials found a modest satiety effect of protein but no effect on intake at the next meal (Leidy 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/).
Dan, from his own practice: "Personally I am carrying a lot of muscle and fairly lean so I can deliberately eat a bowl of cereal or extra rice or fruit or whatever. My appetite is not out of control. more carbs give me bigger pumps and better chances of setting PRs." And: "most people will eat enough carbs and do just fine. they aren't very muscular or physically active." He ate 300 g of carbs on one recent day (anecdote). I did not find an answer from Dan written for this objection itself.

The claim is untested. I found no study that compares the benefit of carbohydrate by muscularity. Related findings are small: 29 trained men who chose higher carbohydrate gained more arm curl strength (+6.6% versus +3.0%) with no significant difference in lean tissue, but groups were not randomized (Ribeiro 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37114196/). A review found glycogen depletion from lifting unlikely and low glycogen did not blunt a key growth signal (Escobar 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27993175/).

How the method treats it: Mode 2 has no limit on carbs, and nutritious carbs are encouraged, so a muscular person already has room. Adding carbs to Mode 1 would cost the morning appetite benefit Dan reports. Plain label: Dan's experience, one person, not a finding.
Dan also: "i agree that few low carb diets can do that. I also think people who do keto or super low carb diets have poor adherence rates long term." This is not that diet. Mode 1 is not ketogenic, Dan's own Mode 1 is about 30 g of carbohydrate, and Mode 2 has no limit. "like i ate 300g of carbs yesterday" (one day, his anecdote). On the first days: "might be a little mentally foggy for a few days just adapting, but it's not a zero carb diet."

Gallstones are tied to the rate of loss. The risk rises sharply above about 1.5 kg a week (Weinsier 1995, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7847427/). On a 500 kcal a day program for 3 months, symptomatic gallstones were 152 versus 44 per 10,000 person-years compared with a milder diet (Johansson 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23736359/). That is a different, far lower-calorie diet. Keto flu rests on forum posts: 101 of 300 users reported symptoms, mostly in the first weeks (Bostock 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32232045/). No controlled trial was found. Electrolytes and hair loss: not found.

Whether adherence to keto or very low carb diets is poor over the long term is Dan's view, and it is not tested here. The one 12-month trial found measures only study completion, not how closely people followed the diet: 79% of the low-carb group and 82% of the low-fat group completed the intervention, and both groups received dietary counseling at regular intervals (Bazzano 2014, 148 adults, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25178568/). That is far more support than a typical dieter gets, so it says little about real-world adherence. The abstract gives no figure for adherence to the diet itself.

### Can I do a failure set on Mode 1 with no carbs?
Dan: "One single set, are you kidding me? If that's the case you have insulin issues, not storing glycogen from yesterday properly. That's all the more reason to stick to Mode 1 until it feels better." The "insulin issues" inference is Dan's reasoning.
Dan's view, paraphrased: if someone truly cannot do a failure set without eating carbs right before the workout, humans would not have survived this long. That is his opinion, not a finding.

Evidence, stated plainly: muscle glycogen use follows how much work is done. In a meta-analysis of 20 studies, depletion was larger with more sets and longer sessions (Hamidvand 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41420384/). Trained men on a 39-minute, three-exercise leg session lost about 27% of vastus lateralis glycogen (Haff 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10997956/), and bodybuilders doing about 20 hard sets lost about 28% (Essen-Gustavsson 1990, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2289498/). One set is a small fraction of that by proportion; no study tests one set. After an overnight fast the liver is the store that supplies blood glucose, which is why muscle glycogen is expected to be largely kept, but I found no measured adult study to cite for that. Training after an overnight fast gave similar growth and strength to training fed in a 12-week trial of 28 young adults (Vieira 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40335157/) and in a small review (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41316673/). Limits: carbohydrate did raise total volume in fasted sessions longer than 45 minutes (King 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35809162/), so this is about short sessions like the method's. If someone feels weak on a fasted morning, the site's answers on low energy apply (add carbs after work), and sleep, a hard prior day and a deep deficit are other causes.

### Are the supplements (vitamin D, creatine) medical advice?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "I'm not a doctor. Look up the research and make the call."
Dan also: "People don't have to take 4,000 if they don't want to, but it's certainly more than 400 IU. This research has been going on for a long time." And: "I don't believe that the recommended dose of 600 or 400 IU is sufficient, and I believe there is considerable scientific research recently to back this." He has taken 4,000 IU a day for 5 years (his practice, one person, no blood level). It is optional. Blood tests and dose are for a doctor.

Facts: the US recommended intake is 600 IU a day to age 70 and 800 after. 4,000 IU is the upper limit set by the US and EU bodies, not a target.

The case for higher: a recalculation says about 2,909 IU a day is needed for 97.5% of people to reach 50 nmol/L, and 7,248 for people with obesity (Veugelers 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26690210/). That is a blood-level argument, not an outcomes one. In a meta-analysis of 51 studies in elite athletes, about 30% of adults were below 50 nmol/L (Harju 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35882673/).

The case against: 25,871 adults on 2,000 IU for 5.3 years showed no cancer or cardiovascular benefit (Manson 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/). In 311 healthy adults, 4,000 IU for 3 years lowered radial bone density by 2.4% versus 1.2% at 400 IU, with no change in bone strength (Burt 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31454046/). The Endocrine Society suggests against supplementing above the usual intake in healthy adults under 75, and against routine testing (Demay 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/). No lifter trial was found.
Dan also: "Creatine lets you get a few more reps and set PRs." His claim is about reps and PRs, not lean mass.

Evidence supports that. A meta-analysis of 53 trials found a small to moderate upper-body effect (effect size 0.317, bench press 0.265), in tasks under 3 minutes (Lanhers 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328852/). A review of 22 studies found max reps at a set load rose 26% with creatine versus 12% with placebo, with highly variable response (Rawson and Volek 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636102/). Body composition effect size was 0.17 (Branch 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12945830/). Some of creatine's lean mass gain may be water, and the effect on measured muscle size is very small and not clearly different from zero (Burke 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/). Creatine also lifts the PR numbers the method tracks, so it is a small boost to the measuring stick (writer's summary).

Fish oil "is great for joints" is Dan's FAQ line. A meta-analysis of 42 trials found a small pain benefit overall, significant in rheumatoid arthritis and not in osteoarthritis (5 trials, effect -0.17, interval -0.57 to 0.24) (Senftleber 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28067815/). So the claim holds for inflammatory arthritis, not shown for osteoarthritis.

Both are optional and cheap. The alternative, no supplements, costs nothing and loses a small gain.

### Isn't skipping milk and post-workout nutrition wrong?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): both are explicit FAQ entries (milk: "Just skip it"; post-workout nutrition is "Not as useful as it is inconvenient").
Dan also: "This question isn't even directed at the eating plan. It's describing something entirely different."

His point stands. Dairy is not skipped. Greek yogurt, cottage cheese and cheese are Mode 1 foods. Only milk as a drink is left out, and Mode 2 is unrestricted. Whole milk is not a protein portion, but nothing stops it in Mode 2 (writer's summary).

For context, calcium intake was below the average requirement in about 38% of US adults in NHANES, before any special diet (Fulgoni 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21865568/). Whether a long deficit affects bone, especially in women, is a separate question. No study on that is in the research files, so none is cited, and Dan did not answer it, because his answer is that the objection misdescribes the plan. What it buys: dairy protein stays available. The alternative, requiring milk, adds a calorie drink inside Mode 1 for little extra over yogurt and cottage cheese (writer's summary).

### Hunger, low energy, weekends, food prep, "I want to change it".
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): each has its own entry, including night hunger, low energy, weekends, prep, and "I want to make changes or pick and choose parts of this".
Dan also: "Why would it?" The restriction is "fewer calories for 8 hours of the day?" Then Mode 2. "While you are in Mode 1 you're eating the most satiating possible per calorie macronutrients."

Protein's fullness effect is modest. Acute trials show more fullness but not lower intake at the next meal, and long-term data are limited and mixed, with adherence the main factor (Leidy 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/). In 19 people, going to 30% protein cut spontaneous intake by 441 kcal a day (Weigle 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/), with no control arm. Hiding vegetables in meals cut daily intake by 202 to 357 kcal with hunger unchanged (Blatt 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289225/). After a 36 hour fast, 24 lean adults ate about 480 kcal more the next day, well short of the deficit (Johnstone 2002, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12461679/).

Dan's idea that unsteady energy means "insulin issues" that this eating should help is his opinion. Sleep, under-eating, iron, thyroid, anxiety and caffeine are other causes (writer's summary), and true blood sugar conditions go to a doctor. Acclimation takes about 4 days, longer if someone is in rough shape, and hunger at a certain time is solved by moving portions, not by Mode 1 hours.

The alternative, bigger daytime meals, trades away the deficit for comfort.

#### Timing and sleep

### Doesn't a big evening meal go against the circadian evidence?
Dan: "don't have to eat a big meal you'll still have 2-3 hours before bed."

The downside, in context: in a 12 week trial, overweight women with metabolic syndrome on a fixed ~1400 kcal lost less weight and had worse glucose and triglyceride changes when most calories came at dinner than when they came at breakfast (Jakubowicz 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23512957/). It is one site, women only, and a shift in meal size, not a shift in window. Late lunch eaters (after 15:00) lost less weight in a 20 week program of 420 people (Garaulet 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23357955/), though they also skipped breakfast more, so the effect is tangled. In a lab study of 20 healthy adults, dinner at 22:00 gave higher night glucose than dinner at 18:00 (Gu 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32525525/).

Where it does not match this method: the method does not require a big evening meal. Mode 2 is free eating with one protein palm, and the person decides its size. Mode 1 already holds about 800 calories of the day's food earlier on. The evening is where social and family eating usually falls, so Mode 2 sits there so the rules do not fight the rest of life.

The alternative, a strict early window, costs more sacrifice: evenings are when most people eat with others. A 12 month trial with both arms calorie restricted found no significant weight advantage for an early eating window (Liu 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35443107/), and a 12 week trial of a late window found none either (Lowe 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32986097/). Contradicting in part, an early window plus calorie restriction gave more weight loss over 14 weeks (Jamshed 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35939311/).

Thin: no large long-term trial compares early and late eating with calories matched, and none tests this split.

### Doesn't skipping breakfast go with worse heart and metabolic health?
Dan: "then don't skip it if you don't want to. that's a behavioral study though and likely doesn't discern between healthy food choices and not. skipping breakfast is a common habit among people who don't watch their eating at all."

The downside: breakfast skippers had a higher heart disease risk (RR 1.27) over 16 years in 26,902 male health professionals (Cahill 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23877060/), and more atherosclerosis in a cross-sectional study where only 3% of 4052 adults skipped (Uzhova 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28982495/).

In context: both are observational. Both adjusted for diet, but neither abstract measures how much diet quality explains the link, so Dan's confounding point is plausible and unquantified. In randomized trials, a meta-analysis of 13 trials found people assigned to breakfast ended slightly heavier (+0.44 kg) (Sievert 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30700403/). Another found skipping lowered weight by 0.54 kg but raised LDL cholesterol by 9.24 mg/dL in 3 trials (Bonnet 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32304359/). A 16 week trial of 309 people found no weight effect (Dhurandhar 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24898236/).

Also, Mode 1 is not a fast. It starts when the person wakes and is protein, vegetables and fat. A person who is not hungry can eat the portions later in Mode 1.

Cost of the alternative: a mandatory big breakfast of mixed foods adds calories and appetite early in the day. Thin: the trials last weeks and none tests this pattern.

### Does a large evening meal hurt sleep and cause reflux?
Dan: "i didn't say it had to be large." The same answer as the circadian entry: Mode 2 is not a set meal, and the person leaves 2 to 3 hours before bed.

The downside, accurately: in 147 people with reflux disease and 294 controls, eating within 3 hours of bed went with 7.45 times the odds of reflux disease (Fujiwara 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16393212/), though that is self-report and the authors note the 3 hour advice lacked clinical support. In 30 reflux patients a meal 2 hours before bed caused more reflux lying down than one 6 hours before (Piesman 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17573791/). A smaller trial found no difference between a meal before 19:00 and one at 21:00 (Orr 1998, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9798810/). On sleep, findings are mixed: dinner 1 hour before bed shortened sleep in 13 young women (Enomoto 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41975458/), but sleep structure was not changed in 20 healthy adults (Duan 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34017207/; Gu 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32525525/).

What the 2 to 3 hour gap buys: it sits inside what these studies treat as the safer side, and poor sleep raises hunger by about 385 kcal a day in experiments (Al Khatib 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804960/). The alternative, no evening eating, costs the social and family meals that make the method keepable. Thin: no trial tests gaps between 2 and 6 hours, and people with reflux disease are a medical case for the doctor line.

### Are energy drinks, diet soda and caffeine a problem in Mode 1?
Dan: "that's all fine i don't care. it's not impactful vs the sacrifice to not have them." Asked about caffeine and sleep, he asked first whether Mode 1 is fasted, and said "i personally don't consume caffeine after 12pm noon." That is his own practice, not a rule.

Mode 1 is not fasted. It is protein, vegetables and fat eaten from waking. The drinks sit under the 30 calorie limit.

The downside is real for sleep. 400 mg of caffeine even 6 hours before bed disturbed sleep in a small placebo-controlled study (Drake 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24235903/). A systematic review found caffeine lengthens time to fall asleep and cuts total sleep, with effects depending on dose and timing and varying by person (Clark and Landolt 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26899133/). Energy drinks range from 50 to 505 mg per can (Reissig 2009, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18809264/).

In context: 400 mg is about four cups of coffee, and morning use is a different case from late-day use. Banning these drinks takes away a small comfort that helps people keep to Mode 1, and poor sleep raises hunger (Al Khatib 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804960/). Thin: caffeine half-life figures were not retrieved, and I read no study on diet soda and appetite for this entry.

#### Mode 1 foods

### Why is a cruciferous vegetable required?
Dan: "nutrient density."

The benefit: it is a way to make sure one vegetable a day is a dense one. On Di Noia's 2014 ranking of 47 fruits and vegetables, cruciferous and leafy greens sit in the top half (watercress 100, kale 49, broccoli 34.9, cauliflower 25) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24901795/). The ranking is the author's own score, for 17 nutrients per 100 kcal, not an official list. Large cohort data tie more cruciferous intake to lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.78 top versus bottom fifth in 134,796 Chinese adults, Zhang 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21593509/), and an umbrella review found only lower-risk associations, but rates the evidence as suggestive at best and calls for trials (Li 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35352732/).

The downside and the contradiction: one cohort found a weak positive link between glucosinolates and heart disease (HR 1.09), with Brussels sprouts at 1 or more servings a week at 1.16 (Ma 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29988715/). All of this is observational.

The cost of dropping the requirement is small, since it is one item. Cost of the requirement: some people dislike or digest these vegetables badly. The method's answer is to pick another food from the list if it is not eaten. Edge cases such as blood thinners or thyroid conditions are dietitian questions, outside the method.

### Is a thumb of fat too little for fat-soluble vitamins?
Dan: "take your vitamin d with fats then."

The downside, accurately: fat helps absorption. Vitamin D3 peaked 32% higher with a fatty meal than a fat-free meal in 50 older adults after one 50,000 IU dose (Dawson-Hughes 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25441954/). Carotenoids were negligible with a fat-free dressing and rose with 6 g and 28 g of oil in 7 people (Brown 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15277161/). Vitamin K1 and retinyl palmitate rose with fat up to 32 g in 12 women (White 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28814399/). A 7 day trial found 3 g of fat in a meal gave the same vitamin E and carotene response as 36 g (Roodenburg 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10799382/), so results conflict.

In context: no study defines a minimum amount, and the Mode 1 day is not fat free. Fat comes from eggs, fish, dark meat and yogurt, and the thumb is added only when the protein is lean. Mode 2 eats with no fat limit. Dan's view is that people need less fat than they think (opinion). His practical fix is to take the vitamin with a fatty meal.

Alternative: adding fat to every meal costs hundreds of calories (Dan's pitfall). Not found: a study of vitamin K or preformed vitamin A needs in a fat-limited morning.

### Aren't the food lists arbitrary (potato out, squash in, beans and lentils out)?
Dan: "calorie content to glycemic index ratio, generally." That is his answer on the lists as a whole; for beans and lentils his published reason is below.

Dan's other answers on the lists: potato is out because it is a root or tuber, and beans and lentils are out because they are good food but are not primarily a protein source and not a non-starchy vegetable (Dan: "Beans and lentils aren't primarily a protein Source or a non-starchy vegetable"). Squash and carrot are okay. Frozen mixes are fine, "this isn't rocket science we just need to generally hit the mark."

Glycemic index values for individual foods could not be retrieved reliably, so none are given here. The best-supported general finding is that legumes are usually low GI while most potato varieties are high GI (Atkinson 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34258626/). That means GI does not explain the beans and lentils exclusion. The reason there is that they are primarily carbohydrate.

Honest limit: the lists are a judgment about calories, fullness and carbohydrate load for a morning, not a derived formula, and Dan's ratio is stated in general terms.

What the lists buy: fewer decisions and a fixed floor. The alternative is a long permitted list or a calculation, which costs focus every day. Any food not on the lists is still allowed in Mode 2.

### Doesn't leaving out fruit, whole grains and legumes remove foods linked to lower disease risk?
Dan: "you can eat as many of those foods as you like." That is in Mode 2. For a person who feels guilty about Mode 2 foods: "like eat fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein."

The downside is that these foods are linked to lower risk: whole grain, per 90 g a day, to lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.83) (Aune 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27301975/); fruit and vegetables, per 200 g a day (RR 0.90) (Aune 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/); legumes, highest versus lowest intake, HR 0.94 (Zargarzadeh 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36811595/). All observational, with small effects and residual confounding.

In context: they are not left out of the day. They are left out of the morning Mode 1 set because those calories and carbohydrate are what Mode 1 spends on protein, vegetables and fat. Mode 2 is where they go, and Dan says eating them there is encouraged when there is the chance. There are two berry allowances, kept separate. The women's morning change is the one exception to Mode 1 Rule 3 for that purpose: up to a half cupped handful (about 15 g) of high-fiber carbs (see "Women" below). Separately, Dan says anyone can sprinkle berries into Mode 1 if they absolutely need to, and it is not a hill to die on. Berries are high fiber and low calorie. Other fruit is not added to Mode 1, apart from the little fruit in the women's allowance.

Alternative: making them mandatory in Mode 1 raises morning calories and appetite, and his claim that appetite stays lower without carbs in the morning is only partly supported by the evidence. It is mixed.

### Are powders and bars as good as whole foods for protein?
Dan: "I don't disagree with that." Whole foods differ in fullness and leucine content. His rule: any protein powder is fine, any style. Bars are for unique days such as travel, not a daily staple.

The downside, accurately: for whey alone, solid and liquid forms cut intake at 1 hour about equally in 14 men (Akhavan 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20733582/). But in 9 older adults a solid meal replacement gave lower hunger, insulin and ghrelin than a liquid one (Tieken 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17533583/). So whole foods may fill more, and the evidence is small and mixed.

Muscle: soy and animal protein gave the same strength and lean mass in a meta-analysis of 9 studies (Messina 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722584/), and vegan and omnivore young men matched at 1.6 g/kg gained alike in 12 weeks, though groups were not randomized (Hevia-Larrain 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33599941/).

What allowing powders buys: a way to hit the portions on a worst week or a trip (Dan's tier list). The alternative, whole food only, costs prep and fails on travel days. Not found: a trial of shake versus mixed whole food in a deficit.

### Isn't it expensive (fish, nuts, avocado, protein powder)?
Dan: "That's easy for an agent to figure out. I personally do this." The agent picks cheaper foods from the existing lists; there is no new rule.

Two points on the premise (writer's summary, from Dan's fat answers). Nuts and avocado are not required. A fat thumb is only added if every protein source is ultra lean, which he says is unlikely for most people, and any protein powder style is fine. No price comparison of Mode 1 foods is in the research files; not found.

What it buys: no extra rule for the person to carry. The alternative is a cost-optimized plan written for them, which is the meal plan the method avoids. The cost to the person is time spent choosing, which the agent takes on.

#### Portions, counting and protein

### Isn't the fat thumb threshold imprecise?
Dan: "no need to be that precise, the benefit gained from accuracy is minimal vs the focus required."

Accurate: a thumb is about 1 tablespoon at most (Dan), and it is only added if every protein source in the day is ultra lean, which is uncommon. Eggs, salmon, dark meat and ground meat already cover it. The rule exists to stop the common pitfall of adding olive oil, avocado and nuts to every meal, which can add hundreds of calories (Dan).

Evidence on precision: hand scales are partly tested. Shinozaki 2025 (1,081 adults, 12,148 meals) found hand counts tracked weighed food at group level (correlations 0.59 to 0.85) with wide per-meal error (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40669561/). A finger-width method put 80% of estimates for geometric foods within 25% of true weight in 67 people (Gibson 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27547392/). Both are different methods from Precision Nutrition's, so they support the idea of useful estimation, not proof of these exact hands.

The benefit: no scale, no app. The cost of precision: logging took 14.6 to 23.2 minutes a day in one program (Harvey 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/). A few grams of fat do not change the result because Mode 2 sets the larger part of the day's energy. Thin: the exact threshold has no evidence behind it. It is a judgment.

#### Restraint, bingeing and Mode 2

### Isn't strict daytime restriction then a free evening a restrict-binge cycle?
Dan: "how restrictive is it truly." / "how is this worse than anything else." / "Fewer calories for 8 hours of the day?"

Context of size: Mode 1 is about 800 calories of the day, a minimum and not a limit. It is not an 800 calorie day, and it is not a fast. Mode 2 has no limit.

The downside is real in the literature. In 496 adolescent girls, fasting for 24 hours (going without eating for weight control) showed stronger and more consistent predictive relations to later binge eating and bulimic pathology than dietary restraint, though the fasting effects were only significantly stronger than restraint for some comparisons (Stice 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19025239/). Rigid control goes with more binges, flexible control with fewer and more success (Westenhoefer 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10349584/). A review found restraint within a structured weight program was not linked to more eating problems (Schaumberg 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26841705/). After a 36 hour fast, next-day intake rose by about 2 MJ against a deficit near 12 MJ in 24 lean adults (Johnstone 2002, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12461679/). Intermittent restriction with breaks showed no difference in eating disorder behaviors in 61 trained adults and lower hunger (Peos 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/).

What this method buys: freedom in the evening and a floor that survives a bad week. Where a person truly cannot manage it, Dan's view is that no eating strategy will work and TTM is the smallest sacrifice possible. Not found: a study of this pattern, or of daytime restriction followed by a free evening.

### Does the rule language raise disordered-eating risk?
Dan: "that standard eliminated all diets."

His point is that any diet with rules would fail that test. Accurately, the risk is real in some groups. In schoolchildren aged 14 to 15, severe dieting raised later eating disorder onset 18 times and moderate dieting 5 times in girls, though psychiatric illness was an independent predictor (Patton 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10082698/). Unhealthy weight-control behaviors in 2,516 adolescents predicted binge eating with loss of control (OR 6.4 in girls) (Neumark-Sztainer 2006, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16567152/). But a meta-analysis found dieting a contradictory predictor (Stice 2002, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12206196/), and body dissatisfaction was the strongest one (Stice 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21764035/).

Context: those are adolescents. The method is for generally healthy adults. A person who says food scares them or who has a history of disordered eating gets one line to check with a doctor, and the method does not treat it. Dan: "that's not a health and fitness problem to solve."

The alternative of "no rules" costs the result: Dan calls general advice inadequate, and no trial compares it with a structured plan. Thin: no study measures rule wording.

### Doesn't "maximize autonomy" conflict with "mandatory" foods?
Dan: "This is the least mandatory structure to create results."

The method writes down only what moves the floor (the discipline bank principle). Mandatory is the Mode 1 foods and amounts, once a day. Everything else is free: Mode 2 is unrestricted, there is no meal plan, no calorie counting, the order and timing of Mode 1 portions is the person's, and the only tracking is a daily yes or no on Mode 1.

The downside is real: a daily requirement is less freedom than none. The alternative with more autonomy is less structure, which Dan says is inadequate for results. The alternative with less autonomy is meal plans, calorie targets or app tracking, which cost more effort and adherence risk. One observational study found flexible dietary control linked to lower weight and fewer binges, and rigid control to the reverse (Westenhoefer 1999, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10349584/). That is an association, and it does not test this design. No study tests "least mandatory structure"; it is Dan's design judgment.

### Doesn't a long deficit lower energy expenditure ("metabolic adaptation")? Where are the diet breaks?
Dan: "Diet breaks happen all the time in Mode 2."

The downside is real but varies a lot by study. Estimates run from about 50 to 100 kcal a day (Muller 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26399868/; Martins 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32844188/, where it was 92 kcal at the end of the diet and gone at one year) to about 500 kcal a day in 14 Biggest Loser contestants after extreme loss (Fothergill 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27136388/). Hall 2022 reads that case as energy compensation (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34816627/).

Diet-break trials are mixed. MATADOR found more fat loss with alternating 2 week blocks, per protocol, in 17 men, but it took 30 weeks versus 16 (Byrne 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/). ICECAP in resistance-trained adults found no difference in fat mass but lower hunger (Peos 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/). Headland 2019 found no difference at 12 months (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30470804/). All used breaks of days or weeks. Mode 2 as a daily break has not been tested.

What it buys: the break needs no schedule and no extra discipline. The alternative, a chronic deficit with planned breaks, adds logistics; its cost shows up as another rule to remember. If loss stalls, the options are more steps or reining in Mode 2.

### Isn't "everything in moderation" simpler and just as effective?
Dan: "Inadequate for results."

No randomized trial of "everything in moderation" against a structured plan was found. The closest, Weight Watchers versus standard primary care, gave 5.06 versus 2.25 kg lost at 12 months, but standard care is not moderation and the study was industry-funded (Jebb 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21906798/). Named diets of every kind give similar results when followed, and adherence matters most (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/; Johnston 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25182101/). That does not show moderation works; it shows no named structure beats another.

The method has moderation in it: Mode 2. What it adds is a floor that does not depend on judgment each day: Mode 1 foods, eaten every day. Moderation is cheap to state but undefined, so the problem shows up as no defined floor and a deficit nobody notices missing. Dan's view is that people want flexibility or hard rules, and cannot have both. The honest statement is that neither has been tested head to head against this method.

#### Training, muscle and the scale

### Can you keep muscle in a deficit with one set per exercise?
Dan: "mode 2 required another protein block." And: "why would you lose muscle if you are getting stronger."

Dan's reasoning is that protein is part of retention and that PRs are the signal that muscle is holding. Both parts need limits.

The downside: no trial tests a low volume program in a deficit. The closest compares 3 and 5 sets per exercise in 38 trained men on 30 kcal/kg for 6 weeks, and lean mass fell in both (Roth 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36114738/). A review says the evidence on volume is insufficient (Roth 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35146569/). A deficit of about 500 kcal a day or more prevented lean mass gain in a meta-analysis, while strength gains stayed comparable (Murphy 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/). That last point cuts against the use of strength as proof: strength can hold while muscle falls.

What the protein part has: high protein kept more lean mass in a deficit with hard training (Longland 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/).

Dan's "getting stronger" is a signal, not proof, as the next entry says. In the writer's summary, the strongest support for the method here is the protein, not the one set. What it buys: low-volume training in a deficit costs little recovery. The alternative, high volume, costs hours and recovery during a deficit, and the studies show no clear advantage.

### How is cruise weight computed, and aren't "maximum FFMI" and "lowest sustainable body fat" undefined?
Dan: cruise weight "is the bottom healthy band combined with the upper band of FFMI." That would be a "level 100" peaked physique. "But there is a range that you get into where your FFMI creeps up and your body fat creeps down and your scale weight is generally the same." "Genetics and wrist and ankle circumference affect it a bit, hence why it's a general range." And: "You don't need to define it. Once you're hovering around that weight you just set PRs and stop worrying about the scale."

So it is a range, not a formula, and the method gives no numbers for the bands. The examples (about 178 lb at 5'9" for a man; about 140, maybe 150, for an average-height woman) are examples only. The downside is real: a height-only number ignores frame, and a person cannot measure FFMI at home. FFMI limit studies are not in the research files.

What it buys: a direction without measurement. The signals are PRs and the mirror. The alternative, a formula, needs a body-fat reading that is error-prone at home and creates false precision. Its problem would show up as chasing a number instead of lifting.

### Isn't a 10% loss in 3 months mostly water, and isn't rapid loss risky for lean mass and gallstones?
These are client reports. Dan: "Overweight people have more than just glycogen and bodyfat to lose. There's a drastic repartitioning of fluids in their body." He expects about a 10% drop in the first 3 months, and also thinks people fail from going backwards, not from speed. Body composition was not measured; not found.

Early loss is largely water. On 800 kcal a day ketogenic, 6 obese subjects lost 467 g a day, of which 61.2% was water, 35.0% fat and 3.8% protein (Yang 1976, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/956398/). Glycogen is stored with 3 to 4 parts water (Kreitzman 1992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1615908/). That was 10 day periods and a ketogenic diet; Mode 1 is not ketogenic.

On speed: 10% of 180 lb over 12 weeks is about 0.6 kg a week (writer's arithmetic), under the 1.5 kg a week where gallstone risk climbs (Weinsier 1995, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7847427/). In a 200-person trial, rapid and gradual loss were regained about equally, about 71% at 144 weeks (Purcell 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25459211/). Among 24 athletes, 0.7% a week kept lean mass and 1.4% a week did not (Garthe 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21558571/).

The alternative, slower loss, costs more months of sacrifice with no better regain in that trial.

#### Safety, nutrients and supplements

### Does it hit all the micronutrients? What about fiber, gut health and plant variety?
Dan: "I didn't say you would hit all your RDA for everything. No diet will unless you have 100% strict control of every food at all times. It does a much better job than most people." On vegetables: "That's three more vegetables than a typical person eats in a day. A lot of these questions are complaining that it's not completely perfect but still super easy to do." And: "I could fix these complaints by taking away more freedom and rolling the dice on adherence." On gaps: "Or just take a multivitamin." "Better than most" is Dan's claim, and nobody has analyzed the Mode 1 foods for nutrient content.

Typical intake is poor. In US surveys, 9.3% of adults met the vegetable recommendation in 2015 and 10.0% in 2019 (Lee-Kwan 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29145355/; Lee 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34990439/). In NHANES, 45% were below the average requirement for magnesium, 38% for calcium and 70% for vitamin D (Fulgoni 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21865568/). A shortfall is not a deficiency. Plant variety and the microbiome: not found in the research files.

Multivitamins: the US task force found insufficient evidence for disease prevention (USPSTF 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35727271/). A review found cancer odds 0.93 with multivitamins, and no cardiovascular benefit (O'Connor 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35727272/). So a multivitamin is an option, not a proven fix.

### Does high protein harm the kidneys?
Dan: the first part of the objection is wrong, and the second part "is not the job of the diet". Stated plainly: in healthy adults, high protein has not been shown to harm the kidneys. Kidney disease is a medical matter.

Evidence: a meta-analysis of 28 randomized trials in 1,358 healthy adults found no difference in kidney filtration with high protein (at least 1.5 g/kg a day, 20% of energy or 100 g a day) (Devries 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30383278/). A review of 26 studies found filtration within the normal range, with mostly moderate-to-high bias and mostly under 6 months (Van Elswyk 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30032227/). Fourteen resistance-trained men ate about 3.3 g/kg for about a year with no adverse change in kidney tests (Antonio 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27807480/). The method's protein, about 1.6 g/kg (Dan's figure), is near that 1.5 g/kg cut.

Limits: short trials, healthy people, small samples. A narrative review says protein restriction may be right with existing kidney disease (Martin 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16174292/). The 2024 kidney guideline was not read. What it buys: the protein that keeps lean mass. Anyone with a kidney condition gets the doctor line.

### Don't saturated fat and red or processed meat raise heart risk?
Dan: "That's debatable still and also not mandatory to do." The Mode 1 lists include chicken, turkey, fish, eggs and yogurt. Beef and pork are options, not requirements. Bacon and sausage only count if lean, and a fat thumb is only added when every protein is ultra lean.

The evidence has two sides. Cutting saturated fat lowered cardiovascular events (relative risk 0.83, moderate certainty) with no clear change in all-cause mortality (0.96) or cardiovascular death (0.95) (Hooper 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32827219/). The American Heart Association advisory favors swapping in polyunsaturated oil (Sacks 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28620111/). A cardiology review disagrees (Astrup 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32562735/). In a pooled analysis, processed meat was linked to heart disease (relative risk 1.42 per 50 g a day) and unprocessed red meat was not (1.00) (Micha 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20479151/). A 2019 guideline reached an opposite practical conclusion, and it is contested (Johnston 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31569235/).

The lean-leaning lists cost nothing in freedom. A rule banning red meat would add a mandatory restriction on a debated point. The agent does not push either side.

### What about vitamin K and warfarin, or raw cruciferous vegetables and the thyroid?
Dan: "These are dietitian questions for edge cases." The decision: blood thinners, thyroid and kidney conditions sit outside the method. The agent says the doctor line once. No cooking or seaweed advice is given.

Why no tip, from the files: for warfarin, the literature stresses a stable habitual vitamin K intake, not avoidance (Holmes 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21914559/). A review finds some brassicas such as collards and Brussels sprouts carry goitrin that could reduce thyroid iodine uptake, while commercial broccoli and kale carry little (Felker 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26946249/). Seaweed iodine ranges from 16 to 8165 micrograms per gram, and some dishes may exceed the 1,100 microgram daily upper limit (Teas 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15588380/). So seaweed advice could do harm.

What it buys: one cruciferous vegetable a day for nutrient density (Dan's reason; Di Noia 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24901795/) with no special rules. The alternative, a rule set for every edge case, adds rules for everyone to cover a few. For those few, the dietitian is the right person.

#### Results, regain and long-term

### Twelve clients is self-selected. Don't dropouts and stalls undermine "it works"?
These are client reports, self-reported by Dan and not independently reviewed. About 12 people have run it. Dan: "1 quit because of divorce happening and her life turned upside down. 1 stopped working out but never stopped the eating and is keeping their weight down. Then a few are in various states of stagnation due to just not doing the workouts." He reports a few at low 20s of pounds in a month, one 27 lb in a season and one 50 lb in 5 months.

The downsides: the sample is small, the people chose to be coached, they could be survivors, and no comparison group exists. This is not a controlled trial and does not prove the method.

Context: dropout is the norm in diet trials. In a 1-year comparison of four named diets, completion was 50% to 65%, and weight loss tracked self-reported adherence (r=0.60), not diet type (r=0.07) (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/). In a 12-month trial of week-on, week-off and 5:2 dieting, only 146 of 332 completed (Headland 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30470804/).

What it buys: the only data currently available is shown with its limits. The anonymous check-ins are meant to build longer data over time. The alternative, claiming nothing, would hide what has been seen.

### Don't most dieters regain? "No rebound" rests on 12 clients.
Dan: "If you stop doing what's making you lose the weight, you will gain the weight back. This is why people fail, not that they aren't losing it fast enough." And: "This approach, if maintained, is the lowest sacrifice, highest freedom for the results it gives." Regain being math is his reasoning. "No rebound" is a client report only, from a small group with short follow-up.

Regain is common. About 71% was regained at 144 weeks in the completers, rapid or gradual, and 76% by intention to treat in both groups (Purcell 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25459211/). About 20% succeed at keeping at least 10% off for a year (Wing and Phelan 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002825/), and 17.3% in a US survey (Kraschnewski 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20479763/). A pooled analysis of structured programs found about 3 kg or 3% of starting weight still off at 5 years (Anderson 2001, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11684524/). In the National Weight Control Registry, more than 87% kept at least 10% off at 10 years, but these are survivors with self-reported weight (Thomas 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355667/).

Nothing here tests this method's maintenance. What it buys: a way of eating that is meant to be kept, so the thing that causes regain, stopping, is less likely. The alternative is any approach people drop, and the weight returns there too.

### Is it sustainable for years with no long-term data?
Dan: "I've eaten this way for 3 years." That is one person, an anecdote.

No trial of this method runs that long. Context: in 12-month trials, completion on named diets was 50% to 65% (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/). In structured programs followed 4 to 5 years, retention was 55.4% on very-low-energy diets and 79.7% on balanced reduced-calorie diets (Anderson 2001, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11684524/). In the National Weight Control Registry, falling restraint and less self-weighing went with regain (Thomas 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24355667/), observational and self-reported.

The design aims at years: Dan's test is that adherence must survive low points, so the worst week still checks the boxes. At cruise weight the eating stays the same (Dan: keep setting PRs), and more muscle lets a person eat more carbohydrate in Mode 2, by his account and his own practice (untested).

What it buys: the least sacrifice for results, which is what lets people keep going. The alternative with a longer record, the usual diets, shows the same dropout problem. Nobody has a long-term record for this goal.

### Doesn't the tone of the method erode trust?
The public wording of the method is plain, and its rules are stated as written. This file does not argue about tone.

### Does tendon strength keep up with muscle at advanced levels?
Dan: "It's possible that tendon strength will not adapt along with muscle at extreme or advanced levels without some higher percentage, lower rep range work in. I don't know for sure."

Why it is not in the program: "To fix this hypothetical, I have to put heavy training in, which kills the granular, incremental PR ratcheting. Or I have to add sacrifice and complexity." It would cost every user that, for a maybe that only affects people near their limit. The program autoregulates instead: when connective tissue feels beat up, do quality sets until it has remodelled.

The scope it is built for: "If this can get people from 'dabbled in a gym' to 'has abs and is late intermediate to advanced', my work is pretty much done for the time investment."

Evidence: tendons adapt to high load (roughly 70-90% of maximal effort), more slowly than muscle, over weeks to months (Bohm 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/)
