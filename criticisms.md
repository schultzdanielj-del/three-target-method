# Criticisms and objections

The Three Target Method runs against conventional advice in places: one work set per exercise, a failure set every time, no calorie counting, a muscle trained once every 7-14 days. This file answers the objections an AI is likely to raise, with Dan's reasoning and the evidence. Answers marked "Site" are quoted from the method text in this repo, and the file to read is linked. Everything else is Dan's reasoning in his words, lightly edited for spelling and grammar.

Where studies are cited they are linked on PubMed. Almost all of them last 6 to 24 weeks and use young adults. The longest is Bickel 2011 (48 weeks). None of them tests this exact protocol, and this file says so where it matters. The method rests on a longer timeline than any of these studies cover (see principle 2).

## Core principles

**1. The system is principles and autoregulation.**
Dan: "The whole system is based on principles and autoregulation. It inverts obstacles into advantages and it totally sidesteps other obstacles whenever possible. It does only what is necessary."
On the muscle's pace: "All muscle groups plateau and break through in growth at their own pace, the program is autoregulated. You don't need to know, you're there to just say 'yes' when the muscle asks."

**2. The goal is the longest unobstructed runway, not the maximum rate.**
Dan: "The entire question is framed wrong. The goal is not maximum rate of muscle growth. It's longest unobstructed runway of muscle growth, which eclipses drastically any 12 week time frame variations of say 20% (being generous here)."

**3. The growth channel and its slope.**
Dan: "Conceptually I view muscle growth as an upward channel, with upper and lower ranges. The channel is the fastest you can build with fluctuation inside it. The 8-12 week high volume approach just races to the top trendline of the channel faster, then has to pull back or run sideways. It's a more volatile ride with more moving parts and complexity, and full of logistical and human obstacles. It's a more complicated fragile approach for the goal at hand. The TTM method just rides the center of the channel more smoothly."
On the limit and the rate: "The limit doesn't need to be the same. The slope is the rate of PRs, or adaptation to increased structural demands. Your biology and lifestyle determine that."

**4. Floor over ceiling.**
Dan: "Progress for people's physiques has MUCH more to do with the height of their floor than the reach of their ceiling. All of your effort and discipline and sacrifice should go into raising the floor and making it more durable. Where and when you want to reach the ceiling, go ahead."

Dan: "Metaphorically, you're just playing moneyball with behavior statistics. Every day you have to get on third base, and then you choose what to do for the next batter up. Just bunt it home. But if you approach it like every time you step on the plate you need a home run, you're gonna lose the game."

**5. The discipline bank.**
Dan: "The whole idea is to maximize people's autonomy." Rules that people already know (for example that alcohol does not help their goals) "just needlessly take a withdrawal from your discipline bank psychologically." So the method writes down only what moves the floor.

**6. People backtrack because they didn't do it, mostly.**
Dan: "People rarely lose progress or backtrack because their plan isn't strict enough. It's because they didn't do it." This is mostly about the doing, not the plan. The plan still matters at the edges: in a 12-month behavioural programme, hunger and deprivation predicted lapses (Forman 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28281136/). That is why the method keeps hunger and sacrifice low.

**7. Readiness: two groups.**
Dan: "How objectively difficult something is has zero bearing on a proportion of someone's adherence. They're simply just not ready or willing at the time." For people who are not ready, difficulty does not matter and nothing the system does will make them do it. For people who are ready, lower sacrifice is what lets them keep going for years. The system's goals are to "minimize overhead and sacrifice and maximize results", which is not the same as making someone feel good about exercise.

**8. Eating rules are for not noticing a deficit.**
Dan: "Most of the rules around eating are just so you mentally and biologically don't notice a deficit as much."

**9. The question is the whole journey, and how many obstacles are on the road.**
Dan: "The question is: what will get me through the whole journey with the least sacrifice possible?" The studies mostly describe the first few months of adaptation, which is about 3 to 5% of a training life by arithmetic. For the rest, Dan's argument is that a high-volume approach is likely to get stuck and lose progress time more often, "for a million reasons": you need deloads if you keep adding volume, you need other phases, and you get dinged by the sheer hours under the bar. Obstacles come one after another. TTM is "a slow train that can't be stopped."
Evidence: this is Dan's argument and it is untested over years. The pieces that are measured are the time (about 64 to 83 hours a year against 255 to 758) and the injury exposure at a given hourly rate (see the entry on the research in context). In one 12 week trial of trained men, the group doing 10 sets per exercise lost lean mass between week 6 and week 12 (Hackett 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184/), one small data point. No trial is long enough to count stalls or deloads.

## Training

### Isn't one work set per exercise (about 2 hard sets per muscle per workout) far below the 10-20 weekly sets research favours?
Dan: "The question we are answering is not what the most muscle growth you can get is. It's what the least sacrifice is that still gets muscle growth, all the way up to an advanced level." And that undersells it: "Because it is unencumbered by plateaus and runs indefinitely, your absolute muscle growth is likely higher."

Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md)): "The benefit you might get from that optimization costs you double or triple the time spent in the gym." Also "Why only one PR set?": "Will this eventually plateau you? Possibly, but it'll happen well past the point where you're in the best shape of your life."

Dan: "I'm not convinced that more volume is better beyond a shorter time frame that doesn't encompass the required timespan to achieve maximum natural muscle mass." See principles 2 and 3.

Dan: "You will also sacrifice much more, so if you are measuring results per unit of sacrifice, you will likely still underscore compared to the TTM system."

High volume costs double or triple the time. To match TTM on results per unit of sacrifice, it would have to deliver double or triple the results, and no study shows that over the long term.

Dan on the count: "It's 2 direct working hard sets + don't forget all of the other movements that hit the muscle group on the side. E.g., triceps getting hit on pressing or biceps on pulling." and "There is plenty of overlap on non-targeted exercises to bridge most of that gap."

Evidence, stated plainly: the dose-response research says more weekly sets give more growth, with diminishing returns (Schoenfeld 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/; Krieger 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300012/; Pelland 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). In trained men, 1 set per exercise gave less size than 3 or 5 sets over 8 weeks, with equal strength (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/). Counting indirect sets at half a set, as Pelland 2026 does, the sample plans in the protocol work out to roughly 1 to 4.5 weekly sets per muscle depending on the plan and how often a letter repeats. That is below the 4-set weekly minimum suggested by Iversen 2021 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34125411/) for most muscles. So overlap narrows the gap but does not close it on paper. The method's position is that the studies span weeks and the goal spans years.

### What does the volume research actually say once you look at the studies in context?
Dan: "my argument is that the very idea of focusing on short term results is the key fragility of the entire science side of things." And: "How fast we go from zero to cruise speed has no bearing on long term muscle growth. It's like saying high volume burns up all the gas in your tank faster. But does your car end up further at the end of the road trip?" Dan frames the research as measuring only the benefit side. It does not price the time, the nutrition demands, or how long progress lasts.

What the studies show, in numbers:
- Higher weekly volume grew muscle more, with diminishing returns. Schoenfeld 2017 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27433992/) found about +0.37% muscle size per extra weekly set, and about 3.9 percentage points more gain for higher versus lower volume (a trend, P=0.074). In an 8 week trial of 34 trained men doing 1, 3 or 5 sets per exercise (6-9, 18-27 and 30-45 weekly sets), mid-thigh thickness rose 3.4% versus 12.5% for 1 versus 5 sets (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30153194/).
- TTM sits below every studied arm. Its roughly 1.3-2 weekly sets per muscle, with each muscle trained less than once a week, falls under the lowest arm of any trial found. The closest arms were untrained people and lasted 12 weeks or less. Nothing at TTM's dose has been tested in trained people over years, so the research cannot say what happens there.
- The "low volume" in the studies is not TTM's volume. The lowest arm in the 8 week trial was 6-9 weekly sets, several times TTM's.
- Failure is not the same set to set. In the trial above, failure was instructed and not verified. Across the pooled studies only about 30% clearly defined momentary failure (Pelland 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/). Dan: "failure at the 14th set of the workout is nowhere near what the muscle can actually do fresh." Reps fell 15-55% across 5 sets with short rest (Ratamess 2007), and no study tested 10 or more consecutive failure sets. Late failure sets probably carry less stimulus, but no study gives a number.
- Some of the early gain is not tissue. Early gains in people new to a stimulus include swelling (Damas 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26280652/). A review of the confound estimated roughly 10-20% of the volume advantage could be non-tissue (swelling, glycogen, water), with low-to-moderate confidence. That is an estimate from arithmetic, not a published figure. In trained men swelling was gone within 24 hours.
- Short trials cannot show the long run. No volume trial found runs 12 months or longer. The only 6 month trial used untrained people.

What it costs, in numbers (modeled, not measured):
- Time. TTM takes about 1.2-1.6 hours a week. Conventional training at 10, 15, 20 and 30 sets per muscle takes about 4.9, 7.3, 9.7 and 14.6 hours a week, which is 3 to 12 times TTM.
- Nutrition. The benefit of carbohydrate for performance was small in sessions under 45 minutes (SMD 0.23, not significant) and larger over 45 minutes (SMD 1.02) (King 2022, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9584980/). Dan: all high volume sessions run over 45 minutes by a mile. TTM sessions run 35-45 minutes, and Mode 1 has no carbohydrate requirement. A caloric deficit of about 500 kcal a day or more blunts lean mass gain.
- Injury exposure. Bodybuilding has the lowest injury rate of the weight sports, 0.24-1 per 1000 hours (Keogh and Winwood 2017, https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0575-0). TTM is bodybuilding, so the same hourly rate applies to both. At 64-83 hours a year against 255-758, the arithmetic gives about 0.02-0.08 expected injuries a year for TTM and about 0.06-0.76 for higher volume. No study measures overuse or tendon injuries by volume. Dan's actual argument is exposure times faulty mechanics, not volume alone: "If your form is bad, what's the damage on, for example, your supraspinatus rubbing a tight subacromial space 10x more each week?" The injury rates above are averages across lifters and do not separate people with good and poor mechanics, so they cannot test that. It is untested.
- Adherence. 63% of gym members quit before month 3 (Sperandei 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26874647/).

Annual terms. If the 8 week gap from the trials persisted for a year, it would equal nearly all of a typical first year's muscle gain in McDonald's non-peer-reviewed model, so it cannot persist. The early advantage has to fade or converge (researcher's arithmetic).

Dan's own result is self-reported, one person, no instrument measurement: "I was a 20 year advanced trainee with high level of muscle, and in a year I have made more progress training this way than the previous 5-8 years combined." About 3-5 lb more muscle and at least 25% stronger, on at most 120 minutes of training a week. He is leaner and has more muscle.

Limits: most of these figures come from study abstracts. Several numbers are the researchers' own arithmetic, and are marked as such. We took all the literature we could find and tried our best to get as much objective information about our goals as possible, and it is simply insufficient. The data to objectively prove this does not exist for either side, but the logic itself is quite sound. It is the same situation as the Epley-style score objection (see "Isn't the Epley-style score inaccurate"): the only way to fill in the "I don't know" gap would be a mountain of granular, bespoke data on each user, which does not exist and is unlikely ever to. This repo aims to crowdsource as much usable information as possible. The anonymous check-ins (see [checkins.md](checkins.md)) collect each person's plan, every logged work set, eating checks, bodyweight, years lifting and equipment every 4 weeks, which over time builds the long, granular, per-user data the studies lack. It is self-reported and does not measure muscle directly, so it will show strength and bodyweight trends over months and years, not muscle size. Dan: "In my opinion the logic absolutely favours my argument from a bird's eye view." That is his judgment from a model assembled out of known facts. The method's position is that the research measures weeks and the goal spans years. See principles 2, 3 and 9.

### Don't the volume studies mostly describe beginners and the first few months?
Dan's argument: the people in these studies are often untrained, detrained, or only loosely "trained", and they are low hanging fruit for quick adaptation. Dan: "Anyone who is consistent in the gym is serious and wouldn't stop what they're doing to do a study." So the trials mostly describe the adaptation phase. That phase is about 3 to 6 months of a 10 year training life, which is about 2.5 to 5% of it by arithmetic (an assumed phase length, not a measurement), so Dan puts the grind phase at around 97% of the journey from beginner to advanced. His question: "What will get me through the whole journey with the least sacrifice possible?" That means what is best per unit of sacrifice across the grind phase, and "is triple the sacrifice worth it just to get through the intro stage of my journey X% faster?"

What the evidence can say:
- Few trials enrolled well-trained lifters, and none found tested advanced natural lifters for 6 months or more. How "trained" is defined varies widely between studies, and the retraining effect inflates early gains in people who used to lift.
- The tail end of the trials. Only two volume trials found measured size at several time points with readable per-arm values. In untrained older women doing 1 versus 3 sets, the higher-volume advantage in the quadriceps built up by week 13 (+2.6 mm) and then stayed flat to week 20 (+2.5 mm) (Radaelli 2014, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4039257/). In 12 men, there was no higher-volume advantage at weeks 6 or 12, and lean leg mass in the 10-sets-per-exercise group fell 1.3 kg from week 6 to 12 (Hackett 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184/). Across both, the advantage was constant or shrinking after the early phase, never widening. That rests on 20 and 12 people with noisy measures. In another study (Haun 2018) the first 3 weeks gave about 5 times the water-adjusted lean mass gain of the next 3 weeks. A strength-athlete study (abstract only) found 50% more volume for weeks 11-17 gave no extra growth.
- In the model, if the early advantage of higher volume lasted all year, the 10-30 set programs would produce about 2.6 to 3.6 times TTM's gain for about 3 to 12 times the hours. If the advantage fades once the stimulus is familiar, TTM wins per hour by a wide margin. This is modeled, not measured. See the entry on the research in context.
- The research does not test the grind phase. TTM's case for it rests on the mechanism argument and on the client record, which is self-reported.

### Wouldn't more volume plus deloads beat steady progress?
Dan's hypothesis, untested: once past the beginner stage, the job is to be there to say yes when the body asks to build muscle, and the limit is likely connective tissue remodelling, which takes time. High volume races up to that wall, then needs a deload or easier phase, waits, and hits it again, with more sacrifice and more mess. Dan: "It's like a traffic jam. If all the cars just moved the same speed, no stop/start. But people race up to close the gap, then hit the brakes. That's what I think is happening with the high volume stuff. The studies are all just going 'see, if you stomp the gas you go faster', but none of them are long enough to see where the brakes need to be hit." And: "Every single high volume study is like, there you go! And you ask, okay then what, and they have no answer."

What the research says:
- Tendon adapts more slowly than muscle. One study found no change in muscle size or tendon stiffness for about 2 months despite a roughly 30% strength gain (Kubo 2010). Another found collagen and muscle protein synthesis timing similar (Miller 2005), which cuts against "muscle outgrows tendon". So the connective tissue limit is plausible and not established.
- No volume trial found runs 12 months or longer, so none shows where a high-volume lifter must back off. The maximum recoverable volume used in coaching is a coaching idea, not a measured quantity. The research has no answer to "then what" once volume is maxed out.
- Rep progression at a fixed load grew muscle as much as load progression over 8 weeks in trained people (Plotkin 2022, https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14142). That is short, at 8-12 reps, and it does not test years of PR-driven progress.

### Why take every work set to failure, every workout?
Dan: "There is plenty of science showing minimum intensity thresholds for muscle growth signal." Failure "will eventually be required to put your body in a novel environment you need to adapt to. It's simply a side effect, not the reason."

It also depends on training state: "Plenty of subjects will experience muscle growth for many sessions without hitting true failure, depending on their initial condition. All that is required is a new level of environmental demand. For those who are untrained, failure isn't required. For those who are advanced, the only edge remaining lies in failure. The system doesn't discriminate."

Evidence: meta-analyses find no clear advantage of true failure over stopping short (Refalo 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/; Grgic 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33497853/). In the resistance-trained subgroup of Grgic 2022, hypertrophy favoured failure (effect size 0.15). Hypertrophy improves gradually as sets end closer to failure (Robinson 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/; the authors call it exploratory). With light loads, going to failure grew muscle where the same load stopped short did not (Lasevicius 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/), which fits the 8-30 rep range. Failure is not shown to be required in general, and it costs more fatigue (Refalo 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/).

### Can I train to failure safely on my own (bench, skullcrushers, leg press)?
Dan: "Exercise selection has its own guide and that's covered. Movements you cannot logistically or technically take to failure are not appropriate for the program. That's why back squats and barbell deadlifts are out."

On barbell bench: "That's up to the discretion of the lifter. If they are experienced they can attempt it because there is lower risk of injury vs spine loaded heavy compounds like squat and deadlift. Users can keep the collars off the bar and dump the plates on either side in the case of tail risk. They can use machine, smith, dumbbell, floor press and those would be bench press variations that don't have that risk."

### Why is each muscle trained only once every 7-14 days?
The 14-day limit is the floor. Dan: "There's multiple studies that show minimum stimulus frequency before deconditioning begins."

Evidence: in young adults, one-ninth of the original training dose kept muscle size for 32 weeks (Bickel 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/). A review concludes strength and size, at least in younger people, can be kept for up to 32 weeks with as little as 1 session a week and 1 set per exercise if relative load is maintained (Spiering 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33629972/). Another review finds intensity matters most and volume can be cut a lot (Mujika and Padilla 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10999420/). When volume is equated, frequency does not significantly change hypertrophy (Schoenfeld 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30558493/).

Limits: these are maintenance studies, not growth studies, and they were done mostly on younger adults. Older adults needed more (Bickel 2011; Spiering 2021). When volume is not equated, higher frequency is favoured. No study tests growth on a once-per-7-to-14-days schedule.

### Why no sooner than 7 days?
Dan: "Less PR frequency." Repeat a workout letter sooner and the body has not finished adapting, so PRs come less often. "Beginners can fast track it, but they're just eating their runway up faster. There's no long-term benefit to it."

### Why no deloads or planned breaks?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "When to take a break"): life provides breaks, and "If you are feeling extremely beat up, you can instead perform a 'quality set' that's 75% of your best performance weight for the same reps."

Limit: the one planned-break trial found (Ogasawara 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23053130/, 14 young men, 24 weeks, much higher volume) found similar gains with planned breaks. It supports taking time off, not training less often.

### Why no barbell squat, deadlift or barbell row?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why no squats or barbell deadlifts?"): "Do you want to be a strong squatter or do you want a kickass physique in minimal weekly time without getting hurt? Choose wisely." Dan adds that movements you cannot logistically or technically take to failure are not appropriate for the program.

### Isn't the Epley-style score inaccurate, and where does +1-4% come from?
Site ([protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md), note on the formula): it is not as accurate above 10 reps, "But it's the most straightforward, practical way to objectively measure a muscle improving". On pace, [faq/training.md](faq/training.md), Check 1: "One PR per muscle group per month is still a good pace." The +1-4% figure is a rule of thumb. The site does not cite a source for it.

### Doesn't the score distort PRs at high reps?
Dan: "I don't see any problems here with both of those answers being true." Both answers stand:
1. The rules already cap it. Big leg movements add weight above 15 reps, and small muscles add reps up to 25+, so very high-rep PRs only happen on small muscles.
2. A new demand is a new demand and the body adapts either way. Over a long runway extra reps turn into extra weight, and that takes muscle.

### Why a high rep range (8-30)?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why is the work set rep range so high?"; [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md)): reps on big compound leg movements become too taxing to perform accurately above 15 reps, so add weight instead.
Evidence: hypertrophy is similar from low to high loads when sets go to failure (Schoenfeld 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/; Morton 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27174923/). Heavier loads gave larger strength gains in those studies.

### Never changing the plan when stalled looks dogmatic. What if all six checks pass?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Making Changes: Don't."): go through the six checks in order before changing anything.

Dan: "Completely depends on their situation." He adds: "I guarantee you the percentage of people who have done everything exactly right and aren't seeing ANY progress in 3 months straight are the people who are pretty jacked and close to their natural limit." and "Most people won't be patient enough to even get close to that situation." If they are not advanced, "there's obviously a huge glaring blind spot in what they're doing", and if they are advanced "they have to decide if they want to bulk etc." For the long-term answer see "What happens once I'm lean?" under Whole system. Anything past that is a coach's call.

### Won't the same movements for years go stale?
Dan: "You're going to have to define what going stale means and what you mean by overuse injuries." On staleness: "Being fully adapted to a movement is the ideal environment to use it to build muscle. That means muscle growth is mandatory." In other words, once skill and coordination on a movement are maxed out, the only way left to beat the old number is more muscle, so a PR on a mastered movement is a clean growth signal. The method keeps 2 movements per muscle for coverage of the muscle.

Dan on his own removals: "Now, once in a blue moon I had to take a movement out. Single arm pulldowns, sissy squats sometimes. But those were all because there was too much overlap and it was more like 3 hard sets or more. Barbell lunge, sissy squat, but also the roman chair situps were hitting rectus femoris hard. The single arm pulldowns was just too many back sets combined with two hand pulldowns, chest supported rows, single arm DB rows. Also the movement was cheatable. Not a moneymaker." And: "Yeah, but it was overlap AND the movement itself just not being biomechanically good for my body." That fits the existing rule: movements are chosen by trial and error for your moneymakers, with no joint pain, and a movement that is bad for your body or cheatable gets swapped ([protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md), [faq/training.md](faq/training.md)). In years, he removed a movement rarely, and each time overlap had pushed a muscle to 3 or more hard sets.

Evidence, stated plainly: no study follows the same movements for years. Over 9 to 12 weeks, groups that kept fixed exercises gained about the same whole-muscle size and 1RM as groups that varied them (Fonseca 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24832974/; Baz-Valle 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31881066/, 21 trained men; Costa 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35481889/). Heavy random rotation did not beat fixed exercises for growth (Baz-Valle 2019), and a systematic review of 8 studies in young men concludes that excessive, random variation may compromise gains while some systematic variation may help regional growth (Kassiano 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/). Excessive variation is therefore the risk, and the method has none. The case against a single movement per muscle: in 9 to 12 weeks the fixed groups missed some quadriceps heads (Fonseca 2014) and some sites along the thigh and arm (Costa 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33440446/). That supports more than one movement per muscle for coverage. No study compared 1, 2 and 3 movements, so "2 movements per muscle" is supported in direction but not tested as a number. These are small studies in young men, 8 to 12 weeks.

### Doesn't the same movements for years cause overuse injuries?
Dan: "There's no reason to get an overuse injury on the right muscle group. Sometimes the connective tissue will feel beat up at more advanced levels. That's your limiting factor. You'll be doing more quality sets and your performance will stagnate until it's remodelled and ready to handle more stress. It might take weeks. That's how your body works. That's not the same as an overuse injury. How would you get overuse doing the biomechanically correct movement for your body to technical only failure, like two times in 10 days?"

Dan on form: "Most people lift badly and have terrible form. For them, there are zero training plans that will give them results beyond a hard ceiling because of that. Imagine them doing 20 work sets hard, you think that's not going to give them overuse issues?" That is why the method is for people who already know how to perform the lifts generally correctly (see "Beginners" under Who it's for). Poor form caps results on any plan, and a high-volume plan multiplies the load on the same bad pattern. This plan has about 2 failure sets per muscle every 7 to 14 days.

Dan: "Many people cannot tolerate taking what the body has to give. They expect a PR at all times for emotional reasons and will violate the program to do so. That's not my problem. It's not the program."

Dan: "If they get overuse, it is the wrong movement, the wrong form, and cheating on executing. Every time."

Evidence, stated plainly: tendon and connective tissue adapt more slowly than muscle. In one small study, tendon stiffness and muscle size did not change until after 2 months of training, while strength rose sooner (Kubo 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996769/, 8 people). A meta-analysis of 27 studies finds tendons adapt to loading over weeks to months, mostly in response to high loads (Bohm 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/). Adult Achilles tendon core is hardly renewed at all after growth (Heinemeier 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23401563/). A physiology review says chronic loading probably makes tendon more load resistant, that tendon overuse injury involves altered collagen and poor collagen breakdown, and that the answer is adjusted loading, not no loading (Kjaer 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15044685/). So "connective tissue lags muscle and needs weeks to months" is supported. Limits: no study times how long tendon takes to recover from a hard run of training, none ties tendon lag to a PR plateau in lifters, and none tests a lighter quality set while it catches up. "Never an overuse injury" cannot be shown from this evidence; the doctor line in "Injuries, conditions, medication, pregnancy, under 18" still applies to pain, and the no joint pain rule stays.

### Is a PR really the growth signal ("tap, tap, BANG")?
Dan: failure and PRs are about adaptation to a new demand. "the slope is the rate of PRs, or adaptation to increased structural demands." Every PR is a new level of environmental demand the body has not met before (see principle 3 and the failure answer).
Limit: no study tests beating the previous number as the driver of growth. The dose-response research points to weekly hard sets. The method treats the PR as the measurable form of increased demand.

### Aren't sissy squats and ATG split squats risky for the knees?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why the ATG split squats and sissy squats?"; the "no joint pain" rule in [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md)). Individual knee history falls under [disclaimer.md](disclaimer.md) and the one-line doctor check in "Injuries, conditions, medication" below.

### Doesn't a lifting-only plan skip cardio?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Why no cardio?"): "Cardio for health is great, add it if you like. But not instead of lifting."
Dan: no structured cardio is required. "You're much better off just adding weekly steps than cardio sessions. The protocol is light enough on recovery that you can add cardio if you want to, or HIIT or whatever, but that will aid fitness primarily."

### The mobility drills are named but not described.
Dan: "Your mobility is the bounding box around the exercises that will work for you. If your box is too small, you cannot perform the program correctly." And: "Once your mobility bounding box allows movements in, many of those movements themselves will increase and maintain mobility on their own." See [faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Mobility drills".

### Calves, forearms and traps are not in the muscle group list.
Dan: "If you want jacked calves, train them." Calves are optional and the person's choice.

### Light lower back work contradicts "everything to failure".
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Heavy spine-loaded movements"): the exception is stated outright, light work only, 2-4 sets of 12-20 reps, nowhere near failure.

### What about missed sessions and travel?
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md)): "Don't do 3 in a row regularly, but catching up is fine." On the road, pick whatever you have access to: you won't set PRs but you can still train to failure.

## Eating

Dan's principle for all of it: "Most of the rules around eating are just so you mentally and biologically don't notice a deficit as much."

Dan's explanation: "You need a caloric deficit. But it can't be aggressively chronic, and it can't be malnutrition. So why not let the normal ebb and flow of life circumstances regulate the deficit/refeeds, and then just make the mandatory foods handle the malnutrition."

"Making the best choice when you are not backed into a corner with restriction AND already jam packed with satiating important foods has a significantly higher chance of happening. Most people don't need to eat perfect, just bring their floor up."

### Without calorie counting, how do you know there is a deficit?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md), "I want to count calories" and "Precision Nutrition Measurements"): "Calories and macros are the ultimate decider of weight. But counting them daily doesn't work in practice." Hand measurements are "sufficiently accurate at a fraction of the time and focus required."
If fat loss stalls, Dan's answer is: "add steps."

### Mode 2 ("whatever you want") can erase the deficit.
Dan: "Are other nutrition strategies exempt from this concern?" and "Correct. There is no single diet strategy that eliminates poor human behavior. This one simply sets them up for the best chance of success."
Evidence: in a 1-year trial of four named diets, weight loss followed self-reported adherence, not diet type (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/). A network meta-analysis found minimal differences between named diets (Johnston 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25182101/). Limit: adherence in Dansinger was self-reported, so people who lost weight may have reported better adherence. "Within reason" is not defined in numbers. The person decides it.

### Mode 1 is very low calorie.
Dan: "It's not for beginners, remember." And: "Mode 1 calories are not all your daily calories." Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "Mode 1 foods are the minimum, not the maximum", and the low-energy answer tells the person to add carbs after work. For anyone with a medical condition or on medication, see "Injuries, conditions, medication" below.

### The same portions for everyone (4 palms, 3 fists, 1-2 thumbs)?
Dan: "It's your mandatory skeleton. The dynamic nature of the diet makes it so that if you're feeling good and not hungry, you can totally just have a mode 1 style dinner as well and then do a 1000+ calorie deficit day if you want. But that's not going to happen every day. Acute temporary calorie fluctuations generally work different than chronic. Acute deficits are powerful, acute surpluses are restorative, chronic of either is bad."
On refeeds: "It's called a 'refeed' or a 'diet break' and now instead of adding a ton of logistical headache and discipline to doing that, it's just built into your normal behavior. It's autoregulated."

Evidence: alternating 2-week diet blocks with 2-week energy-balance blocks gave more fat loss than continuous dieting in men with obesity (MATADOR, Byrne 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/), but that trial was small, per-protocol, and took 30 weeks versus 16. In resistance-trained adults (ICECAP, Peos 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/) there was no difference in fat mass or fat-free mass, but the intermittent group reported lower hunger and desire to eat. A 1-year trial found a 5:2 pattern did no better and no worse than continuous dieting (Headland 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30470804/). No study tested single-day deficits and appetite-driven refeeds, and the "chronic surplus is bad" half is untested.

### Does 0.7 g/lb protein hold up?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "0.7g/lb bodyweight is sufficient for muscle building." Your Mode 1 foods hit this mark regardless of what you eat after. Dan: "You'll hit 0.8g/lb bodyweight without even having to count it."

### Isn't the diet poor in variety and quality, with unconstrained Mode 2?
Dan: this is the floor over ceiling principle (principle 4). Effort and discipline go into raising the floor and making it durable. "Where and when you want to reach the ceiling, go ahead."

Dan's suggested start to Mode 2 is a piece of fruit: fiber and water take the edge off before the free meal, it tops up liver glycogen after Mode 1, and it shows you can still enjoy clean foods in Mode 2. A suggestion, not a rule.

### Where does alcohol fit?
Dan: "The best amount is none but it's your decision." The method does not write it into the rules (principle 5).

### Can you lose fat and build muscle at once?
Dan on results: about 12 people have run it, and their PRs kept coming during the loss: "Yes, insanely." See "Proof" under Whole system.

### The "pain point", insulin and 2-3 week claims.
Dan: "Pain point isn't biological only. It encompasses all factors leading to adherence issues."
There is no hard wall at 2-3 weeks. No study sets that limit. The closest is appetite: hunger rose in the first days and eased within about 3 weeks while ketotic in a very-low-energy diet study (Nymo 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28439092/), and each kg lost raised intake by about 100 kcal/day over 52 weeks in another (Polidori 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804272/). Trials show people can hold a reduced-calorie diet for months with support (Franz 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17904936/). The honest form of the claim is that appetite and adherence pressure builds, and that breaks help. For insulin, see the next entry.

### Why no carbs in Mode 1?
Mode 1 foods keep you full the longest per calorie. Insulin comes second. Dan: "Better insulin sensitivity lets you be okay not eating for longer." and "Better insulin sensitivity helps your hunger regulate better."
Fat loss comes from the deficit. Dan: "Insulin management alone doesn't lose weight. It just makes you not notice caloric deficits nearly as much when it's working right." No study is cited here for the insulin claims.

### Can I do a failure set on Mode 1 with no carbs?
Dan: "One single set, are you kidding me? If that's the case you have insulin issues, not storing glycogen from yesterday properly. That's all the more reason to stick to Mode 1 until it feels better." The "insulin issues" inference is Dan's reasoning.

Evidence, stated plainly: muscle glycogen use follows how much work is done. In a meta-analysis of 20 studies, depletion was larger with more sets and longer sessions (Hamidvand 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41420384/). Trained men on a 39-minute, three-exercise leg session lost about 27% of vastus lateralis glycogen (Haff 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10997956/), and bodybuilders doing about 20 hard sets lost about 28% (Essen-Gustavsson 1990, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2289498/). One set is a small fraction of that by proportion; no study tests one set. After an overnight fast the liver is the store that supplies blood glucose, which is why muscle glycogen is expected to be largely kept, but I found no measured adult study to cite for that. Training after an overnight fast gave similar growth and strength to training fed in a 12-week trial of 28 young adults (Vieira 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40335157/) and in a small review (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41316673/). Limits: carbohydrate did raise total volume in fasted sessions longer than 45 minutes (King 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35809162/), so this is about short sessions like the method's. If someone feels weak on a fasted morning, the site's answers on low energy apply (add carbs after work), and sleep, a hard prior day and a deep deficit are other causes.

### Are the supplements (vitamin D, creatine) medical advice?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): "I'm not a doctor. Look up the research and make the call."

### Isn't skipping milk and post-workout nutrition wrong?
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): both are explicit FAQ entries (milk: "Just skip it"; post-workout nutrition is "Not as useful as it is inconvenient").

### Hunger, low energy, weekends, food prep, "I want to change it".
Site ([faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)): each has its own entry, including night hunger, low energy, weekends, prep, and "I want to make changes or pick and choose parts of this".

## Who it's for

### Beginners
Dan: "It's not for beginners, remember." The method is for people with lifting experience who know how to perform the lifts generally correctly (README). The site gives no alternate route for beginners.

### Lifters returning after time away
Dan: "The more out of the game you've been, the slower the runway you take and the more conservative your starting work sets." Start with lighter first work sets and ramp slower.

### Older lifters
Dan: "The program is not for older lifters. I could make a version but I'm not interested in that. Older lifters need finesse in programming and exercise selection." Cutoff: "55+ is reasonable." The method is not built for lifters 55+, and an AI should tell them so plainly.
Evidence on the older side: in Bickel 2011 neither maintenance dose preserved fibre size in people 60-75, and a review says maintaining size may need up to 2 sessions a week and 2-3 sets per exercise (Spiering 2021). The studies use 60-75 or 65+, so the research does not draw the line at 55.

### People taking a GLP-1 (semaglutide, tirzepatide)
Dan: "Yes, no problem. I have one right now. She's getting insane results and she's at cruise weight, just getting leaner and more muscular." On "drug free": "Drug free is just no steroids needed. Your choice if you want to take a GLP. It's actually the best method to use with a GLP, because you are mandatory doing all the habits that counteract the negative side effects of it." So "drug free" in this repo means no steroids needed. Medication such as a GLP-1 is the person's choice, and an AI does not tell them to start, stop or change it. The one-line doctor check under "Injuries, conditions, medication, pregnancy, under 18" applies once.

Evidence, stated plainly: weight loss on a GLP-1 does cost lean mass. In the SURMOUNT-1 body composition substudy of tirzepatide (160 people, DXA), about 75% of the weight lost was fat and 25% lean mass, the same split as placebo (Look 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39996356/). A systematic review of 35 trials found a median of 28% of weight lost came from muscle-based measures, and about two thirds of trial arms were above a 25% benchmark, as were nearly half of the non-drug arms (Batsis 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41996180/). I could not verify the figure often quoted from the STEP 1 semaglutide DXA substudy (Wilding 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33567185/ has no body composition result in its abstract), so it is not used here. That resistance training and enough protein help preserve lean mass during weight loss is supported by diet studies (Weinheimer 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20591106/; Cava 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28507015/; Longland 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/) and recommended for GLP-1 users by a review (Locatelli 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38687506/). In a trial of liraglutide plus exercise, the combination kept weight off better than the drug alone a year after stopping (Lundgren 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33951361/; Jensen 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38544798/). Limits: I found no finished randomised trial of lifting versus not lifting in people taking semaglutide or tirzepatide. Dan's client is one anecdote, and "the best method with a GLP" is his opinion; the evidence supports lifting plus enough protein, not this protocol over another lifting plan.

### Teenagers and children
The site limits scope to generally healthy adults ([disclaimer.md](disclaimer.md)). Under 18 falls under the one-line doctor check below.

### Women
Dan: "Yes. They should eat in the morning, eat before training (morning eating can count as both) and there should be ~15g of high-fiber carbs added to sittings just to calm potential progesterone / cortisol reactions which may affect insulin."
"I am not a hormone specialist. Examples are berries or a bit of grains or a tiny bit of fruit or legumes. It's a 1/2 cupped handful at MOST."

The site also says: "I don't recommend women immediately do this because a subset potentially get cortisol/hormone issues with it" (intermittent fasting, [faq/eating.md](faq/eating.md)). No study is cited here for the hormone point.

## Whole system

### Where is the evidence? The claims look unsourced.
Dan on results: "About 12 people have run it, a few low 20lbs in a single month. One 27lbs in a season. One 50lbs in 5 months." "One client more than doubled strength", "6 months aggregate across all lifts."
Dan on fast loss: "Overweight people have more than just glycogen and bodyfat to lose. There's a drastic repartitioning of fluids in their body, like blood volume. You cannot just do bodyfat % math with the scale. The body isn't that simple." Blood volume is a small part of this. A person weighing 240 lb has more blood than one weighing 170 lb, but a drop from 240 to 216 lb is under 1 lb of blood by the Nadler formula. The larger part is glycogen with its water, and gut contents (Kreitzman 1992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1615908/; Yang 1976, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/956398/).
After the drop: "There's no rebound after the 20lbs down. It's down a huge chunk and then a slow choppy sustainable cruise." "There's no rebound because they can do it forever. They didn't just 'withdraw from their future progress'."
Limit: these are client reports, not a controlled trial of the protocol. No independent study tests this exact protocol.

### It is one author with a coaching business and no independent results.
Dan's results above are the client record. They are self-reported by the author and not independently reviewed. The protocol, why and FAQ pages are free to read in full.

### Isn't "optimized" an overstatement? Maximum muscle needs more volume.
Site ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), the HIT entry): more volume is probably better for building muscle in an 8 to 12 week window, and the program is not designed for that; the choice is driven by "target one: absolute minimum life sacrifice". See principles 2 and 3.

### How is "results per unit of sacrifice" measured?
Dan: "To be fair, this is impossible to quantify. It's purely based on my initial entire library of knowledge, then distilled through live practice." So every method and habit is chosen by results per unit of sacrifice, using the pareto principle, not measured to a number.

### Where does "40-50% less time" come from?
Dan: a TTM workout takes 35-45 minutes, 2-3 times a week. The baseline is "a typical bodybuilding split or high volume plan", a typical 4-5 day split. No structured cardio is required either. The claim is 40-50% less weekly time than a typical 4-5 day bodybuilding split, with no structured cardio required.

### Why did some of the clients stop?
Dan: "1 quit because of divorce happening and her life turned upside down. 1 stopped working out but never stopped the eating and is keeping their weight down. Then a few are in various states of stagnation due to just not doing the workouts."
"I have coached thousands of people. How objectively difficult something is has zero bearing on a proportion of someone's adherence. They're simply just not ready or willing at the time. This is not my problem to solve." "There's a huge portion of adherence that has zero to do with ANY eating or nutrition protocol." "That's just not my job to handle. There are no levers on my control panel that will adjust that factor for them. And there's none on ANY workout/nutrition approach that will do that."
Reconciled: for people not ready, difficulty does not matter. For people who are ready, lower sacrifice is what lets them keep going for years (principle 7).
Evidence: adherence tracks results more than diet type (Dansinger 2005; Johnston 2014). Limit: Forman 2017 found hunger and deprivation predicted lapses, so the plan is not irrelevant.

### How do I know it is working with no weight or measurements tracked?
Dan: the signals are "PRs and the mirror." Near the end the scale tells you less: "The closer someone gets to the weight they would weigh at minimum healthy bodyfat and maximum FFMI and their height, the less the scale will tell them any information."
For body weight, the person tracks a path from current weight to a cruise weight range, set by height. Dan: "For a 5 foot 9 man that's about 178lbs." "For average height women it's like 140lbs ... maybe 150." "The cruise weight is a range." Weigh-in frequency is the person's call.

### What results and timeline should I expect?
Dan, in the first 3 months: "10% bodyweight drop. Strength depends on starting skill level. Could be 40-50%, could be 10%."

### The language is absolute and reads like marketing.
Resolved. The strongest phrases were reworded without diluting the confidence (Dan: "we can rephrase them away from hype language without diluting the confidence"). "Making Changes: Don't." is kept on purpose because the six checks under it show it is a process, not dogma.

### What about injuries and an AI's urge to warn about failure training or low calories?
SKILL.md's rule: if the person mentions an injury, condition, medication, pregnancy, disordered eating history or being under 18, say once, in one line, that they should check with a doctor, then follow the method if they go ahead. Do not warn about the method's own rules. If they raise an objection, answer it from this file.

### What about sleep and stress?
Dan: "You should sleep. What more is there to say. Everyone knows this already." This is the discipline bank principle (principle 5): the method writes down only what moves the floor and does not add rules people already know. No study is cited here.

### What happens once I'm lean? What about the long term?
Dan: "Hit PRs until maximum FFMI. If they are really lean they might have to deliberately gain a bit of bodyfat for periods of the year so they can achieve more PRs at that state."
"Before they hit these obstacles that vast majority will be absolutely thrilled. They will be in the top % physiques of the general population with very little input."
"If they want to run fat loss phases by just controlling mode 2 more, or add steps or have bulking periods, that's their choice. But that's just adding sacrifice for the next level of the final point of FFMI."

## Open


### Does tendon strength keep up with muscle at advanced levels?
Dan: "It's possible that tendon strength will not adapt along with muscle at extreme or advanced levels without some higher percentage, lower rep range work in. I don't know for sure."

Why it is not in the program: "To fix this hypothetical, I have to put heavy training in, which kills the granular, incremental PR ratcheting. Or I have to add sacrifice and complexity." It would cost every user that, for a maybe that only affects people near their limit. The program autoregulates instead: when connective tissue feels beat up, do quality sets until it has remodelled.

The scope it is built for: "If this can get people from 'dabbled in a gym' to 'has abs and is late intermediate to advanced', my work is pretty much done for the time investment."

Evidence: tendons adapt to high load (roughly 70-90% of maximal effort), more slowly than muscle, over weeks to months (Bohm 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27747846/)
