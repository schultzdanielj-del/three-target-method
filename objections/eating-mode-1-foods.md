# Objections: eating, Mode 1 foods

## Don't protein bars and "sugar alcohols probably don't count" contradict "eat exactly this"?
Dan: "protein bars are a convenience food and to be used when there's no other choice. It's better than nothing but not a daily staple." The list names bars with under 15 g of net carbohydrate, and Dan's earlier answers are that bars are for unique days like travel, and that sugar alcohols probably don't count toward the net carbohydrate figure.

The downside, accurately: sugar alcohols are not calorie free (they provide some calories), and some cause gut discomfort in larger amounts, so "doesn't count" is a simplification (this is general knowledge about sugar alcohols and no study is cited here). The benefit: a person with no other choice still gets a protein portion in, and a rule that has a travel option is easier to keep than one that does not (see the tiers for travel in the eating file). The alternative, no exceptions, costs adherence on the days it is hardest. Bars are not a staple, so the effect on a typical day is small. No study tests bars as a Mode 1 protein source.

## Don't zero-calorie drinks and sweeteners raise appetite, cravings or insulin, and contradict "eat exactly this"?
Dan: "I just don't think it really has a significant impact against the sacrifice it costs to avoid." The allowance is about 30 calories or less, and zero-calorie drinks are fine.

The downside, accurately: the evidence is mixed. In a meta-analysis, replacing sugar with low-calorie sweeteners lowered weight by about 1.35 kg against sugar and by about 1.24 kg against water in 3 comparisons (Rogers 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26365102/), and a later review found 1.06 kg less against sugar and no significant difference against water (Rogers 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33168917/). Randomized trials found no significant BMI effect, while cohort studies link sweetener use to higher BMI and cardiometabolic risk (Azad 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28716847/), which may reflect who drinks them. In 120 healthy adults, saccharin and sucralose impaired glycemic responses over 2 weeks (Suez 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35987213/), while a 17-person crossover found no change in glucose, insulin or insulin sensitivity with sucralose or aspartame (Ahmad 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31697573/). The WHO guideline recommends against non-sugar sweeteners for weight control, conditionally, and a BMJ analysis says the long-term harm evidence is low certainty and mostly observational (WHO 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37256996/; Hedrick 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37813435/).

What the allowance buys: a person who would otherwise give up coffee creamer or diet drinks is far likelier to keep the rest. The alternative, no sweeteners at all, costs adherence, and the evidence that avoiding them helps weight is weak. No study tests them within this structure.

## Why is milk out as a drink when a splash is fine and Greek yogurt is mandatory?
Dan: "how much milk you drink to get 20-25g protein. 2+ cups. that's like 20g sugar all liquid calories." The reasoning is protein per calorie. USDA values per cup: 2% milk has about 8.1 g protein, 12.3 g sugar and 122 kcal, and skim has 8.3 g, 12.5 g and 84 kcal. Plain nonfat Greek yogurt, 170 g, has about 17.3 g protein, 5.5 g sugar and about 100 kcal. So 20 to 25 g of protein from 2% milk is about 2.5 to 3 cups, with roughly 31 to 37 g of sugar and 305 to 366 kcal (the writer's arithmetic), a little more than Dan's estimate. A splash in coffee is a few calories, so it is allowed. What milk buys: some protein, calcium and vitamin D. The alternative, Greek yogurt or a shake made with water, gives the protein with fewer liquid calories.

## Why is a cruciferous vegetable required?
Dan: "nutrient density."

The benefit: it is a way to make sure one vegetable a day is a dense one. On Di Noia's 2014 ranking of 47 fruits and vegetables, cruciferous and leafy greens sit in the top half (watercress 100, kale 49, broccoli 34.9, cauliflower 25) (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24901795/). The ranking is the author's own score, for 17 nutrients per 100 kcal, not an official list. Large cohort data tie more cruciferous intake to lower all-cause mortality (HR 0.78 top versus bottom fifth in 134,796 Chinese adults, Zhang 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21593509/), and an umbrella review found only lower-risk associations, but rates the evidence as suggestive at best and calls for trials (Li 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35352732/).

The downside and the contradiction: one cohort found a weak positive link between glucosinolates and heart disease (HR 1.09), with Brussels sprouts at 1 or more servings a week at 1.16 (Ma 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29988715/). All of this is observational.

The cost of dropping the requirement is small, since it is one item. Cost of the requirement: some people dislike or digest these vegetables badly. The method's answer is to pick another food from the list if it is not eaten. Edge cases such as blood thinners or thyroid conditions are dietitian questions, outside the method.

## Is a thumb of fat too little for fat-soluble vitamins?
Dan: "take your vitamin d with fats then."

The downside, accurately: fat helps absorption. Vitamin D3 peaked 32% higher with a fatty meal than a fat-free meal in 50 older adults after one 50,000 IU dose (Dawson-Hughes 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25441954/). Carotenoids were negligible with a fat-free dressing and rose with 6 g and 28 g of oil in 7 people (Brown 2004, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15277161/). Vitamin K1 and retinyl palmitate rose with fat up to 32 g in 12 women (White 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28814399/). A 7 day trial found 3 g of fat in a meal gave the same vitamin E and carotene response as 36 g (Roodenburg 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10799382/), so results conflict.

In context: no study defines a minimum amount, and the Mode 1 day is not fat free. Fat comes from eggs, fish, dark meat and yogurt, and the thumb is added only when the protein is lean. Mode 2 eats with no fat limit. Dan's view is that people need less fat than they think (opinion). His practical fix is to take the vitamin with a fatty meal.

Alternative: adding fat to every meal costs hundreds of calories (Dan's pitfall). Not found: a study of vitamin K or preformed vitamin A needs in a fat-limited morning.

## Aren't the food lists arbitrary (potato out, squash in, beans and lentils out)?
Dan: "calorie content to glycemic index ratio, generally." That is his answer on the lists as a whole; for beans and lentils his published reason is below.

Dan's other answers on the lists: potato is out because it is a root or tuber, and beans and lentils are out because they are good food but are not primarily a protein source and not a non-starchy vegetable (Dan: "Beans and lentils aren't primarily a protein Source or a non-starchy vegetable"). Squash and carrot are okay. Frozen mixes are fine, "this isn't rocket science we just need to generally hit the mark."

Glycemic index values for individual foods could not be retrieved reliably, so none are given here. The best-supported general finding is that legumes are usually low GI while most potato varieties are high GI (Atkinson 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34258626/). That means GI does not explain the beans and lentils exclusion. The reason there is that they are primarily carbohydrate.

Honest limit: the lists are a judgment about calories, fullness and carbohydrate load for a morning, not a derived formula, and Dan's ratio is stated in general terms.

What the lists buy: fewer decisions and a fixed floor. The alternative is a long permitted list or a calculation, which costs focus every day. Any food not on the lists is still allowed in Mode 2.

## Doesn't leaving out fruit, whole grains and legumes remove foods linked to lower disease risk?
Dan: "you can eat as many of those foods as you like." That is in Mode 2. For a person who feels guilty about Mode 2 foods: "like eat fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein."

The downside is that these foods are linked to lower risk: whole grain, per 90 g a day, to lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.83) (Aune 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27301975/); fruit and vegetables, per 200 g a day (RR 0.90) (Aune 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28338764/); legumes, highest versus lowest intake, HR 0.94 (Zargarzadeh 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36811595/). All observational, with small effects and residual confounding.

In context: they are not left out of the day. They are left out of the morning Mode 1 set because those calories and carbohydrate are what Mode 1 spends on protein, vegetables and fat. Mode 2 is where they go, and Dan says eating them there is encouraged when there is the chance. There are two berry allowances, kept separate. The women's morning change is the one exception to Mode 1 Rule 3 for that purpose: up to a half cupped handful (about 15 g) of high-fiber carbs (see "Women" below). Separately, Dan says anyone can sprinkle berries into Mode 1 if they absolutely need to, and it is not a hill to die on. Berries are high fiber and low calorie. Other fruit is not added to Mode 1, apart from the little fruit in the women's allowance.

Alternative: making them mandatory in Mode 1 raises morning calories and appetite, and his claim that appetite stays lower without carbs in the morning is only partly supported by the evidence. It is mixed.

## Are powders and bars as good as whole foods for protein?
Dan: "I don't disagree with that." Whole foods differ in fullness and leucine content. His rule: any protein powder is fine, any style. Bars are for unique days such as travel, not a daily staple.

The downside, accurately: for whey alone, solid and liquid forms cut intake at 1 hour about equally in 14 men (Akhavan 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20733582/). But in 9 older adults a solid meal replacement gave lower hunger, insulin and ghrelin than a liquid one (Tieken 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17533583/). So whole foods may fill more, and the evidence is small and mixed.

Muscle: soy and animal protein gave the same strength and lean mass in a meta-analysis of 9 studies (Messina 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29722584/), and vegan and omnivore young men matched at 1.6 g/kg gained alike in 12 weeks, though groups were not randomized (Hevia-Larrain 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33599941/).

What allowing powders buys: a way to hit the portions on a worst week or a trip (Dan's tier list). The alternative, whole food only, costs prep and fails on travel days. Not found: a trial of shake versus mixed whole food in a deficit.

## Isn't it expensive (fish, nuts, avocado, protein powder)?
Dan: "That's easy for an agent to figure out. I personally do this." The agent picks cheaper foods from the existing lists; there is no new rule.

Two points on the premise (writer's summary, from Dan's fat answers). Nuts and avocado are not required. A fat thumb is only added if every protein source is ultra lean, which he says is unlikely for most people, and any protein powder style is fine. No price comparison of Mode 1 foods is in the research files; not found.

What it buys: no extra rule for the person to carry. The alternative is a cost-optimized plan written for them, which is the meal plan the method avoids. The cost to the person is time spent choosing, which the agent takes on.
