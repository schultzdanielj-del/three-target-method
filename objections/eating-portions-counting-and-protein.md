# Objections: eating, Portions, counting and protein

## The method says calories decide weight but gives no calorie target, so how can it be evaluated?
Dan: "because calculating the exact calories required to lose weight assumes a few things: 1 - you'll somehow eat exactly that number daily and have no fluctuations in hunger. 2 - that measuring and counting that exact number will be accurate. 3 - that you are accurate that this is the right number. 4 - that doing all of that will still be worth the cost."

The downside, accurately: with no number, the method can only be judged by the weight trend and by whether the rules were followed. What a number would rest on: calorie-need equations are approximate. Mifflin-St Jeor was the most accurate equation, within 10% of measured resting metabolic rate in more people than any other, though the abstract gives no percentage (Frankenfield 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15883556/). A dynamic model puts the time for weight to approach a new level at about a year, so quick rules about weekly loss mislead (Hall 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21872751/). Self-reported intake, the input a counted target needs, is under-reported by about 12 to 38% depending on the method (see the counting entry). What the structure buys: no number to hit, and a weight channel that corrects the plan over weeks. The alternative needs all four of Dan's assumptions to hold. No study tests this method's results.

## Isn't the fat thumb threshold imprecise?
Dan: "no need to be that precise, the benefit gained from accuracy is minimal vs the focus required."

Accurate: a thumb is about 1 tablespoon at most (Dan), and it is only added if every protein source in the day is ultra lean, which is uncommon. Eggs, salmon, dark meat and ground meat already cover it. The rule exists to stop the common pitfall of adding olive oil, avocado and nuts to every meal, which can add hundreds of calories (Dan).

Evidence on precision: hand scales are partly tested. Shinozaki 2025 (1,081 adults, 12,148 meals) found hand counts tracked weighed food at group level (correlations 0.59 to 0.85) with wide per-meal error (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40669561/). A finger-width method put 80% of estimates for geometric foods within 25% of true weight in 67 people (Gibson 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27547392/). Both are different methods from Precision Nutrition's, so they support the idea of useful estimation, not proof of these exact hands.

The benefit: no scale, no app. The cost of precision: logging took 14.6 to 23.2 minutes a day in one program (Harvey 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/). A few grams of fat do not change the result because Mode 2 sets the larger part of the day's energy. Thin: the exact threshold has no evidence behind it. It is a judgment.
