# Objections: eating, alcohol, fat and muscle, carbs and supplements

## Where does alcohol fit?
The method's position is that the best amount of alcohol is none, and that the decision is the person's. The method does not write it into the rules (principle 5).
Alcohol does hit harder on a low-carb, low-calorie day. Alcohol is Mode 2 only, after the Mode 1 foods are eaten (writer's summary).

Muscle: after a heavy session, alcohol at 1.5 g/kg (about 12 drinks) cut muscle protein synthesis by 24% to 37% versus protein alone in 8 men (Parr 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24533082/). That is a very large dose. In men, alcohol raised strength loss at 36 hours (Barnes 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19230764/), but in 13 resistance-trained women it had no effect on torque recovery (Levitt 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28386694/). A signaling study also found an effect in men and none in women (Duplanty 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27135475/).

Hypoglycemia with fasting or low carbohydrate: thin. The only systematic review found is in type 1 diabetes, where 8 of 13 studies linked alcohol to more hypoglycemia (Tetzschner 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29135074/). None was found in healthy dieters.

The benefit is freedom, since no alcohol rule is written (the discipline-bank principle). The alternative, an alcohol ban, adds a rule most people already know. The agent states the downside once and leaves the choice with the person.

## Can you lose fat and build muscle at once?
The method's position is yes: PRs keep coming during the loss. See "Where is the evidence?" under Whole system.
The method does not claim that early PRs reflect muscle and not skill over a short period; no program can make that claim. In Dan's coaching experience this holds for everyone except lean people near their top FFMI. That is coaching experience, not a controlled trial.

The downside: early strength gains are mostly neural. Neural factors drove most of the early gain in 8 weeks of training, and hypertrophy took over after 3 to 5 weeks (Moritani 1979, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/453338/; Sale 1988, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3057313/). So an early PR is not evidence of new muscle. Recomposition has been shown in short studies of young recreational people on high protein (Longland 2016; lean +1.2 kg, fat -4.8 kg over 4 weeks), but no source quantifies it in long-trained lifters. A deficit of 500 kcal a day or more blunts lean mass gain (Murphy 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34623696/).

What the method claims: PRs can keep coming during the loss, which is Dan's own experience over his year of training this way (self-reported). The method does not claim muscle gain from PRs alone.

The alternative, a separate bulk and cut, costs time and a second set of rules. Thin: experience only, no instruments.

## The "pain point", insulin and 2-3 week claims.
The pain point is not only biological. It covers all the factors that lead to adherence problems.
Definition: the pain point is when people start finding it too hard to keep doing, for any reason (emotional, physical, mental), and they stop adhering. The 2-3 week mark comes from Dan's coaching observation over 17 years, not a study.
There is no hard wall at 2-3 weeks. No study sets that limit. The closest is appetite: hunger rose in the first days and eased within about 3 weeks while ketotic in a very-low-energy diet study (Nymo 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28439092/), and each kg lost raised intake by about 100 kcal/day over 52 weeks in another (Polidori 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27804272/). Trials show people can hold a reduced-calorie diet for months with support (Franz 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17904936/). The honest form of the claim is that appetite and adherence pressure builds, and that breaks help. For insulin, see the next entry.
The picture is meant to make it simple to understand which choices to make. The car comparison does not translate directly into the science of how the body works, but it is a useful way to view the structure.

So it is a decision aid, not a physiology claim. The real literature is "metabolic flexibility", the ability to adapt fuel use to demand (Goodpaster and Sparks 2017, Cell Metabolism, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28467922/; Smith 2018, Endocrine Reviews, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29697773/). It has no single agreed measurement, and one analysis argues the usual measure is biased (Galgani 2008, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18765680/). Evidence that carbohydrate restriction changes flexibility is thin: one small randomized crossover of 6 trained athletes over 14 days found no difference (Graybeal 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36673864/). The travel tier 3 guidance, to not eat, rests on the view that this should not be a big deal when metabolism is working well. That is Dan's judgment, untested.

What it buys: a person with no lab can still decide what to eat. Mode 1 every day, carbohydrate where life puts it. The alternative is a precise physiological claim, which would need measurements nobody has at home, or more rules to cover the gap. The method keeps the simple picture and says plainly that it is a picture.

## Why no carbs in Mode 1?
The purpose is to minimize how much someone notices a caloric deficit, not to cause fat loss without one. The deficit does the fat loss. The carb timing is there to make the deficit less noticeable, and nothing in the method claims fat loss without one. What follows is Dan's reasoning with the evidence beside it.

Mode 1 foods keep a person full the longest per calorie. Insulin comes second. In Dan's reasoning, better insulin sensitivity makes it easier to go longer without eating and helps hunger regulate better.
Fat loss comes from the deficit. Insulin management alone does not lose weight; it makes a deficit less noticeable when it is working well. No study is cited here for the insulin claims.
The method borrows from the idea of low insulin but is not a low carb diet. On the point that insulin sensitivity improves with fat loss and not with when carbs are eaten: that is the goal. The weight loss link is supported: in 22 adults with obesity a 7% weight loss raised insulin-mediated glucose uptake by about 48% on a clamp (Kirk 2009, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19208352/), and a lifestyle program cut diabetes incidence by 58% against placebo in 3,234 adults with elevated glucose (Knowler 2002, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11832527/). Dan's view is that cause runs both ways at the same time: weight loss improves insulin sensitivity, and better insulin sensitivity helps weight loss. The trials above show the first direction. Whether better insulin sensitivity makes weight loss easier was checked, and the evidence does not support it so far. Dan's hypothesis is that better insulin sensitivity leads to net-deficit behavior more often. In the DIETFITS trial of 609 adults, insulin secretion did not change how well people did on their assigned diet (P=.47; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29466592/), and in two small studies insulin-resistant and insulin-sensitive dieters lost about the same (McLaughlin 2001, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11436184/; Cornier 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15897479/). Several post hoc subgroup analyses found people with high insulin secretion did better on a low-glycemic or low-carb diet (Ebbeling 2007, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17507345/; Hjorth 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28679551/), which is about which diet fits, not overall success. No controlled study was found showing insulin-sensitive people eat less or feel less hungry, and no study tested the closed loop over time. Dan's simpler version, which does not depend on insulin: the benefit may be purely that a person stays satiated enough to rack up a deficit, and then there is no more time in the day to overeat. That fits the better-supported evidence, that protein and vegetables are filling, and no study tests it as a whole. Dan's point on why the two are hard to separate: to lose the weight you need to eat less, and when you eat less your insulin is lower. Circulating insulin and insulin sensitivity are different measures, but a deficit moves both, which is why observations in people who are losing weight cannot isolate the effect of carbohydrate timing. Dan's question: more time in a low insulin environment should lead to better insulin sensitivity. The evidence is mixed. A small weight-stable trial in men with prediabetes found eating within an early 6-hour window for 5 weeks improved insulin sensitivity (Sutton 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29754952/; sample size not in the abstract), while larger free-living trials and a meta-analysis of early time-restricted eating found no insulin or HOMA benefit. In the 22-adult clamp study, a low-carb and a high-carb deficit diet improved muscle insulin sensitivity equally at 7% weight loss, though the low-carb diet cut liver fat and liver glucose output faster at 48 hours (Kirk 2009, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19208352/). Those are early time-restricted eating trials, which is not this method: there the low insulin stretch is the late afternoon, evening and night after an early eating window, and here it is the morning, with Mode 2 later in the day. They are nearest evidence for the general idea of long low insulin periods, not tests of the method. So the idea is plausible and partly supported, and not confirmed. Dan's point is that dropping weight is the main thing associated with better insulin regulation, and that the open question is how people maintain the deficit needed to drop the weight. The method's job is to make the deficit sustainable. Dan's insulin explanation for the appetite effect is his reasoning and no study is cited for it. The objection overreaches if it is read as saying that when carbohydrate is eaten has no effect on glucose and insulin: eating carbohydrate raises glucose and insulin each time, and timing can matter. In a 12-week randomized trial of overweight and obese women with metabolic syndrome on the same 1,400 kcal diet, a pattern with the big meal at breakfast gave larger falls in fasting glucose, insulin and HOMA-IR than the same calories with the big meal at dinner (Jakubowicz 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23512957/). That trial studied only women with obesity and metabolic syndrome eating 1,400 kcal, so it cannot be applied to healthy lifters, men, or people eating more. It varied calories by time of day, not carbohydrate, and it favors the opposite pattern to a light, protein-first morning, which is why it is also in the entry on evening meals. What is not established is that carb-free mornings improve underlying insulin sensitivity beyond the fat loss they help produce.

Dan also holds that appetite is easier to keep low for the first half of the day without carbs. Verified limit: studies are mixed, and no study tests a carb-free first half of the day. Two small studies found no hunger or appetite difference by breakfast composition, and both tested a single morning in people who were not adapted to a carb-free morning. Emilien 2017 gave 33 healthy undergraduates each breakfast type on a separate test day, rated appetite for 4 hours before a lunch and recorded diet diaries for the rest of that day, and found no difference in day-long intake by breakfast type (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27379830/). King 2025 gave 16 resistance-trained adults a breakfast drink 2 hours before a 90-minute lifting session; it was built to test lifting volume, hunger was a side measure, and fullness was higher after both carbohydrate breakfasts than after placebo (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40029149/). Neither tests days or weeks of carb-free mornings. This is not the same as eating no carbs ever, and it is not the same as fasting. Carb foods are eaten the same day, later, and Mode 1 is eating. Ketogenic and fasting trials are the nearest evidence and not tests of it. Dan's idea: keeping insulin low through most of the day by avoiding carb foods (eggs and other protein foods raise it a little; eating anything does) gives a window of less appetite and hunger, so the back half of the day arrives with plenty of room in the calories. Zero carbs is not possible, so the carb window is shorter and starts with fruit, and the day has a large calorie bank to spend. Dan restates it this way: longer periods with little or no insulin release improve the body's ability to regulate hunger signals and to use fat stores in a caloric deficit, without a blood sugar problem creating cravings. That is a claim about adaptation over time, and no study found tests it. Dan's observation from coaching: a metabolically healthy person can accidentally go hours without eating, if distracted or busy, and be fine, while a metabolically unhealthy person cannot, and becomes irritable, has blood sugar problems and tends to eat more overall because of it. That is an observation, not a studied result, and unsteady energy or mood has other causes, such as poor sleep, eating too little the day before, caffeine habits, and medication. His prediction: in aggregate, people who eat carb foods sooner feel hunger more often all day. That is his bet, and no study found tests it over days.

What the research says about that idea, link by link (abstracts only; nothing tests this exact pattern of protein, vegetables and fats until mid-afternoon and then free carbs with intake measured afterward). First, lower daytime insulin: carb-free meals give lower single-meal insulin, but protein foods raise insulin too (Holt 1997, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9356547/). Second, less hunger: the closest design is a 6-month trial of 78 officers with obesity on a low-calorie diet with carbs mostly at dinner, which found lower hunger scores, lower daily insulin and more weight loss than the control diet, though the groups differed in weight loss (Sofer 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21475137/). In single-day crossovers, an egg breakfast gave less hunger and lower lunch intake than a bagel in 21 men (Ratliff 2010, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20226994/) and lower evening intake in 30 men (Fallaize 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22948783/), which is a protein effect as much as a low-carb one. Glucose dips predicted hunger and next-meal timing with small correlations in 1,070 people (Wyatt 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33846643/). Against the insulin mechanism: higher insulin responses went with more fullness in 28 young men (Flint 2006, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17158418/) and with less food eaten in a study of 38 foods (Holt 1996, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8968699/). Third, more calorie room later: untested directly. In a 6-day trial a high-protein breakfast raised fullness but did not change total daily intake (Leidy 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23446906/). The closest opposing evidence is from chronic carbohydrate restriction, which is not this pattern: in two inpatient trials of 20 adults each, people ate more when carbohydrate-restricted, by 689 kcal a day on a ketogenic diet against a low-fat diet over 2 weeks (Hall 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33479499/) and by 551 kcal over the rest of the day after a low-carbohydrate breakfast (Hengist 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39113385/). Hunger rose for about 3 weeks on a ketogenic very-low-calorie diet and then settled, in 31 adults without a control group (Nymo 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28439092/). In short: the appetite benefit is partly supported through protein and glucose stability, the low-insulin explanation is not supported, and the calorie-room claim is untested. Dan's own report: eating carbs earlier in the day raises his appetite for the rest of the day, and when he holds off on carbs he can go 6 or more hours with no food and no energy or hunger problems. That is one person's experience, not a study. Jakubowicz 2012 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22178258/) randomized 193 obese adults to a low-carbohydrate breakfast or a breakfast high in both carbohydrate and protein, and hunger and cravings were higher on the low-carbohydrate breakfast; because protein also differed, that trial cannot isolate carbohydrate. Where an appetite effect is shown, it is for protein, not for carbohydrate: in 19 people, raising protein from 15% to 30% of energy with carbohydrate fixed at 50% lowered spontaneous intake by 441 kcal/day (Weigle 2005, non-randomized, no control arm, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/), and a review of acute trials found a modest satiety effect of protein but no effect on intake at the next meal (Leidy 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/).
From Dan's own practice: he carries a lot of muscle and is fairly lean, so he can deliberately eat a bowl of cereal, extra rice or fruit without his appetite getting out of control, and more carbs give him bigger pumps and better chances of setting PRs. In his coaching experience, most people eat enough carbs and do fine, since they are not very muscular or physically active. He ate 300 g of carbs on one recent day (one person's experience). No separate answer was written for this objection itself.

The claim is untested. No study was found that compares the benefit of carbohydrate by muscularity. Related findings are small: 29 trained men who chose higher carbohydrate gained more arm curl strength (+6.6% versus +3.0%) with no significant difference in lean tissue, but groups were not randomized (Ribeiro 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37114196/). A review found glycogen depletion from lifting unlikely and low glycogen did not blunt a key growth signal (Escobar 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27993175/).

How the method treats it: Mode 2 has no limit on carbs, and nutritious carbs are encouraged, so a muscular person already has room. Adding carbs to Mode 1 would cost the morning appetite benefit Dan reports. Plain label: Dan's experience, one person, not a finding.
Dan agrees that few low carb diets can do that, and thinks keto or very low carb diets tend to have poor adherence long term. This is not that diet. Mode 1 is not ketogenic, Dan's own Mode 1 is about 30 g of carbohydrate, and Mode 2 has no limit. He ate 300 g of carbs on one day (his own experience). On the first days: a person might feel a little mentally foggy for a few days while adapting, but it is not a zero carb diet.

Gallstones are tied to the rate of loss. The risk rises sharply above about 1.5 kg a week (Weinsier 1995, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7847427/). On a 500 kcal a day program for 3 months, symptomatic gallstones were 152 versus 44 per 10,000 person-years compared with a milder diet (Johansson 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23736359/). That is a different, far lower-calorie diet. Keto flu rests on forum posts: 101 of 300 users reported symptoms, mostly in the first weeks (Bostock 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32232045/). No controlled trial was found. Electrolytes and hair loss: not found.

Whether adherence to keto or very low carb diets is poor over the long term is Dan's view, and it is not tested here. The one 12-month trial found measures only study completion, not how closely people followed the diet: 79% of the low-carb group and 82% of the low-fat group completed the intervention, and both groups received dietary counseling at regular intervals (Bazzano 2014, 148 adults, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25178568/). That is far more support than a typical dieter gets, so it says little about real-world adherence. The abstract gives no figure for adherence to the diet itself.

## Can I do a failure set on Mode 1 with no carbs?
The method's position is that yes, a single failure set can be done on Mode 1 with no carbs. Dan's reasoning: if a person cannot manage one failure set without carbs right before the workout, that points to insulin issues, with glycogen from the day before not being stored properly, which is all the more reason to stay with Mode 1 until it feels better. The "insulin issues" inference is Dan's reasoning.
Dan's view is also that humans would not have survived this long if a single hard set needed carbs right beforehand. That is his opinion, not a finding.

Evidence, stated plainly: muscle glycogen use follows how much work is done. In a meta-analysis of 20 studies, depletion was larger with more sets and longer sessions (Hamidvand 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41420384/). Trained men on a 39-minute, three-exercise leg session lost about 27% of vastus lateralis glycogen (Haff 2000, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10997956/), and bodybuilders doing about 20 hard sets lost about 28% (Essen-Gustavsson 1990, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2289498/). One set is a small fraction of that by proportion; no study tests one set. After an overnight fast the liver is the store that supplies blood glucose, which is why muscle glycogen is expected to be largely kept, but no measured adult study is cited for that. Training after an overnight fast gave similar growth and strength to training fed in a 12-week trial of 28 young adults (Vieira 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40335157/) and in a small review (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41316673/). Limits: carbohydrate did raise total volume in fasted sessions longer than 45 minutes (King 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35809162/), so this is about short sessions like the method's. If someone feels weak on a fasted morning, the site's answers on low energy apply (add carbs after work), and sleep, a hard prior day and a deep deficit are other causes.

## Are the supplements (vitamin D, creatine) medical advice?
Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md)): "I'm not a doctor. Look up the research and make the call."
Dan's position: a person does not have to take 4,000 IU, but he believes the recommended 600 or 400 IU is not sufficient and that considerable recent research backs this. He has taken 4,000 IU a day for 5 years (his practice, one person, no blood level). It is optional. Blood tests and dose are for a doctor.

Facts: the US recommended intake is 600 IU a day to age 70 and 800 after. 4,000 IU is the upper limit set by the US and EU bodies, not a target.

The case for higher: a recalculation says about 2,909 IU a day is needed for 97.5% of people to reach 50 nmol/L, and 7,248 for people with obesity (Veugelers 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26690210/). That is a blood-level argument, not an outcomes one. In a meta-analysis of 51 studies in elite athletes, about 30% of adults were below 50 nmol/L (Harju 2022, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35882673/).

The case against: 25,871 adults on 2,000 IU for 5.3 years showed no cancer or cardiovascular benefit (Manson 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30415629/). In 311 healthy adults, 4,000 IU for 3 years lowered radial bone density by 2.4% versus 1.2% at 400 IU, with no change in bone strength (Burt 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31454046/). The Endocrine Society suggests against supplementing above the usual intake in healthy adults under 75, and against routine testing (Demay 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/). No lifter trial was found.
Creatine's claimed benefit is a few more reps and more PRs, not lean mass.

Evidence supports that. A meta-analysis of 53 trials found a small to moderate upper-body effect (effect size 0.317, bench press 0.265), in tasks under 3 minutes (Lanhers 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27328852/). A review of 22 studies found max reps at a set load rose 26% with creatine versus 12% with placebo, with highly variable response (Rawson and Volek 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14636102/). Body composition effect size was 0.17 (Branch 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12945830/). Some of creatine's lean mass gain may be water, and the effect on measured muscle size is very small and not clearly different from zero (Burke 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37432300/). Creatine also lifts the PR numbers the method tracks, so it is a small boost to the measuring stick (writer's summary).

Fish oil "is great for joints" is Dan's FAQ line. A meta-analysis of 42 trials found a small pain benefit overall, significant in rheumatoid arthritis and not in osteoarthritis (5 trials, effect -0.17, interval -0.57 to 0.24) (Senftleber 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28067815/). So the claim holds for inflammatory arthritis, not shown for osteoarthritis.

Both are optional and cheap. The alternative, no supplements, costs nothing and loses a small gain.

## Isn't skipping milk and post-workout nutrition wrong?
Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md)): both are explicit FAQ entries (milk: "Just skip it"; post-workout nutrition is "Not as useful as it is inconvenient").
The method's position is that this question describes something different from the eating plan.

That position stands. Dairy is not skipped. Greek yogurt, cottage cheese and cheese are Mode 1 foods. Only milk as a drink is left out, and Mode 2 is unrestricted. Whole milk is not a protein portion, but nothing stops it in Mode 2 (writer's summary).

For context, calcium intake was below the average requirement in about 38% of US adults in NHANES, before any special diet (Fulgoni 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21865568/). Whether a long deficit affects bone, especially in women, is a separate question. No study on that is in the research files, so none is cited, and the method does not address it separately, because the objection misdescribes the plan. What it buys: dairy protein stays available. The alternative, requiring milk, adds a calorie drink inside Mode 1 for little extra over yogurt and cottage cheese (writer's summary).

## Hunger, low energy, weekends, food prep, "I want to change it".
Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md)): each has its own entry, including night hunger, low energy, weekends, prep, and "I want to make changes or pick and choose parts of this".
The method's position is that these should not be a problem. The restriction is fewer calories for 8 hours of the day, then Mode 2. While in Mode 1, a person is eating the most satiating macronutrients per calorie.

Protein's fullness effect is modest. Acute trials show more fullness but not lower intake at the next meal, and long-term data are limited and mixed, with adherence the main factor (Leidy 2015, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25926512/). In 19 people, going to 30% protein cut spontaneous intake by 441 kcal a day (Weigle 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16002798/), with no control arm. Hiding vegetables in meals cut daily intake by 202 to 357 kcal with hunger unchanged (Blatt 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21289225/). After a 36 hour fast, 24 lean adults ate about 480 kcal more the next day, well short of the deficit (Johnstone 2002, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12461679/).

Dan's idea that unsteady energy can reflect insulin regulation that this eating should help is his opinion. Sleep, under-eating, iron, thyroid, anxiety and caffeine are other causes (writer's summary), and true blood sugar conditions go to a doctor. Acclimation takes about 4 days, longer if someone is in rough shape, and hunger at a certain time is solved by moving portions, not by Mode 1 hours.

The alternative, bigger daytime meals, trades away the deficit for comfort.
