Weigh each downside against what it buys. Every method has costs, and many criticisms swap one problem for a bigger one elsewhere (Dan).

Research gaps, stated once. Nutrition studies are mostly short, small, and run in people who do not match how anyone actually lives, and most test isolated pieces, such as a protein amount, an eating window or a named diet. None tests this system as a whole, or the long-term question of what a person can keep doing for years. Dan's view is that the important questions are largely unstudied, that the field is a set of disconnected studies, and that a system like this one is not considered in it. This file says where evidence is thin or contradicts, and gives the nearest evidence where a study does not match the method.

The principle behind all of it: most of the rules around eating exist so that a person does not notice a deficit as much, mentally or biologically.

The explanation: a caloric deficit is needed, but it cannot be aggressively chronic and it cannot be malnutrition. So the normal ebb and flow of life circumstances regulates the deficit and the refeeds, and the mandatory foods cover the malnutrition risk.

The reasoning is that making the best choice is far more likely when a person is not backed into a corner with restriction and is already filled with satiating, important foods. Most people do not need to eat perfectly; they need to bring their floor up.

## Without calorie counting, how do you know there is a deficit?
Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md), "I want to count calories" and "Precision Nutrition Measurements"): "Calories and macros are the ultimate decider of weight. But counting them daily doesn't work in practice." Hand measurements are "sufficiently accurate at a fraction of the time and focus required."
If fat loss stalls, the answer is to add steps.
The question the method asks is how accurate the measurement needs to be for long-term results, and what the added benefit of measuring is against the adherence risk.

On the objection that a palm could mean 80 g one day or 160 g another: the relevant figure is the average across a month, which could be about 120 g a day. Day-to-day error averages out over a month when it is random. It does not average out when the error is consistent, for example always taking the bigger palm. The Japanese hand-scale study found average differences near zero with wide error per meal, which fits the random pattern (Shinozaki 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40669561/).

The practical point is that the only alternative to hand portions is counting calories and measuring food, and that has its own costs (below).

The idea is to strike a balance between weighing and measuring every calorie, which can itself be quite accurate, and not paying attention to food at all. Dan's judgment is that hand portion measurement gives the best ratio of work to accuracy. No study compares accuracy per unit of effort across methods. The hand portions come from the Precision Nutrition method, which Dan says has a considerable amount of case studies. What is published: three small papers on Precision Nutrition Coaching with company-affiliated authors, none of which measures portion accuracy: a single-arm pilot in 46 cancer survivors (49% retained at 12 months, 5.3% weight loss; Lynch 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28392934/), a randomized trial of 35 women after premature surgical menopause (fitness and bone endpoints; Sturgeon 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27873046/), and a single-arm pilot in women (28 completers, 50% of those who started, 8.6% weight loss at 12 months; Heroux 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30135822/). The company's own case studies are customer reports. They can show that people who stay in the program lose weight. They cannot show how accurate the portions are or how the method compares with counting.

The downside, accurately: the evidence on the exact palm, fist and thumb system is thin. The closest test is Shinozaki 2025, a cross-sectional study of 1,081 Japanese adults over 12,148 meals with weighed records, using a hand scale of fists for grains and fruit, palms for protein and handfuls for vegetables: self-reported hand counts correlated with weighed grams at 0.59 for grains, 0.72 for protein, 0.76 for vegetables and 0.85 for fruit, with wide limits of agreement per meal and average differences close to zero (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40669561/). It is a Japanese hand system, not Precision Nutrition's, and the hand counts were self-reported. Another study tested a finger-width method: 80% of estimates within 25% of true weight, 13% within 10% (Gibson 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27547392/). A review of 136 estimation aids found mean error running from 92% under to 413% over (Fadeiye 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42176340/). People underestimate many portions (Almiron-Roig 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23932948/).

Weighing costs time (Harvey 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30801989/: 14.6 to 23.2 minutes a day), and people stop. In context the portions are a minimum, so error that lands high does not cause a deficit problem, and Mode 2 and the weight trend carry the rest.

Not found: any trial comparing weighed food with estimated food on long-term weight loss. The question of long-term accuracy is therefore open, not answered, and the evidence neither confirms nor rejects hands.
Dan's reasoning: if accuracy is about equal, hands are the better method, because food quality is better and the effort is lower.

Counting is not accurate either. Self-reported intake undercounted energy by 12 to 14% (24 hour recall) and 31 to 36% (food frequency) in men, and 16 to 20% and 34 to 38% in women (Subar 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12835280/). Pooled studies found an average 28% underreport with questionnaires, more at higher BMI (Freedman 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24918187/). In 10 self-described diet-resistant people, intake was underreported by 47% (Lichtman 1992, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1454084/). That is a small selected group. App databases differ from lab values, with correlations of 0.73 to 0.96 but lower means for some nutrients (Griffiths 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29534771/), and these tests use recalls entered by researchers.

Honest limit: no study compares hand estimation with counting head to head, so "a wash" is Dan's judgment. The argument rests on effort and food quality: if error is similar, the cheaper method wins. Counting costs time and shifts choices toward numbers (Dan's opinion). Dan does not advise against it.

The method does monitor: a daily yes or no on whether the Mode 1 portions were eaten, and a weight trend. It advises against counting calories in an app. Dan's reasoning (his opinion): if people eat the right foods the macros line up well enough, and people who chase numbers rarely eat the right foods. So the tracking is of the types and amounts of Mode 1 foods.

Evidence: a review of 22 studies found a consistent link between self-monitoring and weight loss but rated it weak (Burke 2011, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/). People who tracked consistently lost 2.42 kg more at 3 months in a trial of 100, but they differed in other ways, and causation is unknown (Patel 2020, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31396820/). In a 24 month trial, paper, PDA and PDA with feedback did not differ in weight change (Burke 2012, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22704741/): adherence mattered more than method. Only about a quarter tracked daily over 12 weeks.

Not found: any trial of tracking food groups or portions instead of calories. That gap cuts both ways. The cost of calorie logging is the time and the dropout shown above, and the risk of a simpler check is that it measures less. The weight channel is the check.

## Mode 2 ("whatever you want") can erase the deficit.
On the objection that Mode 2 is unmeasured, so the weekly deficit could be zero and the scale is the only feedback: the answer is to tone down Mode 2. In practice it will be extremely clear which Mode 2 behavior is stalling progress. When progress stalls, the options are to tone down Mode 2 or add steps (see the stall rules in the eating file), and the weight channel over weeks is the feedback. Not measured: no study tests whether people can identify stalling behavior in free eating periods.

Other nutrition strategies have the same limit: no single diet strategy removes the effect of human behavior. This one sets a person up for the best chance of success.
Obstacles can be fixed; not doing the plan cannot be fixed by the plan. The rules give the most possible freedom and the least sacrifice while still reaching the goal, and following them is the person's choice.
Evidence: in a 1-year trial of four named diets, weight loss followed self-reported adherence, not diet type (Dansinger 2005, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15632335/). A network meta-analysis found minimal differences between named diets (Johnston 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25182101/). Limit: adherence in Dansinger was self-reported, so people who lost weight may have reported better adherence. "Within reason" is not defined in numbers. The person decides it.

## Mode 1 is very low calorie.
The method is not for beginners. Mode 1 calories are not all of a person's daily calories, and Mode 2 follows. The Mode 1 foods are roughly 800 calories of the day, a minimum of mandatory food types and general amounts. They are not an 800 calorie day. The AI does not use this as a reassurance: the Mode 1 foods are a minimum, not the day's total. Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md)): "Mode 1 foods are the minimum, not the maximum", and the low-energy answer tells the person to add carbs after work. For anyone with a medical condition or on medication, see "Injuries, conditions, medication" below. Mode 2 has no cap.

Low energy availability is real. In 29 sedentary women over 5 days, hormone pulsing was unchanged at 30 kcal per kg of lean mass and changed below it (Loucks and Thuma 2003, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12519869/). A later randomized study of 35 women (Lieberman 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29023359/) found the odds of a menstrual disturbance fell 9% per unit of energy availability (odds ratio 0.91) and that no specific value emerged as a threshold; the authors describe disturbances as increasing linearly as energy availability falls, and a 2026 update moves away from a fixed threshold (Williams 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41474492/). All of these set total daily intake over days. None tested a feeding window, and no study measures total intake on this method.

What it buys: a firm floor of protein and vegetables without counting. The risk sits with the person who also eats little in Mode 2, which the method does not measure. The alternative, a calorie floor, needs counting, with the error and effort that brings. A woman with a missed period gets the one-line doctor check.

## The same portions for everyone (4 palms, 3 fists, 1-2 thumbs)?
The portions are the mandatory skeleton. The dynamic nature of the diet means that on a day when a person feels good and is not hungry, they can have a Mode 1 style dinner as well and run a 1000+ calorie deficit day. That will not happen every day. Acute temporary calorie fluctuations generally work differently from chronic ones: acute deficits are powerful, acute surpluses are restorative, and chronic versions of either are a problem.
On refeeds: this is what is called a refeed or a diet break, but instead of adding logistics and discipline, it is built into normal behavior and autoregulated.

Evidence: alternating 2-week diet blocks with 2-week energy-balance blocks gave more fat loss than continuous dieting in men with obesity (MATADOR, Byrne 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28925405/), but that trial was small, per-protocol, and took 30 weeks versus 16. In resistance-trained adults (ICECAP, Peos 2021, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33587549/) there was no difference in fat mass or fat-free mass, but the intermittent group reported lower hunger and desire to eat. A 1-year trial found a 5:2 pattern did no better and no worse than continuous dieting (Headland 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30470804/). No study tested single-day deficits and appetite-driven refeeds, and the "chronic surplus is bad" half is untested.
The scaling applies to protein and vegetable intake. Dan's view is that bigger people have bigger hands, that this gets them about 90% of the way there, and that anyone who needs more makes up the difference in Mode 2.

Writer's summary: hands scale the protein and vegetable portions to body size. Total energy is handled in Mode 2, which the person sets by appetite and the weight trend.

The downside: hand size tracks frame and height. A short heavy person gets a smaller palm than a tall lean one, which gives less protein than their body weight might suggest. Gains from added protein plateau around 1.62 g/kg a day (Morton 2018, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/), and the Mode 1 portions plus the Mode 2 palm come to roughly 0.76 g/lb for a 180 lb man (the arithmetic in the protein entry below).

What it buys: one simple scaling rule with nothing to measure. The alternative is a calculator or table, which asks for weight, body fat and activity inputs and a daily number to hit. Not found: any study comparing hand size with lean mass or protein need, so the claim that hand size fits body size is Dan's reasoning, not tested.

## Does 0.7 g/lb protein hold up?
Site ([faq/eating.md](../faq/eating.md)): "About 1.6g/kg (0.73g/lb) bodyweight is sufficient for muscle building." The site line was changed on 2026-10-22 from "0.7g/lb bodyweight is sufficient for muscle building." The Mode 1 foods hit this mark regardless of what is eaten after, with no need to count it all. The method's position is that the protein target follows the research, and that eating the portions reaches it, meaning about 1.6 g/kg (0.73 g/lb). The Mode 1 palm portions plus the Mode 2 palm come to roughly 0.76 g/lb for a 180 lb man, counting only the protein foods. That is a floor. Incidental protein from foods outside the protein category is not counted, and often adds up by the end of the day to close to a palm of protein.
Verified limit: Morton 2018 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28698222/) found gains from added protein plateau around 1.62 g/kg/day for muscle gain. Studies in a calorie deficit point higher (Helms 2014, Int J Sport Nutr Exerc Metab 24(2):127-138, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24092765/; Longland 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817506/).
Dan's view is that muscle protein synthesis is irrelevant. On the four-palms gap, his answer was that nobody would eat that way, which is a point about the premise.

The premise: the objection pictures all four palms in one sitting, then up to 16 hours with nothing. Mode 1 portions are spread across the waking hours in any order, and Mode 2 adds a fifth palm in the evening, so most days spread across several meals.

Evidence on distribution: acute studies show per-meal effects. An even three-meal split gave 25% higher 24 hour protein synthesis than a skewed one in 8 people (Mamerow 2014, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24477298/). 4 x 20 g beat 2 x 40 g and 8 x 10 g in trained men (Areta 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23459753/). But 100 g in one dose gave a larger and longer response than 25 g (Trommelen 2023, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38118410/), against a fixed ceiling.

Longer term, evidence is thinner. A 6 week trial of 24 rugby players found no difference in lean mass between distributions (1.4 versus 1.5 kg; MacKenzie-Shalders 2016, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26132746/). A review judged the benefit of distribution limited and not separable from total protein (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32429355/). Total protein was the strongest predictor in a 2013 meta-analysis (Schoenfeld 2013, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24299050/).

The view that distribution is not relevant is stronger than the evidence, which says distribution is unresolved once total protein is matched. Cost of spreading protein by a schedule: more rules and a clock. Not found: a long-term trial of distribution in trained adults matched for total protein.

## Isn't Mode 1 repetitive?
Mode 1 is not the same foods every day. The food lists give many choices for each portion, and the person picks any order and combination.
If someone wants more variety than the lists give, Dan's view is that they are valuing food as entertainment more than being lean. He calls it an unbelievably reasonable deal, the best possible one. If it is not a good enough deal, Dan's view is that the goal is not a high enough priority for them, which is fine. It is their call, and the rule stays as written.

## Can Mode 2 quality be poor?
This is the floor over ceiling principle (principle 4). Effort and discipline go into raising the floor and making it durable. A person can reach for the ceiling whenever and wherever they want.

Even a very poor Mode 2, say nothing but gummy bears, leaves a person in decent shape, because Mode 1 is already done. The point of the rules is that the floor is quite high. That is the worst case scenario. A typical Mode 2 includes the extra palm of protein and incidental protein from other foods, which together can add up to a palm or more. What the worst case still covers: the Mode 1 protein, vegetables and fats, about 110 g of protein for a 180 lb man in the FAQ example, which is above the standard recommended intake of 0.8 g/kg a day (about 65 g for that man, the writer's arithmetic). What it does not guarantee: the Mode 2 palm or a calorie deficit. That is the trade: a high floor for very few rules, with the ceiling left to the person.

Dan's suggested start to Mode 2 is a piece of fruit: fiber and water take the edge off before the free meal, it tops up liver glycogen after Mode 1, and it shows you can still enjoy clean foods in Mode 2. A suggestion, not a rule.

If someone feels guilty about Mode 2 foods, the answer is to eat nutritious foods instead. Examples: fruit, rice, whole grains, potato, more veg, another protein.

Note: the original combined objection (poor variety, yet unconstrained Mode 2) is internally inconsistent. Mode 1 is the fixed part and Mode 2 is the free part, so both cannot be the problem at once.
Dan does not recommend ultra processed foods, but Mode 2 food choice is not a win or fail condition. A win is Mode 1 adhered to. Mode 2 also requires another protein block.

The downside: in 20 adults in a hospital ward, an ultra-processed diet raised intake by 508 calories a day over two weeks, and weight rose 0.9 kg (Hall 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31105044/). In 55 adults over 8 weeks, a minimally processed diet lost 2.06% versus 1.05% on ultra-processed, a gap of 1.01 percentage points (Dicken 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40760353/). Both diets lost weight.

In context: those were whole-day menus that people were given. Mode 2 is one part of the day, after Mode 1 has already supplied protein, vegetables and fat, plus a required protein palm that crowds out some junk. The method does not set a Mode 2 menu. Dan's advice is to eat nutritious foods whenever there is the chance, and that is also his answer to someone who feels guilty about Mode 2.

The alternative, banning ultra-processed foods, adds a rule to the evening where social eating happens, which raises adherence risk. Not found: a trial of ultra-processed foods inside a partly structured day.
