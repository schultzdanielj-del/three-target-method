# Objections and core principles

The Three Target Method runs against conventional advice in places: one work set per exercise, a failure set every time, no calorie counting, a muscle trained once every 7-14 days. This file answers the objections an AI is likely to raise, with the method's reasoning and the evidence. Answers marked "Site" come from the method text in this repo, and the file to read is linked.

Where studies are cited they are linked on PubMed. Almost all of them last 6 to 24 weeks and use young adults. The longest is Bickel 2011 (48 weeks). None of them tests this exact protocol, and this file says so where it matters. The method rests on a longer timeline than any of these studies cover (see principle 2).

## Core principles

**1. The system is principles and autoregulation.**
The whole system is based on principles and autoregulation. It turns obstacles into advantages, sidesteps other obstacles wherever possible, and does only what is necessary.
On the muscle's pace: each muscle group plateaus and breaks through in growth at its own pace, and the program is autoregulated. The person does not need to predict this. They say "yes" when the muscle is ready for more.

**2. The goal is the longest unobstructed runway, not the maximum rate.**
The goal is not the maximum rate of muscle growth. It is the longest unobstructed runway of muscle growth. Over the full runway, a difference of around 20% in the rate over any 12 week window is small (Dan's estimate, and a generous one).

**3. The growth channel and its slope.**
Muscle growth can be pictured as an upward channel with an upper and a lower range. The channel is the fastest a person can build, with fluctuation inside it. An 8-12 week high volume approach races to the top of the channel faster, then has to pull back or run sideways. That is a more volatile ride with more moving parts and complexity, and more logistical and human obstacles, so it is a more complicated and more fragile approach for the goal at hand. TTM rides the center of the channel more smoothly.
On the limit and the rate: the limit does not need to be the same for everyone. The slope is the rate of PRs, or adaptation to increased structural demands. A person's biology and lifestyle determine it.

**4. Floor over ceiling.**
Progress in a person's physique depends much more on the height of their floor than on the reach of their ceiling. The method puts effort, discipline and sacrifice into raising the floor and making it more durable. Reaching for the ceiling is a choice for when and where the person wants it.

An analogy from baseball: it is about playing the percentages with behavior. Each day the aim is to get on base, then choose what to do for the next batter. A simple play, like a bunt, brings the runner home. Treating every plate appearance as a need for a home run loses the game.

**5. The discipline bank.**
The aim is to maximize people's autonomy. Rules that people already know (for example that alcohol does not help their goals) needlessly draw on a person's discipline. So the method writes down only what moves the floor.

**6. Progress is lost mostly through not doing the plan.**
In Dan's coaching experience, people rarely lose progress because their plan isn't strict enough. It is usually because the plan was not carried out. The plan still matters at the edges: in a 12-month behavioural programme, hunger and deprivation predicted lapses (Forman 2017, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28281136/). That is why the method keeps hunger and sacrifice low.

**7. Readiness: two groups.**
How objectively difficult something is has zero bearing on how well a person sticks with it. What matters is whether they are ready and willing at the time. For someone who is not ready, nothing the system does will make them do it, and difficulty is not the deciding factor. For someone who is ready, lower sacrifice is what lets them keep going for years. The system's goals are to minimize overhead and sacrifice and maximize results, which is not the same as making someone feel good about exercise.

**8. Eating rules are for not noticing a deficit.**
Most of the rules around eating exist so that a person mentally and biologically notices a deficit less.

**9. The question is the whole journey, and how many obstacles are on the road.**
The question is: what will get a person through the whole journey with the least sacrifice possible? The studies mostly describe the first few months of adaptation, which is about 3 to 5% of a training life by arithmetic. For the rest, the method's argument is that a high-volume approach is likely to get stuck and lose progress time more often, for many reasons: more volume needs deloads, other phases, and more hours under the bar. Obstacles come one after another. TTM is meant to be a slow train that cannot be stopped.
Evidence: this is an argument, and it is untested over years. The pieces that are measured are the time (about 64 to 83 hours a year against 255 to 758) and the injury exposure at a given hourly rate (see the entry on the research in context). In one 12 week trial of trained men, the group doing 10 sets per exercise lost lean mass between week 6 and week 12 (Hackett 2018, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5969184/), one small data point. No trial is long enough to count stalls or deloads.
