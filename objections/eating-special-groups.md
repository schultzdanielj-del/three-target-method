# Objections: eating, Special groups

## Could a cruise weight of 140 to 150 lb for women push some toward underweight?
No. Cruise weight is defined by composition: the weight at a healthy low body fat range combined with a high fat-free mass index. It describes a lean, muscular woman and not an underweight one. It is a general range, and genetics and frame (wrist and ankle size) move it. The 140 to 150 lb figure is a reference point for an average-height woman, not a target for every woman. Two factors can raise the cruise weight. One is genetics, because nobody can say in advance who will be an FFMI outlier. The other is frame size, which is already covered by wrist and ankle circumference if the person wants their agent to add it, and which is autoregulated for height. The same logic applies to men: an average-height man is never going to be 200 lb or more with visible abs, because that would need too high an FFMI.

The downside, accurately: a number quoted without that context can be misread as a goal weight, and a woman who is already below her own range is underweight. For her the rule is the underweight rule: eat the Mode 1 foods every day by the end of the day, then whatever else she wants, with no deficit. The benefit of a composition-based range is that it follows how she looks and performs, and the PRs, not a scale number. The alternative, a fixed height-weight table, ignores muscle and frame. No study tests this definition of cruise weight, and no FFMI or body fat numbers are given here as a rule.

## On a GLP-1 with no appetite, isn't forcing the protein a risk?
Dan's position is that someone on a GLP-1 medication still needs the protein, so even on a GLP-1 the Mode 1 foods are eaten, and the person is not told not to do it. The agent does not use that as a reassurance: the Mode 1 foods are a minimum, not the day's total. Appetite loss is a medication effect, and food does not remove it. Skipping the protein as well means losing that protein.

Evidence: on tirzepatide about 75% of weight lost was fat and 25% lean mass, the same split as placebo (Look 2025, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39996356/). A meta-analysis of 20 trials put the lean share at 35.2% for semaglutide, 25.4% for tirzepatide, 26.2% for lifestyle and 17.5% for lifestyle plus resistance training, by indirect comparison (Eisa 2026, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41877354/). A review proposes resistance training as an add-on (Locatelli 2024, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38687506/). No published randomized trial of protein or exercise in people on a GLP-1 with a lean-mass result was found; only a registered protocol exists.

The benefit is keeping protein and lifting going. The alternative, skipping protein, loses the protein, and nobody has measured that it is safe. The portions can be eaten in any order across Mode 1 hours (writer's summary). The agent does not tell anyone to stop or change the drug. Nausea or dehydration goes to their doctor.

## What about pregnancy and nursing?
The method's position is that the fat loss plan is not for people who are pregnant or nursing, and that the method does not recommend eating for fat loss specifically in those periods. The Mode 1 foods are a different matter: for a person without a kidney medical condition, including the Mode 1 foods is optimal whatever the goal (Dan's view).

So the fat loss diet is not for them; the Mode 1 foods are simply food. "Optimal" is Dan's opinion. Nothing in the research files covers pregnancy or nursing diets, so there is no evidence to cite either way. The only related check is protein and kidneys in healthy adults (see the kidney entry). The reason there is no deficit and no window is that this is not a fat loss situation. Pregnancy and nursing guidance was not verified here.

What it buys: they are not left without guidance, and they are not put in a deficit. The alternative, refusing to help, gives them nothing. The agent says the doctor line once, tells them the fat loss plan is not for them, tells them the Mode 1 foods are good as ordinary food for anyone without a kidney condition (a kidney condition goes to their doctor), and does not give medical advice.

## What about athletes with high energy needs, or anyone whose goal is not fat loss?
When the goal is not fat loss, the Mode 1 foods do not need to be eaten at particular times. Eating the Mode 1 foods every day fits with pretty much any successful eating style.

So the rule is the same as for underweight people. Eat all the Mode 1 foods every day, then whatever else you need. No window, no Rule 3. "Any successful eating style" is the method's opinion and has not been tested.

The downside is that nothing has tested this for endurance athletes or two-a-day training. The energy availability studies (see the women's entry) show total intake matters, which is why the extra eating is unrestricted here. What it buys: the protein and vegetable floor stays, and fuel is not capped. The alternative is applying the fat loss window to someone who needs energy, where the problem would show up as under-fuelling. The agent does not write a fueling plan.

## What about cultural and religious eating, including a religious fast?
The current level of accommodation is to skip a food and pick another from the lists. For a fast such as Ramadan, the plan cannot start at waking, so the rule becomes: eat all the Mode 1 foods every day by the end of the day.

So a person skips foods they will not eat and picks others from the lists. If a whole category is excluded, the plan cannot be done as written. During a religious fast the window is dropped and all Mode 1 foods are eaten by the end of the day, the same shape as the underweight and non-fat-loss rule.

The downside is that there is no fixed deficit mechanism in that case, and nothing in the research files tests fasting patterns such as Ramadan, so no study is cited. What it buys: the protein and vegetable floor survives, and the person is not told to break a religious practice. The alternative, no accommodation, leaves them with no plan.

## Is this safe for someone with diabetes?
The method cannot give dietitian advice for a medical condition. Its view is that, for type 2 diabetes, the Mode 1 window should be helpful and not harmful. The reasoning is that the body needs to get better at switching between fuel sources, which can be slow, and that asking too much of it too soon can cause a blood sugar crash, which is a medical emergency that needs sugar. Treating every bit of hunger with sugar fixes the emergency but sustains the chronic condition: that is triage, not treatment. That helpfulness claim is the method's opinion. No study in the research files supports it.

The downside is concrete. Low blood sugar is the main risk, mainly for people on insulin and some glucose-lowering drugs, and low carbohydrate eating with less food changes that risk. A search for evidence on this found nothing usable; not found. Another medication class has a named risk: SGLT2 inhibitors raise ketoacidosis risk in type 1 diabetes, sometimes with near-normal glucose (Danne 2019, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30728224/). The regulator's notice was not verified.

The benefit, if the view is right, is steadier eating for type 2. The alternative, leaving a person to eat anything with no structure, has its own cost. The agent says the doctor line once, gives no dosing or medication advice and no treatment advice, and does not guess which drugs are involved.
