# Programming: how to build a person's plan

These rules come from his real client plans and his own answers. They add to the movement lists in [protocol/training.md](../protocol/training.md); they never replace them. Where a rule says "their call" or "depends", ask the person and let them decide.

The plan is built around what the person has: equipment availability is the most frequent limit. People's equipment and mobility limitations are by far their biggest obstacles. Ask what equipment they have (gym, home, dumbbells up to what weight, bench, bar, bands, TRX) before choosing anything. Dumbbell minimum for training at home: women need dumbbells up to 50 lb, men up to 90 lb. It has to be a full range with small jumps between weights, not 2 or 3 weights with huge jumps. A suspension trainer (TRX) can replace weights, but the person has to measure the strap length and where they stand very accurately, because that is the yardstick for resistance. Bands are not a good choice, because there is no way to reliably maintain the same conditions each session. If all someone has is one pair of light dumbbells (for example 20 lb) and no other weights, they have nothing to progress to, so they are not going to get long term results. The problem is the lack of any other weights, not the 20 lb itself. For every movement, the person needs at least one weight that they can hit the bottom of the rep range with, and that they cannot beat the top of the rep range with. One weight on its own lets them do only a fraction of the plan correctly. People with less than this equipment, unless they are already really strong and not overweight, have no options. It is a non starter.

Some people have two places to train (home and a commercial gym): give each slot a home option and a gym option for the same muscle.

Someone who trains in a crowded commercial gym can also get a second option on the slots where the equipment is often taken, for the same muscle (for example a machine press or incline pushups, a reverse pec deck or a bent over rear delt fly, a back raise or a Romanian deadlift).

Source of these rules: Dan's coaching observations from 17 years of coaching, 10,000+ hours of one on one coaching, across easily over 1000 people. They are anecdotal, not studies. Many of them have no direct study behind them, as is true of much of fitness. They are built by taking the known truths and logically assembling a model. Where a study is cited, it is named.

The core premise: the majority of people need much more focused professional intervention with their body mechanics than they are willing to admit. That is their biggest obstacle. They just move poorly. They do not execute the movements correctly at all, and they lack the ranges of motion to get into the correct positions to begin strengthening the right muscles. Most people say they understand this in passing, but they do not truly believe it or embrace it. They want to show up, do the work, and have it work, and then they get punished for it. No rep or set scheme, protocol or training plan fixes this. A lot of "what if" questions come from people who refuse to accept this. If you do not move correctly, you will not achieve your goals, period. This is about goals, not progress: a person can still progress in their current state of mobility, but their progress will have a hard ceiling well below anything that will truly satisfy them, so their goals are not achievable in it. Studies show people with limited mobility still gain muscle and strength, which does not contradict this, because they do not test whether the person reaches the goals they set.

This is why the program is not for complete beginners. A complete beginner can run the program, but running it by themselves and succeeding has slim chances, because of this movement issue. The single best thing that would improve their chances is being coached on how well they move and what their limitations are.

A beginner is also unlikely to succeed long term at any training program, for the same reason. The majority of gym goers never address these bottlenecks and just move from program to program, hoping something will break them through in spite of it. The only ways they work around the bottleneck are poor intensity, which gives no results, avoiding that area completely, which creates imbalances, or slowly getting hurt over the decades.

Talk to the person in layman's terms and simple English. Do not use biomechanical terms with them.
