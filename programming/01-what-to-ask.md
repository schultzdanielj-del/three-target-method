# 1. What to ask before building a plan

The more questions the person answers, the better the potential plan they will get without direct coach eyes on them. The plan can get as good as it can get without a coach seeing the person move and lift, and no further.

Ask the person, one question at a time:
- How long they have been training.
- What their goals are.
- What their experience is with exercises in the gym.
- Which movements are the easiest for them and which are the trickiest.
- Their history of injury.
- Whether anything hurts.
- What they are doing right now.
- What equipment and environment they have access to.

Most people want a nice lean, fit looking body: a bit of abs, fitting into smaller clothes. Getting that look takes more muscle, a lower bodyfat level and more strength than most people expect. That is just to get a regular fit person look. Most people have not had a reason to learn what a given physique requires. So the goal is always to get as lean as possible and build as much muscle as possible, even for a regular look the person may hesitate to say they want. Lowering the target on purpose makes it harder to reach.

Goals are usually vague. Generally, people just want to be leaner and have more muscle, but the language they use can disguise that. People are often hesitant to state what they truly want, and may not be sure what it means, so draw it out as you talk with them. Someone who says they only care about performance or health usually means the health and performance you get from being leaner and having more muscle. It is extremely rare for someone's goals to require a completely different approach. Most people do not need specialization of any kind.

These questions do not replace watching the person lift. Watching somebody lift is the single best way to determine which exercises, and which technique on each exercise, are best for them. Ask for a video.

The big idea: almost everyone makes the lion's share of their progress from consistent, clear cut measures of success, using the right movements for their body, performed correctly. That gets them further than they could ever imagine. Keep it simple: aim for a set performance with the same sound technique, and trim away everything else they do. Staying with that takes them beyond what they thought possible (Dan's opinion).

Remember the program principle: the question is never "what else can I add?" It is "what is left that I can take out?" and the answer is "nothing".
