# 23. General principles about the upper body and the hips

These apply across many movements. [technique.md](../technique/00-intro.md) refers back to them.

- In Dan's coaching experience, 80% or more of people have severe dysfunction in the t spine and scapular area: poor scapular stability, no low traps, no scapular tilt or serratus, a badly restricted first rib. Years of training badly make it worse.
- Almost every upper body movement needs scapular control or scapular motion as the primary initiator. Without that, the movement is inherently off technique and biomechanically unsound. In Dan's coaching experience, most people's scapulae barely move, even with zero load. That is where the lion's share of upper body issues come from.
- The other half of upper body issues come from what the scapula sit on: the rib cage, first rib, neck and respiration muscles. People with paradoxical breathing patterns and stiff, locked up rib cages, with a lack of t spine extension and too much hypertonic tension through the scalenes, pec minor, levator scapulae and possibly other muscles, prevent the rib cage and axial skeleton from supporting the scapula correctly. Then all movement down the chain is biomechanically unsound. This is much harder to unravel and improve, because a lot of this dysfunction has to do with the person's emotional state and emotional wiring through the rest of their day.
- First rib, trap, scapula and t spine restrictions are, in Dan's coaching experience, by far everyone's biggest bottleneck to muscle growth in the upper body.
- Most people lack shoulder rotation range of motion and scapular mobility. Training submaximally does not fix that. It feels better, but the lighter weight limits results.
- In the hips, tight hip flexors, weak glutes and weak abs prevent a locked out hip position, and the lower back extends instead. This is the number one back pain and injury reason. Tight hamstrings are often caused by weak abs, weak glutes and tight hip flexors.
- In Dan's coaching experience, most people, even trained gym goers, are weaker than they expect in lockout positions (ball leg curl, hip thrust, back raise).
- Judging a rep: pressing movements are black and white, because they are hardest in the bottom half of the lift, so if the person does not get close to finishing, the rep does not count. In pulling movements the person can get close to finishing the lift, so whether or not a rep was sound is more of a judgement call. This matters when deciding what counts as a PR.
- For any single leg movement, everybody is different. The person tries a bunch of different single leg movements and picks the one that most consistently and reliably has them feeling their glute at the bottom of the lift on the weak leg side.
- Most people have a weak side and a strong side. In all unilateral lifts, where a left and right side are performed one after the other, perform the weaker side first and match the reps with the stronger side.
- Breathing and bracing. Dan's position: bracing with a breath hold during a rep gives a tangible benefit, higher injury prevention and performance, and you can breathe between reps. The risk of a cerebrovascular injury is nebulous and hard to quantify. So focus on how you breathe and where in your body you breathe, not when. Worrying about when to breathe adds pointless mental overhead to the set that is better spent on technique quality.
  - What the research shows. Spine: bracing raised a lumbar stability index by 32% compared with hollowing the abdomen, with 15% more spinal compression (Grenier and McGill 2007, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 8 healthy men). Performance: in 24 men, Valsalva and lung packing had the shortest sticking-region time on a bench press 1RM, and reverse breathing lowered the maximal load (Blazek et al. 2021, Biology of Sport). A weightlifting belt added about 5 kg (4.6%) to the squat 1RM in 25 trained athletes (Huang et al., Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2026) and gave faster reps at 90% 1RM in 14 men (Zink et al. 2001).
  - Limits. No study has measured fewer injuries from bracing or breath holding. The belt studies test the belt, not breath holding alone. No clean test of breath holding versus normal breathing was found for squats or deadlifts. Exhaling through the sticking point was tested once, in the bench press, and that study did not support it. Peak blood pressure during lifting is very high with a Valsalva (10 male athletes on the leg press at 100% 1RM: 311/284 with a Valsalva versus 198/175 with slow exhalation; Narloch and Brandstater 1995, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation), and guidelines caution people with heart and blood pressure conditions against holding their breath. Those people would already fail a PAR-Q, which stops the plan until a doctor says it is okay (see [section 24](24-rules-do-not-cover-the-person.md)).

Squatting movements (double leg)
- The general rule: get the crease of the hip below or at the knee joint at the bottom of the lift. Technique adjustments and exercise selection are in pursuit of that goal. The goal is a pain free, biomechanically sound, loaded hip crease below the knee position at the bottom of double leg exercises. The sissy squat and the leg extension do not count.
- Technique adjustments: elevate the heels, or change the foot position, so the knees can travel further forward and the geometry works for their body type. Or push the knees apart more, so the lateral distance between the hip and the knee from a side view is shorter and the geometry works better. The more the knees are pushed apart, the more the load shifts into the adductors, so keep that in mind.
- Exercise selection therefore has more to do with the person's mobility limitations, skill level, strength level and limb proportions.
- Front loading squatting or lunging movements lets the person stay more upright and get deeper into the lift, reaching that ideal position. People with longer femurs in relation to their shins generally do better with front loaded movements: goblet style, or with the bar racked across the collarbones. In Dan's coaching experience, many people find the discomfort of acclimating the collarbones to holding weight across them hard to work through. Building that resilience opens up more exercise options; without it, the exercise selection is more limited.
