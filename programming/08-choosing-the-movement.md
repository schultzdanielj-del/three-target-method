# 8. Choosing the movement, muscle by muscle

How to check technique on each movement is in [technique.md](../technique/00-intro.md).

Chest
- A low incline or incline dumbbell press first, then a fly, machine or second angle. Flat barbell bench is easy to game with arch, leg drive and positioning, so use a Spoto press instead.
- Fly movements are easy to cheat, so the second chest movement is often a decline dumbbell bench instead.
- No bench (dumbbells only): pushups and floor flies.
- If scapular stability or elbow position at the bottom is bad on the incline DB bench, use a pushup instead.
- A high incline press is not really a chest movement. It is a vertical press, so it fills the vertical press slot, not a chest slot.

Shoulders
- Lateral raises plus a vertical press. High incline can stand in for the press.
- For people who cannot press overhead (pain, AC joint, poor overhead mobility such as first rib or thoracic spine restrictions), use incline or high incline presses and/or front raises. For example, someone with poor overhead mobility gets two lateral raise movements and a high incline press.
- In Dan's coaching experience, 80% or more of people have a restricted t spine and scapular area (see [section 23](23-upper-body-and-hips-principles.md)), so most people do not get an overhead press at first. Give them lateral raises with side lying external rotations, and low incline or high incline presses.
- A landmine press is used instead of a seated DB press when the person's upper body mechanics under load have not been seen yet and they spend long hours at a desk.
- If lateral raises hurt (for example a neck nerve problem), use Y raises instead, if the Y raises do not hurt.
- Y raise: a cheap way to get some lateral delt work while building shoulder mobility and scapular stability. It can fill the second shoulder slot for people who need that.
- To check whether someone can overhead press, ask for a video of them doing it and/or listen to their feedback on how the set feels. Training submaximally does not fix the restriction. It feels better, but the lighter weight limits results.

Back
- One vertical pull (pulldown, pullup or chinup), a row, and an upper back slot.
- A flared elbow row (single arm dumbbell row, chest supported dumbbell row) counts as the upper back slot, since it hits the rear delts hard. A rear delt fly or face pull can also fill it.
- A pulldown never needs to be dropped or swapped.
- Best grip for a pullup or pulldown: medium width, neutral (palms facing each other). It is the most biomechanically sound and balanced between strength movement, and it hits the lats optimally without putting the shoulder in a compromising position. A wide grip raises the risk of technique breakdown, and in Dan's experience it tends to work around the lats and mostly the teres major. Narrower or underhand grips can emphasize the biceps too much, and most people (beginners especially) have proportionally stronger biceps than lats. Some people still have an underhand vertical pull in their program, but that is mainly to fill a biceps slot. Research: EMG studies of grip width and lat activation are inconsistent, so this is mainly a technique reason, not a measured difference in lat activation.
- The single arm DB row is a higher skill movement than people think. If the person's skill level is not there, swap it for a chest supported row.
- A chest supported row is only swapped if the chest supported position is uncomfortable for the person. Use a single arm row or a seated cable row instead. Dan might let them do a bent row variation, carefully.
- In Dan's coaching experience, people new to the method nearly always have weak upper backs. Spend a good chunk of time on external rotations, lower traps, serratus and scapular tilt movements. A face pull is the upper back movement to start with, because the rear delt fly is a heavy duty movement. Let the person top out the face pull (when they can no longer stand anchored against the weight), so they beat it, which is a confidence booster, and then upgrade to the rear delt fly.
- A Kelso shrug is for people with particularly weak scapular motion and weak mid and low traps (technique in [technique.md](../technique/back.md)).
- A person with an especially weak upper back can get a fifth back movement. For example, someone who tends to skip back training, doubles up on chest and biceps, and has a shoulder problem and elbow tendonitis. Weak upper backs and overdone chest and biceps go together.
- No pullup bar and no cable machine: the person can't really do the vertical pull category. Band pulldowns are a poor substitute, accepted only when the person will never get a pullup bar.

Biceps and triceps
- Dips count as chest and triceps. The progression is the assisted dip machine first, then bodyweight dips, then a dip belt.
- Pushups and dips can count as a second triceps movement.

Quads
- One single leg movement (reverse lunge, split squat, single leg press) plus one deep knee bend movement (sissy squat, goblet, leg press, hack squat).
- The ATG split squat has a dual purpose. It is a strengthening exercise, especially for beginners and intermediates, and also a very powerful mobility exercise, because it runs the joints through their full range of motion and stretches the hip flexors at the same time. Dan recommends it.
- TRX sissy squats go to a person you think can do them, for example someone who is lean and has previous heavy gym culture experience.
- Sissy squats are not a beginner movement: they need lots of quad flexibility and conditioned knees. Anyone with tight hip flexors should not do them, and they are not suited to someone carrying a lot of excess weight.
- In a lunge video, look at limb proportions, hip flexor tightness, ankle mobility, lumbo pelvic stability and core bracing:
  - Tight hip flexors that cut the stride short: use the ATG split squat instead.
  - Poor ankle mobility: put seated calf raises in the plan, and no deep squatting without a heel lift.
  - Poor lumbo pelvic stability or core bracing: use the ATG split squat and front loaded movements.
  - Limb proportions that make the movement awkward (long femurs): avoid squatting if you can.

Glutes and hamstrings
- A leg curl (ball, machine, Nordic or glute ham raise) plus a hip thrust. Glutes also get hit by the hip thrust and the single leg quad movement.
- If a person gets a leg curl and a hip thrust, a backoff set on the leg curl is common (the same movement again, taken to technical failure, 10-15% lighter).
- Everyone can do a hip thrust. Many people find they are weaker than expected and need to learn to lock out right with bodyweight first.
- No bench and no barbell: use a single leg hip thrust instead of the barbell hip thrust.
- No leg curl machine and no stability ball: Nordic ham curls, for time, only if the person is already really strong. Otherwise they get a stability ball. Someone who qualifies for Nordics is already consistent with the plan and carries little excess weight.
- A person with low back issues and weak glutes and hips, whose lockout technique is poor, gets lunges and a back raise as light sets (fixed sets and reps, for example 2x12-15 per side and 3x12-20). They need lots of glute lockout reps, not work sets to failure.

Hinges (back raise, reverse hyper, Romanian deadlift, swings)
- A contextual call for that person. A Romanian deadlift is only for a good lifter whose technique you know well, and it is lighter, on fixed reps and sets (2-3 light sets), not a work set to failure. Put it near the end, so the spine and core are still fresh, and use less weight.
- A back raise performed with the right technique is not spine loaded. It is glute and hamstring loaded. The exercise name is a misnomer.
- The reverse hyper is the only movement that always gets two sets. Others might be in the mix: dumbbell external rotations might be, and potentially Romanian deadlift variations, if the person's equipment limitations and context permit it.

Abs
- Whatever the person can do with their equipment. Order is flexible. Put abs first if the person is likely to skip them.
- The Roman chair side hold is an advanced exercise that most users do not reach. Weighted side planks are more than sufficient to develop the obliques, quadratus lumborum and lateral core. Dan has Roman chair side holds in his own program because he has a very narrow hip structure and a very narrow waist, so adding oblique muscle gives him a good shape. That is not the case for every individual.
- Women might only want enough lateral core training for biomechanical integrity and injury prevention. There is no reason for them to push oblique muscle development as far as it can go, and it may not suit the shape they are going for. Once side planks reach 90 seconds per side, depending on the user's goals and body type, they do not always need to stay in the program, or can become a maintenance set at the end of the workout for injury prevention.

Other
- Put calves first on a letter for a person who would skip them.
- Movements the site excludes (see [protocol/training.md](../protocol/training.md) and [faq/training.md](../faq/training.md)) stay excluded. Equipment limits can force an exception: a barbell row near the end of the workout is acceptable for a person who is not yet strong enough to get into trouble with it, when they have no other row option.
- A very strong person who has outgrown the heaviest dumbbells in their gym can use a head supported barbell row.
- For any movement, ask the person to send a video of a set.
