# Programming: how to build a person's plan

These rules come from his real client plans and his own answers. They add to the movement lists in [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md); they never replace them. Where a rule says "their call" or "depends", ask the person and let them decide.

The plan is built around what the person has: equipment availability is the most frequent limit. People's equipment and mobility limitations are by far their biggest obstacles. Ask what equipment they have (gym, home, dumbbells up to what weight, bench, bar, bands, TRX) before choosing anything. Dumbbell minimum for training at home: women need dumbbells up to 50 lb, men up to 90 lb. It has to be a full range with small jumps between weights, not 2 or 3 weights with huge jumps. A suspension trainer (TRX) can replace weights, but the person has to measure the strap length and where they stand very accurately, because that is the yardstick for resistance. Bands are not a good choice, because there is no way to reliably maintain the same conditions each session. If all someone has is one pair of light dumbbells (for example 20 lb) and no other weights, they have nothing to progress to, so they are not going to get long term results. The problem is the lack of any other weights, not the 20 lb itself. For every movement, the person needs at least one weight that they can hit the bottom of the rep range with, and that they cannot beat the top of the rep range with. One weight on its own lets them do only a fraction of the plan correctly. People with less than this equipment, unless they are already really strong and not overweight, have no options. It is a non starter.

Some people have two places to train (home and a commercial gym): give each slot a home option and a gym option for the same muscle.

Someone who trains in a crowded commercial gym can also get a second option on the slots where the equipment is often taken, for the same muscle (for example a machine press or incline pushups, a reverse pec deck or a bent over rear delt fly, a back raise or a Romanian deadlift).

Source of these rules: Dan's coaching observations from 17 years of coaching, 10,000+ hours of one on one coaching, across easily over 1000 people. They are anecdotal, not studies. Many of them have no direct study behind them, as is true of much of fitness. They are built by taking the known truths and logically assembling a model. Where a study is cited, it is named.

The core premise: the majority of people need much more focused professional intervention with their body mechanics than they are willing to admit. That is their biggest obstacle. They just move poorly. They do not execute the movements correctly at all, and they lack the ranges of motion to get into the correct positions to begin strengthening the right muscles. Most people say they understand this in passing, but they do not truly believe it or embrace it. They want to show up, do the work, and have it work, and then they get punished for it. No rep or set scheme, protocol or training plan fixes this. A lot of "what if" questions come from people who refuse to accept this. If you do not move correctly, you will not achieve your goals, period. This is about goals, not progress: a person can still progress in their current state of mobility, but their progress will have a hard ceiling well below anything that will truly satisfy them, so their goals are not achievable in it. Studies show people with limited mobility still gain muscle and strength, which does not contradict this, because they do not test whether the person reaches the goals they set.

This is why the program is not for complete beginners. A complete beginner can run the program, but running it by themselves and succeeding has slim chances, because of this movement issue. The single best thing that would improve their chances is being coached on how well they move and what their limitations are.

A beginner is also unlikely to succeed long term at any training program, for the same reason. The majority of gym goers never address these bottlenecks and just move from program to program, hoping something will break them through in spite of it. The only ways they work around the bottleneck are poor intensity, which gives no results, avoiding that area completely, which creates imbalances, or slowly getting hurt over the decades.

Talk to the person in layman's terms and simple English. Do not use biomechanical terms with them.

## 1. What to ask before building a plan

The more questions the person answers, the better the potential plan they will get without direct coach eyes on them. The plan can get as good as it can get without a coach seeing the person move and lift, and no further.

Ask the person, one question at a time:
- How long they have been training.
- What their goals are.
- What their experience is with exercises in the gym.
- Which movements are the easiest for them and which are the trickiest.
- Their history of injury.
- Whether anything hurts.
- What they are doing right now.
- What equipment and environment they have access to.

Most people want a nice lean, fit looking body: a bit of abs, fitting into smaller clothes. They need way more muscle than they think to look like that, they need to lose way more bodyfat than they think, and they need to be considerably stronger than they think they will need to be. That is just to get a regular fit person look. The average person does not have an accurate understanding of physiques and what is required. So the goal is always to get as lean as possible and build as much muscle as possible, even to get a regular look that they are frightened to say they want. Deliberately reducing their target will have them fall even more short.

Goals are usually vague. Generally, people just want to be leaner and have more muscle, but the language they use can disguise that. People are apprehensive about stating what they truly want and may not even understand what it means, so draw it out as you talk with them. Someone who says they only care about performance or health usually means the health and performance you get from being leaner and having more muscle. It is extremely rare for someone's goals to require a completely different approach. Most people are nowhere close to needing specialization of any kind.

These questions do not replace watching the person lift. Watching somebody lift is the single best way to determine which exercises, and which technique on each exercise, are best for them. Ask for a video.

The big idea: almost everyone makes the lion's share of their progress from consistent, clear cut measures of success, using the right movements for their body, performed correctly. That gets them further than they could ever imagine. Keep it simple: aim for a set performance with the same sound technique, and trim away everything else they do. Just sitting on that takes them beyond what they thought possible (Dan's opinion).

Remember the program principle: the question is never "what else can I add?" It is "what is left that I can take out?" and the answer is "nothing".

## 2. Letters and schedule

- Ideally each muscle (each letter) is hit every 8 to 10 days. 14 days is just the limit.
- 3 letters for 2 workouts a week, 4 letters for 3 workouts a week. 2 letters would mean 1 day a week, which is not enough frequency.
- If the person cannot do 2 workouts a week, the method is not for them. A one day plan could be made, but it would be a longer workout and would cap progress around late beginner or early intermediate. The type of person who can only give once a week is almost never adherent to once a week either, and no lowering of the bar creates adherence for them. Do not build a one day plan.
- Some 4 letter clients end up training about 2 days a week. They should be training 3 days a week; they are just not adherent. Asking less of them does not fix that, and making it easier rarely increases adherence. Do not shrink the plan to match low adherence.
- Grouping depends on the person. The usual 4 letter split is chest and shoulders, back, arms, legs. The usual 3 letter split is push, pull, legs.
- Example of a different split: a highly adherent, higher skilled, more experienced woman with female physique goals gets more emphasis on lower body, shoulders and back (legs split over two letters, arms folded into the push and pull letters).
- Legs are more taxing on the body. Give leg letters fewer exercises (usually 4) where you can. Other letters have 4 to 6 exercises.
- People have no absolute frame of reference for what a full effort is, or what doing everything is. They take their current plan and emotionally try to scale it back. This plan is the bare minimum that will get constant forward progress, and it cannot be negotiated any lower. That was already done for them: they are not counting calories, they eat simple, they have freedom, and they work out as little as possible. It is already negotiated as low as logic allows in the best possible system. They will not feel that. To them it feels like a lot, so the urge will be to go even lower and box themselves out of any results.
- If the person pushes to shorten or change the plan: the plan cannot be shortened. It can be added to, with weekly steps, non lifting activity, or additional mobility work (unlimited).
- They can also train muscle groups that are not trained yet, for example add calves if they are not in. They can add 2 light sets of external rotations or hip abductions ("sprinkles on top"). They cannot add more volume to existing muscle groups. The effect will be the opposite of what they expect.
- The negotiation has nothing to do with reality. It is people emotionally running from accountability. People are smart, so the strategies they use are sophisticated: therapy language, unassailable statements, logical reasoning. All of it happens without them knowing, in pursuit of their own failure.
- This plan is already built for time limited people. Anything less is not sufficient. If a person wants less than this, they do not care enough about getting in shape, and that is okay, but do not shorten the plan and do not entertain the stories people tell.

## 3. When the person wants a specific muscle bigger

- It is a subtle change, not an overhaul. Put the exercise that hits that muscle as early in the workout as possible, and/or choose movements for other muscle groups that overlap with it.
- Wanting a bigger muscle does not mean doing more sets on it. Do not add sets. A client who refuses the prescribed 2 working sets and does 10 or more keeps getting elbow pain. Short studies find more weekly sets grow muscle more over 8 to 12 weeks, with diminishing returns. TTM chooses the longest runway at the least sacrifice, so it does not add sets (see [criticisms.md](criticisms.md)). Dan broke a plateau and added half an inch to his arms by dropping to just 2 work sets of biceps.
- Men get no default emphasis. Wanting a bigger upper body or bigger legs is everyone's goal.

Age (under 55) changes nothing in the plan. The program is auto-regulated: you set personal bests when the body is ready to do it. Biomechanics and mobility differ across all ages, and being younger does not mean someone performs exercises better. It depends on the lifter's individual experience and training age.

## 4. Someone with a main sport

For a person whose main training is a sport (example: a boxer), the work is spread out in small letters (example: 4 letters of 2 exercises each) so it can be added to a sport session without impacting performance too much. The person can hit their muscle groups at either place they train (for example a limited home gym or the sport gym).

## 5. Do not accommodate self imposed limits

One client had only a suspension trainer and a possible AC joint sprain, and he was consistent with the eating but not the lifting. His plan took out direct shoulder work, which did no harm, and could not train the lower back at all with only a suspension trainer. The plan was to use the suspension trainer for 6 to 8 weeks, save the lunch money and buy a single adjustable dumbbell. He never did it. This was accommodating a person's self imposed rules about what they will and will not do. Do not offer it to other people. It does not replace the equipment minimum in the intro.

Most people start with a goal and then negotiate themselves completely out of any chance of achieving it with decisions like this.

## 6. Very lean, underweight lifters

The training plan does not change. The eating does: underweight people do not need a caloric deficit, so the rule against other foods in Mode 1 does not apply to them. They just get their veggies and protein (all the Mode 1 foods) and otherwise eat whatever they want all day. Eating more carb foods early in the day stimulates their appetite more. They do not need to eat a big surplus. They can build muscle at the rate the body puts it on, or build muscle at that same rate and also get fat, then lose the fat and end up where they would have ended up anyway. Underweight people usually undereat, so eating enough may feel like a surplus to them when it is not really one.

Once someone is around their cruise weight, they should not try to push the scale up more (Dan's opinion).

## 7. Movements per muscle

Two movements per muscle group is the default, with these notes:

| Muscle | Movements | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Chest | 2 | Dips count as chest and triceps, but technique matters for the chest part. |
| Shoulders | 2 | A vertical press and a lateral raise. A high incline press is pretty much a vertical press. |
| Triceps | 2 | Pushups, dips and similar can count as the second triceps movement. |
| Biceps | 2 | Underhand grip pulling counts as biceps work (see overlap). |
| Back | 4 | Split into lats and upper back. |
| Quads | 2 | One single leg movement plus one deep knee bend movement. |
| Glutes and hamstrings | 2 | A leg curl plus a hip thrust. |
| Abs | 2 | What the person can do with the equipment they have, usually a static hold plus a dynamic movement. |
| Calves | optional | See section 10. |

Biceps and triceps can be 1 isolation movement, with the second work set handled by a compound (a pulling movement for biceps, a pressing movement for triceps).

A woman is assumed to have female physique goals (more emphasis on lower body, shoulders and back) unless you get to know her more. It is just a heuristic.

Why some plans have only 1 biceps and 1 triceps movement: underhand grip chinups hit biceps hard, and a 3 letter split can only fit so much, so overlap does the rest.

Glutes and hamstrings are two separate muscle groups. A person whose goals are glute and hamstring dominant gets the maximum slots filled: 2 direct movements for glutes and 2 direct movements for hamstrings (4 in total, for example a leg curl, a back raise, a hip thrust and a hip abduction).

If a woman wants a female physique, bias toward glutes, lateral hip and hamstrings first, and split them across letters so they are hit fresher and harder. Missing a set of chest or direct arm work is no big deal. Missing glute, lateral hip or hamstring work is.

## 8. Choosing the movement, muscle by muscle

How to check technique on each movement is in [technique.md](technique.md).

Chest
- A low incline or incline dumbbell press first, then a fly, machine or second angle. Flat barbell bench is easy to game with arch, leg drive and positioning, so use a Spoto press instead.
- Fly movements are easy to cheat, so the second chest movement is often a decline dumbbell bench instead.
- No bench (dumbbells only): pushups and floor flies.
- If scapular stability or elbow position at the bottom is bad on the incline DB bench, use a pushup instead.
- A high incline press is not really a chest movement. It is a vertical press, so it fills the vertical press slot, not a chest slot.

Shoulders
- Lateral raises plus a vertical press. High incline can stand in for the press.
- For people who cannot press overhead (pain, AC joint, poor overhead mobility such as first rib or thoracic spine restrictions), use incline or high incline presses and/or front raises. Example: one client gets two lateral raise movements and a high incline press.
- 80% or more of people have a dysfunctional t spine and scapular area (see section 23), so most people do not get an overhead press at first. Give them lateral raises with side lying external rotations, and low incline or high incline presses.
- A landmine press is used instead of a seated DB press when the person's upper body mechanics under load have not been seen yet and they work a desk job (example: a software engineer).
- If lateral raises hurt (for example a c spine nerve impingement from computer work), use Y raises instead, if the Y raises do not hurt.
- Y raise: a cheap way to get some lateral delt work while building shoulder mobility and scapular stability. It can fill the second shoulder slot for people who need that.
- To check whether someone can overhead press, ask for a video of them doing it and/or listen to their feedback on how the set feels. Training submaximally does not solve the dysfunction. It only hurts less, so they keep the weight lighter and do not get results.

Back
- One vertical pull (pulldown, pullup or chinup), a row, and an upper back slot.
- A flared elbow row (single arm dumbbell row, chest supported dumbbell row) counts as the upper back slot, since it hits the rear delts hard. A rear delt fly or face pull can also fill it.
- A pulldown never needs to be dropped or swapped.
- Best grip for a pullup or pulldown: medium width, neutral (palms facing each other). It is the most biomechanically sound and balanced between strength movement, and it hits the lats optimally without putting the shoulder in a compromising position. A wide grip raises the risk of technique breakdown, and Dan has seen it work around the lats and mostly the teres major. Narrower or underhand grips can emphasize the biceps too much, and most people (beginners especially) have proportionally stronger biceps than lats. Some people still have an underhand vertical pull in their program, but that is mainly to fill a biceps slot. Research: EMG studies of grip width and lat activation are inconsistent, so this is mainly a technique reason, not a measured difference in lat activation.
- The single arm DB row is a higher skill movement than people think. If the person's skill level is not there, swap it for a chest supported row.
- A chest supported row is only swapped if the person's breasts are too big for it to be comfortable. Use a single arm row or a seated cable row instead. Dan might let them do a bent row variation, smartly.
- New clients nearly always have weak upper backs. Spend a good chunk of time on external rotations, lower traps, serratus and scapular tilt movements. A face pull is the upper back movement to start with, because the rear delt fly is a heavy duty movement. Let the person top out the face pull (when they can no longer stand anchored against the weight), so they beat it, which is a confidence booster, and then upgrade to the rear delt fly.
- A Kelso shrug is for people with particularly weak scapular motion and weak mid and low traps (technique in [technique.md](technique.md)).
- A person with an especially weak upper back can get a fifth back movement. Example: a client who skips back day, doubles up on chest and biceps, and has a bad shoulder and elbow tendonitis. Weak upper backs and overdone chest and biceps go together.
- No pullup bar and no cable machine: the person can't really do the vertical pull category. Band pulldowns are a poor substitute that Dan accepts only when the person will never get a bar.

Biceps and triceps
- Dips count as chest and triceps. The progression is the assisted dip machine first, then bodyweight dips, then a dip belt.
- Pushups and dips can count as a second triceps movement.

Quads
- One single leg movement (reverse lunge, split squat, single leg press) plus one deep knee bend movement (sissy squat, goblet, leg press, hack squat).
- The ATG split squat has a dual purpose. It is a strengthening exercise, especially for beginners and intermediates, and also a very powerful mobility exercise, because it runs the joints through their full range of motion and stretches the hip flexors at the same time. Dan recommends it.
- TRX sissy squats go to a person you think can do them, for example someone who is lean and has previous heavy gym culture experience.
- Sissy squats are not a beginner movement: they need lots of quad flexibility and conditioned knees. Anyone with tight hip flexors should not do them, and they are not for overweight people.
- In a lunge video, look at limb proportions, hip flexor tightness, ankle mobility, lumbo pelvic stability and core bracing:
  - Tight hip flexors that cut the stride short: use the ATG split squat instead.
  - Poor ankle mobility: put seated calf raises in the plan, and no deep squatting without a heel lift.
  - Poor lumbo pelvic stability or core bracing: use the ATG split squat and front loaded movements.
  - Limb proportions that make the movement awkward (long femurs): avoid squatting if you can.

Glutes and hamstrings
- A leg curl (ball, machine, Nordic or glute ham raise) plus a hip thrust. Glutes also get hit by the hip thrust and the single leg quad movement.
- If a person gets a leg curl and a hip thrust, a backoff set on the leg curl is common (the same movement again, taken to technical failure, 10-15% lighter).
- Everyone can do a hip thrust. A lot of people are much weaker than they think and need to learn to lock out right with bodyweight first.
- No bench and no barbell: use a single leg hip thrust instead of the barbell hip thrust.
- No leg curl machine and no stability ball: Nordic ham curls, for time, only if the person is already really strong. Otherwise they get a stability ball. The person who qualifies for Nordics does the plan, is consistent, and is not fat.
- A person with low back issues and weak glutes and hips, whose lockout technique is poor, gets lunges and a back raise as light sets (fixed sets and reps, for example 2x12-15 per side and 3x12-20). They need lots of glute lockout reps, not work sets to failure.

Hinges (back raise, reverse hyper, Romanian deadlift, swings)
- A contextual call for that person. A Romanian deadlift is only for a good lifter whose technique you know well, and it is lighter, on fixed reps and sets (2-3 light sets), not a work set to failure. Put it near the end, so the spine and core are still fresh, and use less weight.
- A back raise performed with the right technique is not spine loaded. It is glute and hamstring loaded. The exercise name is a misnomer.
- The reverse hyper is the only movement that always gets two sets. Others might be in the mix: dumbbell external rotations might be, and potentially Romanian deadlift variations, if the person's equipment limitations and context permit it.

Abs
- Whatever the person can do with their equipment. Order is flexible. Put abs first if the person is likely to skip them.
- The Roman chair side hold is an advanced exercise that almost every user will never get to. Weighted side planks are more than sufficient to develop the obliques, quadratus lumborum and lateral core. Dan has Roman chair side holds in his own program because he has a very narrow hip structure and a very narrow waist, so adding oblique muscle gives him a good shape. That is not the case for every individual.
- Female clients might only want enough lateral core training for biomechanical integrity and injury prevention. There is no reason for them to push oblique muscle development as far as it can go, and it may be detrimental to the shape of their physique. Once side planks reach 90 seconds per side, depending on the user's goals and body type, they do not always need to stay in the program, or can become a maintenance set at the end of the workout for injury prevention.

Other
- Put calves first on a letter for a person who would skip them.
- Movements the site excludes (see [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md) and [faq/training.md](faq/training.md)) stay excluded. Equipment limits can force an exception: a barbell row near the end of the workout is acceptable for a person who is not yet strong enough to get into trouble with it, when they have no other row option.
- A very strong person who has outgrown the heaviest dumbbells in their gym can use a head supported barbell row.
- For any movement, ask the person to send a video of a set.

## 9. Overlap

- Count overlap across the full cycle of letters, not per workout or per week.
- Three or more hard sets on a muscle, counting overlap, is not always too much. It depends on the person.
- Underhand grip pulling counts toward biceps. Not every pulldown or row is heavy on the biceps.
- Dips count as chest and triceps. Pushups and dips can count as a second triceps movement.
- Whether sissy squats, barbell lunges and Roman chair situps overlap on the rectus femoris matters depends on how the person feels doing them. There is no fixed ban.

## 10. Calves

Calves are optional and are usually 1 movement. Put them on an upper body letter, not on leg day. If the person's legs are split across two days, calves can go on one of the leg days. You cannot train calves first and then do lunges and squats on cooked calves, and putting them at the end of leg day is a little too tiring and mentally likely to be skipped.

A person who wants big calves can get two calf movements (for example a double leg calf raise and a seated calf raise), like the 2 movements per muscle used for other groups.

## 11. Order inside a workout

- Compounds first. This is needed. Rarely, an isolation goes first because the person cannot make the compound heavy enough for them, so they do not need as much load to stimulate the muscle.
- Never vertical press before horizontal. It feels terrible. Horizontal then vertical is fine.
- Biceps before triceps does not matter.
- Spine loaded back movements are less ideal. Perform them later in the session if possible, so the core musculature is fresh but the back musculature is fatigued, which means they do not need as much weight. If the person has lower back issues, avoid them completely, because there will be too much focus on back health and positioning and not enough focus on intensity of contraction.
- Abs and calves float (see sections 8 and 10).
- Sissy squats go last on the leg letter. The knees need to be warmed up, and being pre-fatigued means you use less load, so the knees do not blow up.
- Fly movements are easy to cheat, so the second chest movement is often a decline dumbbell bench instead.

## 12. Reps

- Work sets: 15-25 reps for everything except leg compounds.
- Leg compounds go lower. Single leg quads: 8-15.
- The reason for the lower leg range is the metabolic and cardio bottleneck. You want to barf.
- The static hold range stays as written in [SKILL.md](SKILL.md).

## 13. Tempo

- Move fast enough that the technique stays extremely strict. No free falls and no bouncing. There is no need to deliberately count slowly on the lowering part.
- The science: a 2025 meta-analysis of 14 studies (Enes et al., Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research) found tempo has minimal effect on muscle growth. An earlier review (Schoenfeld, Ogborn and Krieger, 2015, Sports Medicine) found lowering and lifting speeds from 0.5 to 8 seconds per rep gave similar growth, with reps over 10 seconds likely worse. So deliberately slowing the lowering phase is not needed for growth.
- There is another reason not to prescribe a lowering count. Lowering length adds a dimension to the lift that is logistically hard to normalize, which reduces the chance of an accurate PR signal. What matters most is the accuracy of the technique.

## 14. What failure means

Failure means the person was unable to achieve a rep with the same technique they used for the previous reps. The set ends there. A rep done with worse technique is not counted.

## 15. Starting points and ramp

- First set on any movement: aim for 15-25 reps, log it, beat it. Most people ignore this because they still approach it with convention in their head. Tell them again.
- No ramp in. There are already the fewest sets possible, so a new client starts the full plan from the first week. As long as they set a decent baseline starting number, the first few workouts are auto-regulated: an inexperienced person's first work sets are not real work sets, because their body cannot yet show the muscle's maximum potential.
- The starting number: for a less experienced person it might be capped by their technical ability and execution ability. For an advanced person it is just what feels good.
- The biggest mistake people make is doing too many sets, and going too heavy, so the starting number lands at too low a rep range.
- Early weeks: when the reps pass the top of the range, add about 10% weight, erring toward rounding down.
- Returning lifters: it is their call, and it is different for each area of the body. A body area with a history of injury should start very conservatively and be built slowly. Areas with no history can start harder. Dan's own low back (injured 15 years ago) is why his lunges, hip thrusts and reverse hypers started very conservatively while sissy squats and glute ham raises did not.

Progression after the early weeks
- Add the smallest increment the person's weights allow in the gym. If the smallest jump is 5 lb, they will have to go all the way up toward 30 reps first, so that the 5 lb jump keeps them at the bottom end of the rep range.
- Sometimes the person has to alternate between the heavier and the lighter weight, attempting PRs every other workout on each one, and ratchet back and forth until they make forward progress.
- A PR can be set from a different weight. As long as the reps stay inside the rep range, it is completely valid. Example: if the best PR is at 20 lb, but a PR at 25 lb would still be inside the rep range, they can try 25 lb, or the original 20 lb for more reps, or 15 lb if that is also still inside the range. Work backwards from the score formula ((weight x reps x 0.0333) + weight) to figure out what a PR would be at a different weight.

Switching gyms or machines: not all machines are the same. On a different machine, set a new baseline (log it as a new first set to beat) instead of carrying over the old number.

## 16. When a movement is not working

- Pain: try a different exercise for the same muscle.
- Equipment maxed out (the heaviest weight they have is not enough): change the movement. Example with a 50 lb dumbbell ceiling: a single arm dumbbell bench press that tops out becomes a TRX chest press with the feet up; a row that tops out becomes a single arm TRX row.
- A stall with no pain and no equipment limit has not happened yet without one of the six checks being broken. Walk the person through the six checks ("Making Changes: Don't." in [faq/training.md](faq/training.md)) instead of swapping.
- Swapping for variety is not a programming rule. If a client asks for a different movement for their own reasons, it is the client's call.

## 17. Mobility and body type

- Poor overhead mobility (for example first rib or thoracic spine restrictions) means no overhead press. Example: one client gets two lateral raise movements and a high incline press instead.
- If a person cannot get into a position, they do mobility work and you pick forgiving movements.
- If the person cannot perform the exercises right because of mobility issues, they need to fix that, and it is their choice. If they are happy with the subset of movements they can perform correctly, so be it. If they start feeling hurt or getting joint problems, it is their responsibility to address it. They can warm up with mobility, do more of it outside the workouts, or do it between sets. If they are not qualified to do the program, it is what it is.
- It is the person's responsibility to perform the movements with proper technique and range of motion.
- Body type changes the movement choice. Long femurs will hate squatting: use lunges, Bulgarian split squats, maybe reverse Nordics. Heels elevated front squats or goblet squats might work if the proportions are not extreme.
- Tight hips (for example a big belly and tight hip flexors): sissy squats are out. Back raises are maybe, since these people tend to arch the low back too much. This is the main reason Romanian deadlifts are left out of most plans: many people are so tight in the hips that they butcher the movement. The light Romanian deadlift (2-4 sets of 12-20) in the site FAQ is for people who can do it well. The guide is not just for beginners, so both are true. Bulgarians and reverse lunges may also be a no, because the person cannot get a long enough stride.
- A good chunk of people are too tight to train legs with any movement at all. They need mobility first.
- Do your best with what the person has. Some people want the Mona Lisa and all they have is crayons.

## 18. Overweight clients

Minimize the movements where the client feels their body is getting in the way. For example, bent over exercises with the stomach in the way can be uncomfortable. Some clients are so overweight that there is no reason to do any core work at all, because all of it will be uncomfortable.

- Overweight clients can do chest supported rows, single arm rows and pulldowns. For legs they can do single leg exercises such as step ups, or box squats with bodyweight.
- Some people are so overweight and deconditioned that none of the work they do in the gym is very effective, and they have so many contraindications and limitations that they will not be able to perform the right movements correctly anyway. For them, the only benefit of gym time is psychological: it boosts their confidence in doing hard things.
- Someone that overweight does not have a health and fitness problem. They have an emotional problem that needs solving, and then health and fitness come down to a more workable level. With over 60 pounds to lose, and certainly over 100, nearly all of their progress will not happen in the gym. It will happen in their heart and their mind. The intervention to drop the first 20 to 30 pounds is extremely basic.
- The question is not what works, because a million things work. It is what ranks at the top of the list. For really overweight people it is honestly not lifting. It is steps and nutrition.
- They need to lose the weight, period, and that happens with diet. They say "I know, I know", but then all their focus is still on the lifting.
- Research. Trials show resistance training builds lean mass in overweight adults, but these are supervised programs, with beginner-level gains from going from zero to beginner. No trial was found of training done optimally by people this overweight, or by people who cannot perform the movements correctly.

## 19. Pregnancy

Dan is not qualified to train pregnant clients, and he has no records or experience running TTM with pregnant clients. In general, what a pregnant client does depends on her state of fitness before the pregnancy: the better the state of fitness she starts from, the more she can do. Typically she does as much as she can until it feels like she cannot do it. As the stomach gets bigger and the hips get looser, some lower body exercises and a lot of core exercises become off limits. She continues with what feels good, and can push hard without feeling awkward. She will not make huge progress while pregnant: the goal is maintenance, general health and general conditioning. Some of the rules for very overweight clients apply, because the stomach obstructs positions. Joint laxity also needs to be taken care of.

## 20. Past injuries

What changes depends on why and how the injury happened. The overall decision principle: choose the exercise that runs the joint through the biggest range of motion with the least amount of discomfort.

Normal discomfort versus pain that means stop and swap the movement:
- Muscle soreness and muscle burning (lactic acid) are normal. Connective tissue pain and sharp impingement pain are not.
- During the exercise, they should not feel any sharp pinching, and no pain right where the muscle connects to the joint. They should feel it in the belly of the muscle.
- In the days after, the muscle should be stiff and sore, but only when they move. They should not feel it when they are not moving.

Knee pain: it comes from a combination of tight quads and hip flexors, very weak hips, and poor technique. Most people lack hip range of motion and hip strength, so the glutes and hip joint do not take their fair share of the load and the knee joint takes all of it. Over time that makes the quads very tight, which pulls on the patellar tendon and makes it worse. The solution: foam roll the quads a lot and stretch them with the rear foot elevated hip flexor stretch (one of the mobility drills). Then they need to learn to use their glutes and hips better and not cheat their leg motions. If the issue keeps coming back, it is an adherence issue: poor adherence to the technique and to the mobility work. Most people would rather get the reps at any cost than sink their hips into a full range of motion. It can be damaging to the ego to find that their actual strength is significantly lower than they assumed, which is what happens when a leg movement is done correctly. Many people say they have strong legs but have never run them through more than a fraction of the range of motion.

Elbow tendonitis: it is always directly related to poor shoulder mechanics. Remove all the exercises where they feel it the most, and work on improving shoulder mechanics. Exercise selection shifts toward movements that make the shoulders move better, and away from movements that hurt the elbow. It takes many, many weeks to reverse. Someone who develops elbow tendonitis has been moving poorly for many weeks: it is a slow problem to cause and a slow problem to fix.

Leg day warmup: ATG split squats, maybe some foam rolling on the quads if they feel stiff, and mobility work if the person is tight. Upper body day warmup: nothing. Nothing is mandatory (see [protocol/training.md](protocol/training.md)).

## 21. A fracture or other injury while healing

Take out the movements that affect the injured area while it heals. Once it feels better, put them back in and let the person pick a comfortable starting point (the first set on a returning movement, see section 15).

Finding the cause: isolate the movement that is causing the pain. Dan's own shoulder pain was caused by single arm pulldowns. He took them out, waited a few weeks for the pain to fade, and was fine.

A person in physio for a nerve problem can start on 3 letters. When they graduate from physio and want to lift more, move them to 4 letters.

## 22. The 3 mobility drills

The FAQ on the site has 3 mobility drills ([faq/training.md](faq/training.md), "Mobility drills"): the Hip Flexor Opener, the Ribcage Release and Dislocates. Send people to them when they cannot get into the positions, or when they have pain that comes from stiffness.
- Dislocates (with a band): a general scapular range of motion opener. About 17 muscles attach to the scapula, and all of them need to be loose enough for the scapula to get into every single position. If the scapula cannot get into a position, the person cannot strengthen its ability to stay there. The scapula needs to move, and also be able to stay in any position, for healthy shoulders and for the health of any other joint in the upper body.
- Ribcage Release (side lying rotation drill with diaphragmatic breathing): if the rib cage and breathing patterns are dysfunctional, all the tension, including at the scapula, returns extremely fast. This drill releases the rib cage so it can rotate and move, so the tension is not held in the wrong spot. It also restores internal pressure and core stability where it is required. Most people have zero internal core stability because of a paradoxical breathing pattern and locked up, hypertonic accessory breathing muscles.
- Hip Flexor Opener (rear foot elevated hip flexor stretch): for the lower body, release the hip flexors. This solves almost every problem. Looser hip flexors put less stress on the patellar tendon and let the person extend through the glutes and hips and sink into their range of motion. With tight hip flexors the hips already start in a slightly flexed position, which cuts the range of motion. At the bottom of an exercise the person cannot fire the glutes out of it, bunches up in the crease of the hip (a pinching motion), and shifts their center of gravity around just to go deeper. That overloads the quads and puts too much work on the patellar tendon. It is the most uncomfortable stretch to do, and people refuse to do it.

## 23. General principles about the upper body and the hips

These apply across many movements. [technique.md](technique.md) refers back to them.

- 80% or more of people are incredibly dysfunctional in the t spine and scapular area: poor scapular stability, no low traps, no scapular tilt or serratus, a jacked up first rib. Years of training badly make it worse.
- Almost every upper body movement needs scapular control or scapular motion as the primary initiator. Without that, the movement is inherently off technique and biomechanically unsound. Most people's scapulae barely move at all, even with zero load. That is where the lion's share of upper body issues come from.
- The other half of upper body issues come from what the scapula sit on: the rib cage, first rib, neck and respiration muscles. People with paradoxical breathing patterns and stiff, locked up rib cages, with a lack of t spine extension and too much hypertonic tension through the scalenes, pec minor, levator scapulae and possibly other muscles, prevent the rib cage and axial skeleton from supporting the scapula correctly. Then all movement down the chain is biomechanically unsound. This is much harder to unravel and improve, because a lot of this dysfunction has to do with the person's emotional state and emotional wiring through the rest of their day.
- First rib, trap, scapula and t spine restrictions are by far everyone's biggest bottleneck to muscle growth in the upper body.
- Most people lack shoulder rotation range of motion and scapular mobility. Training submaximally does not fix that. It only hurts less, so they keep the weight lighter and do not get results.
- In the hips, tight hip flexors, weak glutes and weak abs prevent a locked out hip position, and the lower back extends instead. This is the number one back pain and injury reason. Tight hamstrings are often caused by weak abs, weak glutes and tight hip flexors.
- Most people are much weaker than they think, even trained gym goers, in lockout positions (ball leg curl, hip thrust, back raise).
- Judging a rep: pressing movements are black and white, because they are hardest in the bottom half of the lift, so if the person does not get close to finishing, the rep does not count. In pulling movements the person can get close to finishing the lift, so whether or not a rep was sound is more of a judgement call. This matters when deciding what counts as a PR.
- For any single leg movement, everybody is different. The person tries a bunch of different single leg movements and picks the one that most consistently and reliably has them feeling their glute at the bottom of the lift on the weak leg side.
- Most people have a weak side and a strong side. In all unilateral lifts, where a left and right side are performed one after the other, perform the weaker side first and match the reps with the stronger side.
- Breathing and bracing. Dan's position: bracing with a breath hold during a rep gives a tangible benefit, higher injury prevention and performance, and you can breathe between reps. The risk of a cerebrovascular injury is nebulous and hard to quantify. So focus on how you breathe and where in your body you breathe, not when. Worrying about when to breathe adds pointless mental overhead to the set that is better spent on technique quality.
  - What the research shows. Spine: bracing raised a lumbar stability index by 32% compared with hollowing the abdomen, with 15% more spinal compression (Grenier and McGill 2007, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 8 healthy men). Performance: in 24 men, Valsalva and lung packing had the shortest sticking-region time on a bench press 1RM, and reverse breathing lowered the maximal load (Blazek et al. 2021, Biology of Sport). A weightlifting belt added about 5 kg (4.6%) to the squat 1RM in 25 trained athletes (Huang et al., Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, 2026) and gave faster reps at 90% 1RM in 14 men (Zink et al. 2001).
  - Limits. No study has measured fewer injuries from bracing or breath holding. The belt studies test the belt, not breath holding alone. No clean test of breath holding versus normal breathing was found for squats or deadlifts. Exhaling through the sticking point was tested once, in the bench press, and that study did not support it. Peak blood pressure during lifting is very high with a Valsalva (10 male athletes on the leg press at 100% 1RM: 311/284 with a Valsalva versus 198/175 with slow exhalation; Narloch and Brandstater 1995, Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation), and guidelines caution people with heart and blood pressure conditions against holding their breath. Those people would already fail a PAR-Q, which stops the plan until a doctor says it is okay (see section 24).

Squatting movements (double leg)
- The general rule: get the crease of the hip below or at the knee joint at the bottom of the lift. Technique adjustments and exercise selection are in pursuit of that goal. The goal is a pain free, biomechanically sound, loaded hip crease below the knee position at the bottom of double leg exercises. The sissy squat and the leg extension do not count.
- Technique adjustments: elevate the heels, or change the foot position, so the knees can travel further forward and the geometry works for their body type. Or push the knees apart more, so the lateral distance between the hip and the knee from a side view is shorter and the geometry works better. The more the knees are pushed apart, the more the load shifts into the adductors, so keep that in mind.
- Exercise selection therefore has more to do with the person's mobility limitations, skill level, strength level and limb proportions.
- Front loading squatting or lunging movements lets the person stay more upright and get deeper into the lift, reaching that ideal position. People with longer femurs in relation to their shins generally do better with front loaded movements: goblet style, or with the bar racked across the collarbones. The issue is that a majority of users and clients are unwilling to endure the discomfort needed to acclimate the collarbones to holding weight across them. If they are not willing to cross that gap and build resilience to it, their exercise selection is more limited.

## 24. When the rules do not cover the person

Building a plan is a contextual skill. It takes very granular information about the person and a huge amount of training data to do well, and these rules only let you put together a rudimentary plan. When a person's situation is not covered, say plainly that there is not enough information about them, and not enough granular data, to make the right contextual decision for them. Do not invent a rule to fill the gap.

Stop conditions: if the person would fail a PAR-Q (the standard physical activity readiness questionnaire), do not build a plan for them until a doctor says it is okay. Once the doctor says okay, they can go. Even then there are no black and white rules here: it depends on the specific context of that person.

Beginners: a beginner is someone who has never lifted before. If they have never lifted weights, any program done any way will give them results. They should pick a basic cookie cutter plan and work out 2 to 3 times a week for at least 6 weeks, ideally 12, and they will get great results. That plan is not part of this system, and this repo does not define it.

Someone who has lifted before but has been out for years can do TTM, because they know how to perform the movements.

The biggest obstacle is that people are not performing the movements with even remotely good technique. An AI cannot see the lifting, and the fix is not in this repo. A person with poor technique needs someone who coaches well to watch them move and give feedback, or they need to watch a lot of demo videos and maybe film themselves. Do your best with what the person has.

Many people think an AI can solve every problem for them, and it cannot. Lifting technique and adherence are two things an AI can never solve. Say so plainly when the person's problem is one of them.
